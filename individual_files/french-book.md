# A thousand hours, a language for life

This is an original course for an English-speaking adult beginning French and working toward the broad communicative abilities associated with C1. No previous French or specialist grammar vocabulary is assumed. New grammatical terms are introduced through examples. French examples use standard accents and English translations; translations illustrate meaning, not a claim that every sentence has one unique equivalent.

The ten chapters are substantial teaching chapters. Each supports ten ten-hour learning units. The 1,000 hours include deliberate independent reading and listening, vocabulary review, conversation, writing, revision, projects, and assessment. They are not a claim that this book takes 1,000 hours to read. Take approximately 100 weeks at 10 hours a week, 67 weeks at 15, or 50 weeks at 20, with rest and revision adjusted to your life. Some learners need more time. C1 is not guaranteed by elapsed study time.

The course begins with pronunciation and first conversations, builds the noun phrase and tense system, then develops complex clauses, register, literary recognition, synthesis, and advanced interaction. Its CEFR labels are pedagogical alignments. The CEFR describes language activities and competence; it does not prescribe this particular grammar sequence or a universal French vocabulary list. “Complete” here means a connected course across the requested range, with all ten chapters and working practice tools, not every word, specialist field, regional form, or historical usage in French.

Use the companion for reference-matched grammar, vocabulary, bilingual translation, dictation with meaning, and speaking practice. Make a portfolio rather than collecting only scores. Keep early and revised writing, listening diagnoses, short recordings, translations with commentary, and longer projects. Seek periodic feedback from a proficient human speaker. At advanced stages, include real conversations and substantial authentic French media. A complete learning environment must eventually extend beyond a textbook, and the course teaches the habits needed to do that independently.

# 01 · Sounds, sense, and first conversations

**Hours 1–100 · Beginning to A1 foundations.** You need no previous French. By the end of this stage you should be able to introduce yourself, exchange a few personal details, ask for clarification, read a short introduction, and hear important differences between familiar French sounds. The aim is intelligibility and useful understanding. An English accent is not a moral failure; losing a distinction that changes the message is a practical problem you can investigate.

## Begin with a conversation you can understand

Imagine arriving for your first language exchange. You do not yet know a hundred rules. You need a way to greet someone, identify yourself, and keep the conversation open. Here is an original exchange. Read it once for meaning before studying its parts.

> **Nora :** Bonjour ! Je m’appelle Nora. Et vous ?  
> **Sami :** Bonjour, je m’appelle Sami. Vous parlez anglais ?  
> **Nora :** Oui, un peu. J’apprends le français.  
> **Sami :** Moi aussi. Vous habitez ici ?  
> **Nora :** Non, j’habite à Boston. Je suis ici pour une semaine.  
> **Sami :** D’accord. Bienvenue !

**Meaning:** Nora says hello and gives her name. Sami introduces himself and asks whether she speaks English. Nora says a little and explains that she is learning French. Sami says “me too” and asks whether she lives here. Nora says no, she lives in Boston and is here for a week. Sami acknowledges this and welcomes her.

Several things already work differently from English. French normally gives an ordinary statement an expressed subject: **je**, “I,” or **vous**, “you.” In **je m’appelle**, the small **m’** belongs to the verb construction **s’appeler**, literally calling oneself. For now learn the entire useful expression rather than trying to replace “my name is” word by word. **À Boston** means “in Boston”; a preposition’s appropriate translation depends on what follows it. **Un peu** means “a little,” and is a chunk you can use before understanding every possible quantity expression.

The question **Vous habitez ici ?** has statement word order, with rising or otherwise interrogative intonation in speech and a question mark in writing. That is a valid conversational question. You do not need to produce inversion on your first day. You do need to recognize the social difference between **tu** and **vous**, which we will build from situations below. Begin by playing individual sentences using the site’s French voice. Then say them without the voice. Finally change one detail: your name, your city, or the length of your visit. That change turns imitation into communication.

## Writing is a clue to sound, not an English pronunciation code

French uses the Latin alphabet, but familiar letters do not automatically have English sounds. The written form **vous** contains four letters; in many ordinary contexts its spoken form has two main sounds, /v/ and /u/. The last letter is not usually pronounced in isolation. In **vous avez**, a linking /z/ appears between the words. A useful learning unit is therefore often a phrase with its pronunciation, not an isolated spelling.

Slashes in this chapter enclose a broad sound transcription using the International Phonetic Alphabet, or IPA. You do not need to memorize the entire IPA. A symbol names a sound more consistently than an English spelling approximation would. The transcription is a reference model, with regional and individual variation. French does not have a single accent shared identically by every native speaker. Listen to more than one proficient speaker as you progress, while using a stable model for your own early practice.

The vowels in **si** /si/, “if,” and **sous** /su/, “under,” are useful starting points. Hold a clean vowel without adding an English-style glide. Then compare **sous** /su/ with **su** /sy/, “known” in a verb form you will study later. To explore /y/, begin near the tongue position of /i/ while rounding the lips. This is a physical cue, not a guarantee that the resulting sound is correct. Alternate listening and speaking; compare recordings rather than relying only on how the sound feels inside your mouth. **Tu** /ty/ and **tout** /tu/ are a practical contrast because confusing them can change the word.

French **é** commonly signals /e/, as in **été**, “summer.” **È** and **ê** often help signal /ɛ/, as in **mère**, “mother,” and **tête**, “head.” Accent marks contribute to spelling and may distinguish pronunciation or meaning. Do not treat them as decorative. At the same time, spelling-to-sound relationships have exceptions and variation. Learn **a** “has” and **à** “to/at/in” as different written words even when they sound alike. Context tells the listener which word is intended; accents help the reader.

### Nasal vowels are vowels

In **bon** /bɔ̃/, air also passes through the nose during the vowel. Do not automatically pronounce a separate final /n/. Compare **bon**, “good,” with **bonne** /bɔn/, the feminine form: the second commonly has an oral vowel followed by /n/. In **sans** /sɑ̃/, “without,” the final written **s** is normally silent. In **vin** /vɛ̃/, “wine,” the vowel differs from **vent** /vɑ̃/, “wind.” Some speakers merge distinctions that others retain, including **brin** and **brun**; this is part of variation, not evidence that all nasal vowels are interchangeable.

For a listening exercise, have a proficient speaker or a reliable dictionary recording play **bon, bonne, beau, banc, vin, vent** in an unpredictable order. Point to the written word before saying anything. If identification is unreliable, speaking faster will not solve it. Reduce the set to two contrasting words, listen in short phrases, and rebuild. Synthetic voices are convenient examples, but a single synthetic rendering should not settle a disputed pronunciation question. Human recordings in context are an important supplement.

### The quiet e and the French r

The vowel often written **e** without an accent may be weakened or omitted depending on the word, surrounding sounds, register, and speaker. **Je** can be pronounced carefully with a vowel, while rapid everyday speech may compress sequences such as **je ne sais pas**. Learn the full written form first and begin recognizing common reductions. Do not imitate every reduction before you can identify the words. Listening knowledge can lead production knowledge: recognizing **j’sais pas** does not require using that spelling in a formal message.

The French /ʁ/ is commonly produced toward the back of the mouth. Begin gently with words such as **rue**, **Paris**, and **merci**. Forcing a harsh scrape is unnecessary and can make you tense. Your first goal is a recognizable consonant in a clear word, not a theatrical imitation. Regional French r sounds vary. If your r is imperfect but the listener understands you, prioritize distinctions that are currently causing confusion, then return to refinement.

## Consonants, linking, and phrase rhythm

Useful letter patterns include **ch** /ʃ/ in **chat**, **j** /ʒ/ in **jour**, and **gn** /ɲ/ in **montagne** for many speakers. French **c** is commonly /s/ before **e, i, y**, and /k/ elsewhere; **ç** preserves the /s/ sound before **a, o, u**, as in **ça** and **français**. **G** commonly alternates between /ʒ/ before **e, i, y** and /g/ elsewhere. These are productive decoding clues, but check unfamiliar words rather than treating a short rule as a complete dictionary.

Many final consonant letters are silent, but many are pronounced. **Petit** commonly ends without /t/ in isolation; **petite** has /t/. **Avec** has a final /k/. **Hiver** normally has a final r sound. “Never pronounce the last consonant” is therefore a bad rule. Learn a word’s normal pronunciation together with the spelling and notice how it behaves inside phrases. The letters **-ent** in **ils parlent**, “they speak,” are silent; the word sounds like **parle**, not like English “parent.” This distinction becomes essential when the present tense develops in Chapter 2.

**Liaison** is a linking consonant that appears in certain environments: **les amis** commonly has /z/ between the words, although **les** alone does not end in a spoken /z/. **Enchaînement** is the smooth linking of an already pronounced final consonant to the next vowel, as in **avec elle**. You do not need those terms to speak, but they explain why word boundaries in audio may not match the spaces on the page. Listen to the phrase **vous avez un ami** as a flowing sequence rather than four separate sound boxes.

Some liaisons are usual or required in a given construction, some are optional and sensitive to register, and some are prohibited. An early productive set is **un ami**, **les amis**, **vous avez**, and **nous allons**. Avoid mechanically adding liaison after every written consonant. In particular, do not add a linking /t/ after **et**, “and.” Words with an **h aspiré**, such as **le héros**, block normal elision and certain liaisons despite the h not being pronounced as an English h. Words with **h muet**, such as **l’homme**, permit them. Dictionaries identify this distinction; ordinary spelling alone does not reliably tell you.

French rhythm is organized around groups of words, with prominence often toward the end of a group. Compare an English habit of stressing each content word strongly with the smoother grouping in **Bonjour / je m’appelle Nora / et j’habite à Boston**. The slashes here mark possible sense groups, not mandatory pauses. First read slowly with meaningful grouping. Next keep the grouping while reducing unnecessary gaps. A slower clear sentence is more useful than a rushed imitation in which articles disappear.

## A noun comes with grammatical information

A noun names something: a person, place, thing, event, or idea. French nouns have grammatical gender, usually masculine or feminine. This is a property of the noun in the language. It is not a statement that tables or books have biological sex. Learn **le livre**, “the book,” and **la table**, “the table,” as packages. If you learn only **livre**, you postpone information needed whenever you use an article or adjective with it.

The basic definite articles are **le** before a masculine singular noun, **la** before a feminine singular noun, and **les** before plural nouns of either gender. Definite roughly means the reference is identified or treated as a class in context; it does not map perfectly to English “the” in every sentence. **L’** replaces **le** or **la** before many vowel-starting words and words with mute h: **l’école**, “the school,” is feminine; **l’ami**, “the male friend,” is masculine. Because **l’** hides the gender, a vocabulary note can add **une école** or **un ami** to keep the information visible.

The basic indefinite articles are **un**, **une**, and plural **des**. **Un livre** is “a book,” **une table** is “a table,” and **des livres** is often “books” or “some books.” English commonly omits an article where French uses **des**. Do not infer that an English blank automatically produces a French blank. Later chapters refine articles for quantities, generic meanings, and negation. For now compare **Voici un livre**, “Here is a book,” with **Voici le livre**, “Here is the book.” The second assumes the listener can identify which one.

Plural spelling frequently adds **s**, while the noun’s spoken form often stays the same. **Le livre** and **les livres** may be distinguished primarily by the article in speech. **Un ami** and **des amis** also have linking patterns that help convey the phrase. Train your ear on the whole noun phrase. Some plurals have other spellings, such as **un journal / des journaux**; acquire frequent examples as you meet them rather than inventing a universal add-s rule.

### From noun to a small description

An adjective describes a noun. Many adjectives take a written **e** in the feminine and **s** in the plural, but the details vary. Compare **un ami français**, “a French male friend,” and **une amie française**, “a French female friend.” The written changes also affect the usual pronunciation of **français/française**. **Un livre intéressant** becomes **des livres intéressants**. You will learn adjective patterns and position in depth in Chapter 2. The present point is that French makes linked choices inside a phrase: article, noun, and adjective belong together.

Consider how to build “a small house.” First choose the noun with its gender: **une maison**, feminine. Next choose **petit**, “small.” Because the noun is feminine singular, use **petite**. This frequent adjective normally precedes the noun, giving **une petite maison**. Copying the English order happened to work here, but it will not always: “a red house” is normally **une maison rouge**. The reasoning is not “French reverses every adjective.” The reasoning is “learn the usual position and form for this adjective with this noun.”

## Every ordinary finite sentence needs a working verb

A verb expresses an action, process, or state. A **finite** verb is a form tied to a subject and a tense or mood. You can begin without memorizing those labels, but they let us describe a useful pattern: **subject + verb + the information you want to give**. In **Je suis ici**, “I am here,” **je** supplies the speaker, **suis** is the subject’s form of **être**, “to be,” and **ici** gives location. English “I here” is incomplete; ordinary French **je ici** is incomplete for the same practical reason.

| Subject | Être, present | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| je | suis | I am |
| tu | es | you are, familiar singular |
| il / elle / on | est | he / she / one or we is/are, according to context |
| nous | sommes | we are |
| vous | êtes | you are, polite singular or plural |
| ils / elles | sont | they are |

**On** often means conversational “we,” but its verb form is third-person singular: **On est ici**, “We are here.” **Nous sommes ici** is also correct and often more explicit or formal. **Ils** can refer to a masculine or mixed group under conventional grammatical agreement; **elles** refers to a feminine group. French speakers and institutions vary in approaches to inclusive language, which advanced chapters discuss. At this stage recognize the conventional forms without assuming every social identity can be inferred from a pronoun in a textbook.

Use **être** for many identities, qualities, and locations: **Je suis Nora**, **Je suis étudiant**, **Je suis française**, **Je suis à Paris**. These sentences do not share exactly the same structure in every detail. A profession directly after **être** commonly has no indefinite article when unmodified: **Je suis médecin**, “I am a doctor.” **C’est un médecin**, “He/She is a doctor” or “That is a doctor,” is another construction. Do not generalize “remove every article after être”; learn the local construction and let later examples refine it.

**C’est** is a high-value identifying expression. **C’est Nora**, “That is Nora”; **c’est un livre**, “it is a book”; **c’est intéressant**, “it is interesting.” **Il est** or **elle est** commonly describes an already identified person or thing: **Il est intéressant**, “It/He is interesting,” depending on context. You will hear **c’est** used very widely, including informally before plural references. Learn **ce sont des livres** as a useful standard plural identifying form, while expecting variation in real speech.

### Avoir expresses more than possession

| Subject | Avoir, present | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| j’ | ai | I have |
| tu | as | you have |
| il / elle / on | a | he / she / one or we has/have |
| nous | avons | we have |
| vous | avez | you have |
| ils / elles | ont | they have |

The apostrophe in **j’ai** reflects elision: **je** loses its vowel before the vowel sound here. It is not an English possessive mark. **J’ai un livre**, “I have a book,” uses **avoir**, “to have,” for possession. French also uses **avoir** in common expressions where English uses “be”: **J’ai vingt ans**, “I am twenty years old”; **j’ai faim**, “I am hungry”; **j’ai soif**, “I am thirsty”; **j’ai froid**, “I am cold”; **j’ai besoin d’aide**, “I need help.” Learn the whole patterns. Translating individual English verbs mechanically would produce errors such as **je suis vingt ans**.

Work through “We are thirty years old.” Choose the subject **nous**. Age uses **avoir**, so the verb is **avons**. Thirty is **trente** and years in the age construction is **ans**. The result is **Nous avons trente ans**. Now change the subject to polite “you”: **Vous avez trente ans**. The number did not determine the verb; the subject did. This is the beginning of conjugation: a relationship between the verb form and the sentence’s subject, not a random collection of spellings.

**Il y a** means “there is” or “there are” in a very common presentational construction: **Il y a un café ici**, “There is a café here”; **Il y a deux cafés**, “There are two cafés.” At this level learn it as a whole expression. The written **il** does not identify a male person here. Ask **Il y a un café ici ?** in ordinary conversation, or **Est-ce qu’il y a un café ici ?**, “Is there a café here?” The second question frame is explained next.

## Questions, polite choices, and keeping the conversation alive

Use **vous** when addressing more than one person and commonly when addressing one adult you do not know, particularly in a professional or service situation. Use **tu** in many familiar relationships. Practices vary by region, age, community, and workplace; there is no universal rule that friends always use one form immediately. **On peut se tutoyer ?**, “Can we use tu with each other?”, is a useful expression when appropriate. Choose a polite opening and follow the other person’s preference rather than treating pronoun choice as a test you must guess silently.

Three question strategies occur frequently. A statement with question intonation, **Vous parlez français ?**, is common in conversation. **Est-ce que vous parlez français ?** adds a question frame without changing the subject-verb order. Inversion gives **Parlez-vous français ?**, which you will encounter in more formal contexts and set expressions. These forms can ask the same practical question with different register. For active production now, use statement questions and **est-ce que** confidently. Recognize inversion without forcing it into every informal exchange.

Question words identify missing information: **qui**, “who”; **où**, “where”; **quand**, “when”; **comment**, “how”; **pourquoi**, “why”; **combien**, “how much/many”; and **quel/quelle/quels/quelles**, “which/what” before a noun. **Où habitez-vous ?** asks where you live. **Vous habitez où ?** is a common conversational alternative. **Comment vous appelez-vous ?** asks your name politely. Treat this as a full useful pattern while the reflexive and inversion pieces become clearer later.

Negation often uses **ne … pas** around the finite verb: **Je ne suis pas français**, “I am not French”; **Vous ne parlez pas anglais**, “You do not speak English.” Before a vowel, **ne** becomes **n’**: **Je n’ai pas de voiture**, “I do not have a car.” Notice **de voiture** rather than **une voiture** in this ordinary negated possession. Chapter 3 explains the broader article pattern and exceptions. You can use the correct chunk now without pretending that “not” maps to a single word in a single position.

In everyday speech, **ne** is often absent: **Je sais pas**, “I don’t know.” Recognize it, but keep **ne** in your careful written work and practice full forms so that you understand the structure. Do not confuse **pas** with a stand-alone English verb “don’t.” **Je pas français** lacks the required **suis**. One reliable editing routine is to underline the subject, find its finite verb, and then locate both parts of the careful negative frame.

### Repair phrases are real language ability

You will misunderstand things. A useful beginner knows how to respond without pretending. **Pardon ?** can request repetition. **Vous pouvez répéter, s’il vous plaît ?** means “Can you repeat, please?” **Plus lentement, s’il vous plaît**, “More slowly, please.” **Je ne comprends pas**, “I don’t understand.” **Qu’est-ce que ça veut dire ?**, “What does that mean?” **Comment ça s’écrit ?**, “How is that written?” These expressions help make incoming language learnable. They are not evidence that you have failed the conversation.

Build a short repair exchange. Person A says an unfamiliar town name. Person B says **Pardon, comment ça s’écrit ?** Person A spells it. Person B repeats it and checks **C’est bien ça ?**, “Is that right?” Switch roles. The goal is a successful information transfer. Record whether the town name arrived correctly, rather than counting how many sophisticated words you used. This task also prepares you for telephone numbers and appointments, where accuracy matters more than a smooth uninterrupted performance.

## Numbers, time, and small information exchanges

Begin with **zéro, un, deux, trois, quatre, cinq, six, sept, huit, neuf, dix**. Then learn **onze, douze, treize, quatorze, quinze, seize**, followed by **dix-sept, dix-huit, dix-neuf**. The tens **vingt, trente, quarante, cinquante, soixante** provide useful building blocks. In the French forms commonly used in France, seventy is **soixante-dix**, eighty **quatre-vingts**, and ninety **quatre-vingt-dix**. Regional systems include **septante** and **nonante**, and **huitante** in some places. Recognize the system used by your interlocutor without describing another francophone region’s standard as defective.

For early dictation, avoid beginning with a hundred random large numbers. First discriminate pairs such as **deux/douze**, **six/seize**, **trois/treize**, and **quatre/quatorze**. Next practice small meaningful groups: a price, an age, a classroom number. Ask for repetition and check the final answer. **C’est douze ou deux ?** means “Is that twelve or two?” When spelling and number conventions become more complex, consult a reference; a spoken exchange is often successfully repaired before the learner can explain every hyphen rule.

Days of the week are **lundi, mardi, mercredi, jeudi, vendredi, samedi, dimanche**. They normally begin with lowercase letters in French. **Lundi**, without an article in a suitable context, can mean “on Monday”; **le lundi** commonly describes a regular Monday habit. **Aujourd’hui**, “today,” **demain**, “tomorrow,” and **hier**, “yesterday,” are useful even before you can produce all the tenses that combine with them. In early plans you can say **Demain, je suis à Paris**, with a future interpretation supplied by the context; Chapter 3 adds explicit future constructions.

For clock time, **Il est deux heures**, “It is two o’clock,” and **Il est une heure**, “It is one o’clock,” are good basic patterns. **À deux heures** means “at two o’clock.” Distinguish the time statement from the prepositional appointment: **Il est deux heures** versus **Le rendez-vous est à deux heures**. The twenty-four-hour clock is widely useful for schedules. **Quatorze heures trente** corresponds to 14:30. Learn to read the form before attempting to pronounce every complicated time at conversational speed.

## Learn vocabulary as usable relationships

A vocabulary record should help you do something. For a noun, store an article and a short context: **la gare — La gare est ici**, “The station is here.” For a verb, store the infinitive and one pattern: **habiter à — J’habite à Boston**, “I live in Boston.” For a fixed expression, store the whole expression: **s’il vous plaît**, “please.” A translation alone cannot tell you whether a noun is feminine or which preposition follows a verb. A hundred isolated labels can therefore be less useful than fifty phrases you can actually retrieve.

Use retrieval in both directions. Seeing **la gare** and recognizing “station” is receptive knowledge. Starting from “Where is the station?” and producing **Où est la gare ?** requires a different route. Practice both, and add listening: hear **Où est la gare ?**, write it, and explain the meaning. Finally use it in a tiny exchange: ask for the station, receive a direction, and confirm it. The same phrase now connects spelling, sound, meaning, and social purpose.

The curated course bank is a foundation, not a complete inventory of French. As you read and listen, collect useful items that recur in contexts you care about. Record one meaning at a time; many words have several. **Le temps** can relate to time or weather in different constructions. Copying an entire dictionary entry into a flashcard can make recall ambiguous. Prefer a contextual cue that fixes the intended meaning. Add broader senses after the first becomes familiar, keeping the relationship between them visible.

Review new vocabulary after a gap, not only while it remains in short-term memory. The companion schedules practiced items with increasing intervals after successful reference matches or explicit self-marks. An error returns sooner. This is a simple study aid, not a validated model of your memory. Continue to use words in reading, listening, and conversation. If you remember a card but cannot understand the word in a sentence, increase contextual input rather than repeatedly congratulating yourself on the card.

## An original reading: two people, two reasons to learn

> Je m’appelle Lina. J’habite à Montréal et je travaille dans un café. Je parle anglais et un peu français. Le matin, je dis bonjour aux clients. Parfois, je ne comprends pas une question. Je demande : « Vous pouvez répéter, s’il vous plaît ? » Les clients parlent plus lentement. J’apprends un mot, puis un autre. Mon ami Karim habite à Lyon. Il apprend le français aussi. Nous avons un rendez-vous en ligne le mercredi. Nous parlons dix minutes en français, puis dix minutes en anglais. Ce n’est pas toujours facile, mais c’est intéressant.

**Translation:** My name is Lina. I live in Montréal and I work in a café. I speak English and a little French. In the morning, I say hello to the customers. Sometimes I don’t understand a question. I ask, “Can you repeat, please?” The customers speak more slowly. I learn one word, then another. My friend Karim lives in Lyon. He is learning French too. We have an online appointment on Wednesdays. We speak French for ten minutes, then English for ten minutes. It is not always easy, but it is interesting.

The passage contains a few forms that you need only understand in context now. **Je travaille** means “I work,” and **nous parlons** means “we speak”; Chapter 2 develops their present-tense patterns. **Aux clients** includes a contraction of a preposition and an article, developed in the next chapter. You can still understand the practical scene without extracting those rules from one exposure. This is a useful reading habit: resolve the words needed for the main message, then return to a selected structure for focused study.

First answer in English: where does Lina live, what does she do when a question is unclear, and how is the language exchange divided? Next answer in short French phrases: **À Montréal. Elle demande de répéter. Dix minutes en français, puis dix minutes en anglais.** These are reference answers; a full sentence is also possible. Now write three sentences about your own learning situation. Preserve a familiar structure and change meaningful content: **J’habite à …**, **Je parle …**, **J’apprends le français parce que …**. **Parce que**, “because,” introduces your reason; if the reason is beyond your current French, write a simpler one or ask for help.

## Guided translation: reasoning before speed

Translate the following into French without reading the key. Then compare both wording and grammatical choices. Multiple translations may be legitimate, especially when English leaves politeness or gender unspecified. State the intended context when you choose a form.

1. Hello, my name is Alex.
2. I am twenty years old.
3. We are here. Use explicit nous.
4. Do you speak English? Address an unfamiliar adult.
5. I do not understand.
6. There is a book on the table.
7. Where is the station?
8. Can you repeat, please? Address one person politely.
9. I live in Boston, but I am in Paris today.
10. It is interesting, but it is not easy.

**Explained key.** (1) **Bonjour, je m’appelle Alex.** The naming expression uses **m’**, not English possessive “my.” (2) **J’ai vingt ans.** Age uses avoir, and **je** elides. (3) **Nous sommes ici.** The verb agrees with nous. (4) **Vous parlez anglais ?** or **Est-ce que vous parlez anglais ?** Both fit polite address. (5) **Je ne comprends pas.** Learn the full repair phrase. (6) **Il y a un livre sur la table.** **Sur** means “on” here; the table is an identified reference. (7) **Où est la gare ?** Preserve the accent in où. (8) **Vous pouvez répéter, s’il vous plaît ?** The longer familiar phrase is useful before a full modal-verb explanation. (9) **J’habite à Boston, mais je suis à Paris aujourd’hui.** A city commonly follows à in this pattern; location uses être. (10) **C’est intéressant, mais ce n’est pas facile.** Elision produces n’est, and the adjective is used as a general evaluation.

Now translate these French sentences into English: **J’ai froid. Vous êtes ici pour une semaine ? C’est une petite maison. Je ne parle pas français. Nous avons un rendez-vous lundi.** Reference meanings are “I am cold”; “Are you here for a week?”; “It is a small house”; “I do not speak French”; and “We have an appointment on Monday.” **I have cold** is not idiomatic English for the first. Translation must preserve the intended message in a natural target-language construction, not preserve the original word order at all costs.

## Listening and speaking as deliberate practice

Use a short phrase for three listening passes. On the first, listen for the situation: greeting, identity, location, or request. On the second, write the words you can identify and leave a gap where needed. On the third, check a transcript and replay the exact difficult segment. Write the reason for the error if you can: an unknown word, a familiar word with unfamiliar linking, a vowel contrast, or a spelling problem. These causes require different remedies. Replaying without identifying the cause often produces familiarity rather than transferable skill.

For shadowing, choose one comprehensible sentence. Listen, pause, repeat, and record. Then attempt speaking with the recording, keeping the sense groups. Compare one feature only, such as the vowel in **tu**, the linking in **vous avez**, or the final consonant of **petite**. Do not score your entire voice as “bad.” A specific observation suggests a next action; a global judgment rarely does. Stop if your throat hurts or your articulation becomes tense. Gentle repeated practice is more productive than forcing an unfamiliar sound.

The speaking page offers a local recording and, where supported, on-device dictation and Chrome’s on-device AI. A transcript is evidence about what a recognizer inferred, not a direct measurement of your pronunciation. It may infer the right word from context despite an unclear sound, or the wrong word despite an intelligible pronunciation. AI can also give incorrect advice. Use these tools as additional observations. A periodic conversation with a proficient human listener remains important, particularly as your goal shifts from individual phrases to spontaneous interaction.

## Diagnose recurring beginner errors

If you write **je suis vingt ans**, the issue is choosing a construction from English rather than the French age pattern. Repair it by practicing three meaningful ages with **avoir**. If you write **une livre** for “a book,” the issue is lexical gender; relearn **un livre** as a unit. If you write **je pas ici**, the sentence has lost its verb; underline **je**, add **suis**, and then place **ne … pas** around it. If you write **vous est**, the subject and verb form do not agree; practice the complete pair **vous êtes** rather than saying “être” repeatedly.

If written French seems manageable but audio sounds like a continuous stream, reduce the audio length and work with known phrases. Identify articles and linking boundaries. If you can imitate a whole phrase but cannot change a name or place, test comprehension and substitution; memorized sound is useful but not yet flexible production. If you understand a question but freeze while answering, prepare two short possible answers and rehearse a real exchange. Each diagnosis identifies an operation to strengthen. None requires assuming that you lack an aptitude for languages.

## Stage project and a fair checkpoint

Create a written profile of sixty to ninety words using only language you can explain. Include your name or a fictional name, city, languages, reason for studying, a simple daily fact, and a polite question for a partner. Record a thirty- to sixty-second version. Then have a two-minute exchange with a partner or a patient tutor, or simulate both roles if no partner is available yet. Include at least one request for clarification and a correct response to it. Save the first version, the feedback, and a revised version.

Judge the work on four separate dimensions, each from 0 to 3. **Message:** can the reader or listener identify the intended personal details? **Basic construction:** are subject, finite verb, and essential noun phrases present? **Intelligibility:** can a patient listener understand familiar phrases without seeing the text? **Interaction:** can you greet, answer a predictable question, and request repetition? Zero means not yet demonstrated, one means possible with substantial support, two means mostly successful with ordinary beginner hesitation, and three means reliable on this limited task. The total is a local study rubric, not an official CEFR rating.

If a dimension remains at zero or one, choose a focused repair task and repeat the project after several days. Do not wait until you sound like an advanced speaker; this stage concerns first conversations. Equally, do not let a quiz score replace the oral task. Recognizing **êtes** among four choices and using **vous êtes** during a conversation are related but different abilities. The next chapter builds the present tense and noun phrase in much greater depth, giving your first conversations more variety, precision, and independence.

## Carry these habits into Chapter 2

Keep articles attached to nouns, patterns attached to verbs, sound attached to spelling, and meaning attached to the situation. Make small personal changes to model sentences. Review after a gap. When a phrase is beyond your grammar, learn enough locally to understand its role and return to the complete pattern later. Your first hundred hours are not a race to accumulate a thousand disconnected labels. They establish a practical system for noticing, understanding, retrieving, and using French; the remaining nine hundred hours make that system more powerful.

# 02 — Everyday sentences: making a small French world

**Level:** A1. **Time:** 100 hours, organized as ten ten-hour units. **Prerequisite:** the sound–spelling habits, basic greetings, and listening routines of Chapter 01. This chapter teaches you to identify people and things, describe a routine, ask a useful question, and arrange a simple meeting. The level label is an instructional judgment; the CEFR describes communicative abilities rather than prescribing one official grammar list.

Imagine meeting a new neighbor. You want to say that you live nearby, introduce your sister, ask about the local market, and explain that you do not work on Sunday. These are short messages, but they contain most of the machinery of an everyday French sentence. You need a noun phrase to name something, a verb to say what happens, and a way to place the message in a conversation. We will build these pieces together rather than memorize isolated translations of English words.

## A sentence has jobs, not an English word order to copy

Start with **Lina travaille ici** — “Lina works here.” *Lina* answers who; *travaille* supplies the event; *ici* locates it. Replace Lina with **elle**: **Elle travaille ici**. The pronoun keeps the same job while allowing you to avoid repeating a name. French normally needs an expressed subject, even when the verb ending seems informative. English “Am tired” may work as an informal note; ordinary French requires **Je suis fatigué** or **Je suis fatiguée**.

The subject pronouns are **je** I, **tu** you addressing one familiar person, **il** he/it, **elle** she/it, **on** one/we/people, **nous** we, **vous** you politely or to several people, **ils** they for an all-male or mixed group, and **elles** they for an all-female group. Here grammatical gender and social reference interact; do not assume that a grammatical masculine noun always describes a male person. **On** is extremely common for conversational “we”: **On habite ici** means “We live here.” Its verb nevertheless has the same form as **il/elle**.

Choose **vous** when addressing an unfamiliar adult in a service or professional setting, unless the other person proposes **tu**. A child, close friend, or familiar peer often receives **tu**. There is regional, workplace, and generational variation. The useful skill is recognizing the relationship being offered and keeping it consistent: **Vous habitez ici ?** must not continue as **Tu travailles où ?** without a change of relationship.

French elides **je** before a vowel sound: **j'habite**, not *je habite*. The apostrophe joins two written pieces into one spoken group. A written **h** does not by itself tell you whether elision occurs; learn the word with its article. **L'hôtel** permits it; **le héros** does not. At this stage, store correct chunks and consult a dictionary for unfamiliar h-words rather than applying an invented sound rule.

## Naming one thing: why nouns travel with articles

English often allows a bare label where French expects a determiner, a small word before the noun. **C'est une table** means “It is a table.” **La table est petite** means “The table is small.” The first sentence introduces a member of a category; the second refers to an identifiable table. The contrast is about how the listener locates the referent, not about whether the object matters.

The indefinite articles are **un** with a masculine singular noun, **une** with a feminine singular noun, and **des** with plural nouns. The definite articles are **le**, **la**, **l'** before an appropriate vowel sound, and **les** in the plural. Learn **un livre**, **une chaise**, **l'école**, **les voisins** as whole entries. If you learn only *livre = book*, you have omitted information needed for almost every later sentence about that book.

Gender is a noun's grammatical class. A table is not biologically female, but **table** belongs to the feminine class and controls **une petite table**. Some endings give useful probabilities: **-tion** and **-sion** are usually feminine; **-ment** is usually masculine. These are tendencies with exceptions and do not replace a dictionary. **La main**, **le problème**, **le musée**, and **la personne** are worth learning precisely because a quick guess can mislead an English speaker.

An article also communicates generality. **J'aime le café** means “I like coffee,” referring to the category. **J'aime les chiens** means “I like dogs.” Do not delete the article because English uses none. Compare **Il y a un chien devant la porte** — “There is a dog in front of the door.” Here you introduce one dog rather than make a general claim about dogs. Later, **Je bois du café** will describe an unspecified amount; Chapter 03 develops that distinction.

Work through a first description. You see an unfamiliar bag. Say **C'est un sac** because you classify it and introduce it. Add **Le sac est bleu** because the bag is now identifiable. Combine the information: **C'est un sac bleu**. The adjective stays in the noun phrase and agrees with its masculine noun. If the object is a chair, use **C'est une chaise bleue**. Changing the noun therefore requires checking the article and adjective, not only replacing one vocabulary item.

## More than one: plural information can be visible and quiet

The regular written plural adds **-s**: **un livre → des livres**, **une petite maison → des petites maisons**. Many final plural marks are not pronounced. The article often carries the most audible number information, and a liaison may make a following vowel connection audible. This explains why listening only for a final *s* will fail. Hear the whole phrase **les amis**, not a sequence of separately sounded letters.

Common alternatives include **un journal → des journaux**, **un travail → des travaux**, **un cadeau → des cadeaux**, and **un jeu → des jeux**. Nouns already ending in **-s**, **-x**, or **-z** normally keep their written form in the plural: **un prix → des prix**. There are exceptions in the families ending in **-al**, **-ail**, and **-ou**, so store high-frequency forms as pairs. A dictionary's plural information is more reliable than extending one pattern to every noun with similar spelling.

When a descriptive adjective comes before a plural noun, formal and careful French often changes **des** to **de**: **de belles maisons**. Conversational use varies, and fixed combinations can preserve **des**. First master ordinary **des maisons**, then recognize **de belles maisons** without interpreting **de** as a missing noun. This is an example of a rule whose surrounding phrase matters more than a one-word translation.

French also contracts definite articles after certain prepositions. **À + le = au**, **à + les = aux**, **de + le = du**, and **de + les = des**. Thus **Je vais au marché** means “I am going to the market”; **Je parle aux voisins** means “I speak to the neighbors.” There is no contraction in **à la gare** or **à l'école**. Notice that **des** can therefore be an indefinite plural article or the contraction of **de les**. The sentence tells you its function.

## Describing accurately: agreement and adjective position

In a basic noun phrase, an adjective agrees with the noun it describes. **Un voisin patient**, **une voisine patiente**, **des voisins patients**, **des voisines patientes** display masculine/feminine and singular/plural. The regular feminine adds **-e**; the regular plural adds **-s**. An adjective ending in written **-e** may show no feminine change: **un collègue calme**, **une collègue calme**. The fact that an ending is silent does not make it optional in writing.

Some adjectives change more substantially: **heureux/heureuse**, **actif/active**, **gentil/gentille**, **blanc/blanche**, **beau/belle**, **nouveau/nouvelle**, **vieux/vieille**. Learn the useful pair with an example rather than trying to reconstruct every form during conversation. Before a masculine singular noun beginning with a vowel sound, certain adjectives use a linking form: **un bel appartement**, **un nouvel ami**, **un vieil immeuble**. These are not feminine forms; the following noun remains masculine.

Many adjectives follow the noun: **une voiture rouge**, **un restaurant français**, **un exercice difficile**. A group of common adjectives often precedes it: **un petit jardin**, **une grande maison**, **un bon repas**, **une nouvelle adresse**. Position can change meaning. **Un ancien collègue** is a former colleague; **un meuble ancien** is an old or antique piece of furniture. **Un grand homme** can mean a great man; **un homme grand** describes height. At A1, learn frequent phrases accurately and notice contrasts instead of claiming a universal “adjectives go after nouns” rule.

Adjectives can follow **être**: **La maison est grande**. They still agree with the subject noun phrase. If you say **Marie est française**, the adjective describes Marie. If you say **Marie parle français**, **français** names the language and is not a feminine adjective modifying Marie. This contrast prevents an understandable but incorrect *elle parle française*. The spelling follows the word's job.

To describe someone respectfully, choose a few relevant details rather than produce a catalogue of appearance. **C'est ma collègue. Elle est très organisée et elle est toujours aimable.** “This is my colleague. She is very organized and she is always kind.” **Très** means very and does not agree. **Toujours** means always and also does not agree. Agreement belongs to adjectives and certain other classes, not to every word near a feminine noun.

## Possession, pointing, and identifying

French possessive determiners agree with the thing possessed, not with the possessor. **Mon frère** and **ma sœur** can both be said by a man or a woman. **Son frère** can mean his brother or her brother; context identifies the owner. The singular owner forms are **mon/ma/mes**, **ton/ta/tes**, and **son/sa/ses**. With several owners or polite address, use **notre/nos**, **votre/vos**, and **leur/leurs**.

Before a feminine noun beginning with a vowel sound, use **mon**, **ton**, or **son** for sound connection: **mon amie**, **ton école**, **son adresse**. This does not change the noun's gender. You still say **mon amie est française**. A useful diagnostic question is “What noun does this determiner introduce?” If the answer is *adresse*, you know why **son adresse** appears even when talking about a woman's address.

The demonstratives are **ce** with a masculine singular noun, **cet** before an appropriate vowel sound, **cette** with a feminine singular noun, and **ces** with plural nouns: **ce quartier**, **cet hôtel**, **cette rue**, **ces magasins**. They point to something recoverable in the situation. French does not always force the English this/that distance distinction; **-ci** and **-là** can make it explicit: **ce livre-ci**, **ce livre-là**.

Distinguish **c'est** from **il est** at an introductory level. **C'est un médecin** identifies a person with an article and noun phrase. **Il est médecin** states a profession without an article when unmodified in this construction. **Il est sympathique** describes him. Plural identification is **ce sont mes amis** in careful standard French, although **c'est mes amis** occurs in informal speech. Recognizing an informal form does not require copying it in formal writing.

## The present tense: a form is a relationship

An infinitive is the dictionary form of a verb: **parler**, **finir**, **prendre**. A conjugated form connects that verb to a subject and a tense. For regular **-er** verbs, remove **-er** and add **-e, -es, -e, -ons, -ez, -ent**: **je parle, tu parles, elle parle, nous parlons, vous parlez, ils parlent**. Several forms sound the same, which is another reason subject pronouns matter. Written **-ent** in this ending is silent.

French present tense covers several English patterns. **Je travaille** can mean “I work,” “I am working,” or contextually “I do work.” Do not build *je suis travaille* to imitate English progressive grammar. **Je suis en train de travailler** explicitly emphasizes an activity in progress, but ordinary **je travaille** is often sufficient. **Je travaille demain** can also refer to an arranged future situation when a time expression makes that clear.

Some spelling changes preserve pronunciation. **Nous mangeons** keeps a soft g before o; **nous commençons** keeps a soft c. Verbs such as **acheter** and **préférer** change a written accent in some forms: **j'achète, nous achetons; je préfère, nous préférons**. Learn a short present-tense set and say it in phrases. The purpose is not to recite charts quickly but to retrieve **nous commençons à neuf heures** when the subject is we.

Regular **-ir** verbs of the **finir** family use **-is, -is, -it, -issons, -issez, -issent**: **je finis**, **nous finissons**. Not every verb ending in **-ir** belongs to this group: **partir** gives **nous partons**, not *nous partissons*. Many common verbs are irregular. The most valuable early forms are **être**: suis, es, est, sommes, êtes, sont; **avoir**: ai, as, a, avons, avez, ont; **aller**: vais, vas, va, allons, allez, vont; **faire**: fais, fais, fait, faisons, faites, font.

These verbs create useful messages immediately. **J'ai deux frères** means “I have two brothers.” **J'ai vingt ans** means “I am twenty years old,” literally using have. **J'ai faim**, **j'ai soif**, **j'ai froid**, and **j'ai besoin d'aide** describe hunger, thirst, feeling cold, and needing help. **Je suis froid** describes coldness as a personal or physical quality and does not normally mean “I feel cold.” Learn the full expression so the verb comes with the meaning.

Add **prendre**: prends, prends, prend, prenons, prenez, prennent; **venir**: viens, viens, vient, venons, venez, viennent; **pouvoir**: peux, peux, peut, pouvons, pouvez, peuvent; **vouloir**: veux, veux, veut, voulons, voulez, veulent; **devoir**: dois, dois, doit, devons, devez, doivent. With an infinitive, only the first verb is conjugated: **Je peux venir** — “I can come.” *Je peux viens* incorrectly gives the second verb its own finite form without a new clause.

Compare **Je sais nager** and **Je peux nager aujourd'hui**. The first says you know how to swim; the second says present circumstances permit it. **Savoir** is savoir a fact or skill, while **connaître** is knowing or being familiar with a person, place, or subject: **Je connais ce quartier**. The distinction is not captured by English “know,” so build separate sentence frames.

## A routine that includes yourself

A pronominal verb includes a pronoun connected to its subject: **se lever**, to get up. The present forms are **je me lève, tu te lèves, il se lève, nous nous levons, vous vous levez, elles se lèvent**. In **je me lave**, the subject washes himself or herself. Some pronominal verbs are less literally reflexive: **je m'appelle Lina**, **je me souviens**. Learn the dictionary form with **se**, because removing it may change the meaning or the required sentence structure.

Before a vowel sound, **me**, **te**, and **se** elide: **je m'habille**, **tu t'appelles**, **elle s'intéresse à la musique**. In a two-verb structure, the reflexive pronoun belongs with the infinitive: **Je vais me coucher**. The conjugated **vais** establishes the subject and near-future frame; **me coucher** names what I am going to do. Do not move **me** in front of **vais** merely because it often appears before a conjugated verb in simple present sentences.

Build a believable routine with time and frequency. **Je me lève vers sept heures. Je prends le petit déjeuner à la maison. Ensuite, je vais au travail en bus. Je rentre à dix-huit heures et je prépare le dîner.** “I get up around seven. I have breakfast at home. Then I go to work by bus. I return home at six p.m. and prepare dinner.” **Vers** expresses approximation; **à** marks the scheduled time. This gives a listener useful temporal structure without requiring a different tense.

Place common frequency adverbs naturally: **Je travaille souvent à la maison**, **Elle est toujours à l'heure**, **Nous allons parfois au cinéma**. Longer time expressions can frame the sentence: **Le samedi, je fais les courses**. The definite article with a day often indicates a repeated habit; **samedi** alone commonly refers to a particular Saturday in context. **Je travaille le lundi** differs from **Je travaille lundi** in precisely this way.

## Saying no without losing the sentence

The basic written negative surrounds the conjugated verb: **Je ne travaille pas**. Before a vowel sound use **n'**: **Je n'habite pas ici**. With an infinitive after a modal verb, negate the conjugated verb: **Je ne peux pas venir**. This means “I cannot come.” **Je peux ne pas venir** has a different scope: “I can choose not to come.” The location of negation affects meaning, not just surface style.

In everyday speech, **ne** is frequently omitted: **Je sais pas**, **On travaille pas demain**. You need to recognize this early because real recordings will contain it. In careful writing and the course's default answer models, keep **ne**. **Pas** remains essential in the ordinary construction. The sentence *je ne travaille* is not the normal A1 way to say “I do not work.” Some advanced literary uses differ, but they are not a reason to drop **pas** in ordinary exercises.

Replace **pas** with other negative expressions: **Je ne travaille jamais le dimanche** — “I never work on Sundays”; **Je ne travaille plus ici** — “I no longer work here”; **Je ne vois personne** — “I see nobody”; **Je ne comprends rien** — “I understand nothing.” Do not automatically add **pas** to **jamais** or **rien** in these basic patterns. **Encore** and **plus** need listening context: **Il travaille encore** means he still works; **Il ne travaille plus** means he no longer works.

Indefinite and partitive articles often become **de** after an ordinary negative: **J'ai un vélo → Je n'ai pas de vélo**; **J'ai des enfants → Je n'ai pas d'enfants**. A definite article remains: **Je n'aime pas le café**. After **être**, retain the article in identification: **Ce n'est pas un problème**. Contrasts can also preserve an article: **Je n'ai pas un frère, j'en ai deux**. First ask what the sentence is denying: existence/quantity, a category preference, or an identification.

## Asking questions that invite answers

There are three broad question patterns. In conversation, keep statement word order and use intonation: **Vous habitez ici ?** A neutral explicit frame is **Est-ce que vous habitez ici ?** Inversion gives **Habitez-vous ici ?**, common in formal questions and certain short expressions. These patterns can ask the same yes/no question but differ in register. Avoid a hybrid such as *Est-ce que habitez-vous ici ?*: it applies two structures at once.

Question words ask for a missing piece: **où** where, **quand** when, **comment** how, **pourquoi** why, **combien** how much/many, **qui** who, and **que/qu'est-ce que** what. **Où est la gare ?** asks where the station is. **Où est-ce que vous travaillez ?** asks where you work. **Vous travaillez où ?** is a natural conversational alternative. **Pourquoi ?** often receives **parce que**: **Parce que j'habite loin** — “Because I live far away.”

Use **quel/quelle/quels/quelles** before a noun: **Quelle heure est-il ?**, **Quel bus prenez-vous ?**, **Quelles langues parlez-vous ?** The form agrees with that noun. To ask “What is your name?” use **Comment vous appelez-vous ?** or **Vous vous appelez comment ?**, rather than translating English *what* mechanically. **Qu'est-ce que vous faites ?** means “What do you do/What are you doing?” **Qui fait le dîner ?** asks who makes dinner; here **qui** itself supplies the subject.

Respond to a negative question with care. **Vous n'habitez pas ici ? — Si, j'habite ici.** **Si** contradicts the negative assumption and means yes, I do. **Non, j'habite à Lyon** agrees that you do not live here. This small word prevents a common conversational confusion. When you do not understand a question, a repair phrase is a real communicative success: **Pardon, vous pouvez répéter plus lentement ?** You have kept the exchange going.

## Numbers, dates, and appointments

Learn numbers in messages, not only as a counting chain. Practice prices, apartment numbers, bus numbers, phone digits, and dates. **Vingt et un**, **trente et un**, and **quarante et un** include **et**, while **vingt-deux** does not. In standard France usage, seventy is **soixante-dix**, eighty **quatre-vingts**, and ninety **quatre-vingt-dix**. Belgian and Swiss usage may include **septante** and **nonante**, with **huitante** used in some Swiss regions. These are legitimate regional forms, not learner errors.

Final written **-s** depends on structure: **quatre-vingts** but **quatre-vingt-un**; **deux cents** but **deux cent trois**. Ordinal **premier/première** means first; **deuxième** means second, and other ordinals commonly use **-ième** with spelling adjustments. In dates, the first day is **le premier mai**; the second is **le deux mai**. Days and months normally take lowercase letters: **mardi**, **novembre**. Do not import English capitalization rules automatically.

Tell time with **Il est**: **Il est neuf heures**, **Il est neuf heures et demie**, **Il est dix heures moins le quart**. For timetables, twenty-four-hour time is clear: **Le train part à dix-sept heures quarante**. Distinguish **il est** for the current time from **à** for an event's time: **Il est huit heures; le cours commence à neuf heures**. **Midi** and **minuit** stand for noon and midnight.

Arrange a meeting with a complete exchange. **Vous êtes libre jeudi ? — Oui, après quinze heures. — On se retrouve devant la gare à quinze heures trente ? — D'accord. À jeudi !** “Are you free Thursday? — Yes, after three. — Shall we meet in front of the station at three thirty? — Agreed. See you Thursday!” The proposal includes day, time, and place, which makes it actionable. If one detail is uncertain, ask a precise follow-up rather than restarting the entire conversation.

## A short neighborhood reading

**Je m'appelle Nora et j'habite dans un petit appartement près de la gare. Mon immeuble est ancien, mais mon appartement est clair et calme. Le matin, je prends un café et je lis les nouvelles. Je travaille dans une librairie. Nous ouvrons à dix heures et nous fermons à dix-neuf heures. Ma collègue Amira est très patiente. Elle parle français, arabe et anglais. Le lundi, la librairie est fermée. Ce jour-là, je fais les courses et je téléphone à ma sœur. Je n'ai pas de voiture, mais le quartier est pratique : il y a une boulangerie, une pharmacie et deux arrêts de bus. Le samedi, des amis viennent chez moi. Nous préparons un repas et nous parlons longtemps.**

Nora lives in a small apartment near the station. Her building is old, while her apartment is bright and quiet. She works in a bookshop whose opening hours structure her day. On Mondays the shop is closed. She has no car but can manage because essential services are nearby. Friends visit on Saturdays. This is a connected portrait, not a list of unrelated present-tense sentences: place, routine, exception, and social life hold it together.

Notice **près de la gare**, which locates the apartment, and **chez moi**, which means at my home rather than a general physical “at me.” **Nous ouvrons** and **nous fermons** use the shop team as subject. **Le lundi** marks a repeated day. **Je n'ai pas de voiture** denies possession and therefore uses **de**. **Il y a** introduces what exists in the neighborhood; the expression remains **il y a** before a plural noun, not *ils y ont*.

Read the passage aloud once for phrasing, then listen using the site's French voice at a comfortable rate. Cover the passage and dictate only the first two sentences. Compare articles, endings, accents, and apostrophes separately. A missed silent plural is an orthographic problem; failing to identify **près de** is a chunk-recognition problem. Record the category so the next practice activity addresses the actual difficulty.

## Guided translation and diagnosis

Translate these messages into French before consulting the models. 1. My sister lives near the station. 2. We do not have a car. 3. What time does the shop open? 4. These houses are old. 5. I am twenty-five and I work in a bookshop. 6. We get up at seven on Mondays. 7. Can you repeat more slowly? 8. I like dogs, but I do not like this dog. 9. Her friend is French. 10. Are you free Thursday afternoon?

Models: **1. Ma sœur habite près de la gare.** *Ma* agrees with sister, and **de la** remains two words because the noun is feminine. **2. Nous n'avons pas de voiture.** The negative denies an indefinite possession. **3. À quelle heure est-ce que le magasin ouvre ?** An acceptable conversational version is **Le magasin ouvre à quelle heure ?** **4. Ces maisons sont anciennes.** Both noun and adjective are plural, and the adjective is feminine.

**5. J'ai vingt-cinq ans et je travaille dans une librairie.** Age uses **avoir**; a bookshop is **une librairie**, while a library is **une bibliothèque**. **6. Nous nous levons à sept heures le lundi.** The repeated **nous** has two jobs: subject and reflexive pronoun. **7. Vous pouvez répéter plus lentement ?** Polite **Pourriez-vous répéter plus lentement ?** will become more transparent when we study the conditional. **8. J'aime les chiens, mais je n'aime pas ce chien.** Generic preference retains the definite article.

**9. Son amie est française** if the friend is female; **Son ami est français** if male. English “her friend” leaves information French agreement may require, so both can be appropriate when context supplies no gender. **10. Vous êtes libre jeudi après-midi ?** With several people, **libres** is plural. Translation is the reconstruction of a message under contextual constraints; a single automatic answer should not erase valid alternatives.

Now translate into English: **Je connais cette rue. Nous sommes toujours à l'heure. Il ne travaille plus ici. Vous avez besoin d'aide ? Il y a de beaux jardins dans ce quartier.** Models: “I know/am familiar with this street.” “We are always on time.” “He no longer works here.” “Do you need help?” “There are beautiful gardens in this neighborhood.” If you wrote “He does not work more here,” you recognized words but missed the negative chunk **ne…plus**.

## Listening, speaking, and the chapter project

For listening, use five rounds with short material: listen for the situation; listen for names and times; listen for the exact target sentence; transcribe; compare with the transcript and listen again. Keep the answer hidden until you have attempted the task. In this chapter, prioritize articles, common verb phrases, and question boundaries. Slow synthetic speech can support decoding, but return to ordinary speed and add real human recordings as your confidence grows.

For speaking, begin with a model such as **Bonjour, je m'appelle… J'habite… Je travaille… Le matin…**. Replace details with truthful or clearly fictional information. Record two versions: one prepared, one after a few minutes without the script. Listen for intelligible vowels, steady phrase groups, and whether a listener can identify who does what and when. Speech recognition can show a transcript; it cannot certify accent quality or diagnose every pronunciation error. When possible, ask a competent speaker which one phrase was hardest to understand.

The project is a **new-neighbor welcome pack**. Write 150–180 words introducing yourself or a fictional resident, describing a home and neighborhood, explaining a weekly routine, and proposing a meeting. Include six varied verbs, three questions, two negatives, and specific times. Add a two-minute spoken version and a simulated exchange in which the listener asks for clarification. Revise the writing after a day, checking noun phrases first, verb forms second, and message completeness third.

# 03 — Time and movement: past events, future plans, and everyday choices

**Level:** A2. **Time:** 100 hours in ten ten-hour units. **Prerequisites:** the present tense, noun phrases, agreement, questions, negation, numbers, and everyday routines of Chapters 01–02. By the end, you will organize a trip, report what happened, buy food, give directions, and explain an intention or obligation. You will also begin replacing repeated objects with pronouns. The sequence is a course design, not an official CEFR grammar prescription.

Consider a small travel problem. Yesterday you bought a ticket. Today you discover that the departure time has changed. Tomorrow you will take a different train. You need language that keeps these three times distinct while allowing you to ask what to do next. French does not express every time relationship by changing a single verb ending: it also uses auxiliary verbs, infinitives, time expressions, and context. This chapter makes those mechanisms visible in manageable messages.

## Locating an event before choosing a form

Begin with **Aujourd'hui, je prends le train** — “Today, I am taking the train.” The time expression and present tense cooperate. **Hier, j'ai pris le train** moves the event into a completed past frame. **Demain, je vais prendre le train** presents a future plan. Notice what changes: **prends** is one conjugated word; **ai pris** combines an auxiliary with a participle; **vais prendre** combines conjugated **aller** with an infinitive.

An **auxiliary** is a verb used to help form a grammatical construction. It is still conjugated, but it does not necessarily keep its ordinary lexical meaning. In **j'ai pris**, **ai** does not mean that I possess taking. It helps place **prendre** in the passé composé. A **past participle** is a form such as **pris**, **parlé**, or **fini**. An **infinitive** is a dictionary form such as **prendre**. Confusing these categories causes errors like *j'ai prendre* or *je vais pris*.

Useful time anchors include **hier**, yesterday; **avant-hier**, the day before yesterday; **ce matin**, this morning; **la semaine dernière**, last week; **aujourd'hui**, today; **ce soir**, this evening; **demain**, tomorrow; **après-demain**, the day after tomorrow; and **la semaine prochaine**, next week. Their interpretation depends on the speaking time. **Le lendemain**, which you will use more in narratives, means the next day relative to a past reference point, not necessarily tomorrow relative to now.

Try a timeline before translating: purchase yesterday, complaint now, replacement trip tomorrow. Then write **J'ai acheté un billet hier. Le train ne part pas à l'heure prévue. Demain, je vais prendre un autre train.** The purpose of the timeline is to protect the event relationships. If you merely choose a French form that resembles an English tense name, you can produce a grammatical sentence that tells the wrong story.

## Building the passé composé with avoir

The most common pattern is present **avoir** plus a past participle: **j'ai parlé, tu as parlé, elle a parlé, nous avons parlé, vous avez parlé, ils ont parlé**. The auxiliary changes with the subject. In this basic construction, the participle does not change merely because the subject is feminine or plural: **Elle a parlé**, **Elles ont parlé**. Later chapters will examine agreement with preceding direct objects; do not invent subject agreement here.

Regular participles are **-er → -é**, **-ir** of the finishing family **→ -i**, and many **-re → -u**: **parler → parlé**, **finir → fini**, **vendre → vendu**. These patterns do not cover all verbs. Learn frequent irregular participles in useful phrases: **faire → fait**, **prendre → pris**, **voir → vu**, **lire → lu**, **écrire → écrit**, **dire → dit**, **mettre → mis**, **avoir → eu**, **être → été**, **boire → bu**, **pouvoir → pu**, **vouloir → voulu**, **devoir → dû**.

Work through **J'ai acheté un billet**. First identify the event: buy a ticket. The subject is I, so choose **ai**. The verb **acheter** belongs to the **-er** pattern, so its participle is **acheté**. Keep **un billet** as the direct object. The complete sentence can mean “I bought a ticket” or “I have bought a ticket,” depending on context. French passé composé and English present perfect do not map one-to-one.

Negation surrounds the auxiliary: **Je n'ai pas acheté de billet**. In a question with **est-ce que**, keep ordinary subject–auxiliary order: **Est-ce que vous avez acheté un billet ?** In inversion: **Avez-vous acheté un billet ?** A short adverb commonly appears between auxiliary and participle: **J'ai déjà acheté un billet**, “I have already bought a ticket”; **Je n'ai jamais pris ce train**, “I have never taken this train.” Longer adverbial phrases can occur elsewhere.

The passé composé commonly presents bounded events or completed sequences: **J'ai fermé la porte, j'ai pris le bus et je suis arrivé à neuf heures**. It is not a universal equivalent of every English past form. Describing a background situation, an ongoing past action, or a repeated past habit will often require the imparfait, developed in Chapter 04. Here, establish the completed event frame first rather than forcing every past meaning into the one form you know.

## Building the passé composé with être

A limited set of intransitive verbs commonly uses **être**, including **aller, venir, arriver, partir, entrer, sortir, monter, descendre, retourner, rester, tomber, naître**, and **mourir** in the relevant constructions. Their participles agree with the subject: **Il est arrivé**, **Elle est arrivée**, **Ils sont arrivés**, **Elles sont arrivées**. The feminine **-e** and plural **-s** are often silent, but they remain part of written grammar.

Do not conclude that “all movement verbs take être.” **Marcher**, **courir**, **voyager**, and **nager** normally take **avoir**: **J'ai marché pendant une heure**. The rule concerns particular verbs and constructions, not whether a person physically changes location. A mnemonic list can help retrieval, but examples reveal the real boundary. **Je suis allé à la gare** and **J'ai couru jusqu'à la gare** describe movement with different auxiliaries.

Several verbs alternate according to whether they take a direct object. **Elle est sortie** means “She went out.” **Elle a sorti les clés** means “She took out the keys.” **Nous sommes montés** means “We went up”; **Nous avons monté les valises** means “We carried the suitcases up.” Do not choose the auxiliary from the infinitive alone when the construction changes. Ask whether the verb is describing the subject's own movement or an action on a directly expressed object.

Now build **Mes amies sont parties**. The subject is third-person plural, so choose **sont**. **Partir** uses **être** here and has participle **parti**. **Mes amies** is feminine plural, so write **parties**. If the group were mixed and referred to as **mes amis**, write **partis** under traditional standard agreement. The written endings encode information that the spoken sentence may not reveal independently.

Pronominal verbs use **être** in compound tenses: **Je me suis levé**, **Elle s'est couchée**. Their agreement is more complex than “always agree with the subject.” Compare **Elle s'est lavée** and **Elle s'est lavé les mains**. In the second, **les mains** is the direct object and follows the verb, while **se** has an indirect role. At this stage, learn these common models accurately; Chapter 04 and later reference material will give you a method for analyzing the roles.

## Reporting an event without overloading the listener

A useful event report answers when, where, what happened, and what the result was. **Hier soir, j'ai perdu mon portefeuille dans le métro. Je suis descendu à République. J'ai cherché dans mon sac, mais je n'ai rien trouvé. Ensuite, j'ai téléphoné au service des objets trouvés.** “Yesterday evening, I lost my wallet on the metro. I got off at République. I searched my bag but found nothing. Then I called the lost-property service.”

Notice the alternation of **avoir** and **être** according to the verb construction, not according to narrative importance. **Descendre** has no direct object here and uses **être**. **Chercher** and **trouver** take **avoir**. **Rien** appears after the auxiliary in **je n'ai rien trouvé**. The connectors **mais** and **ensuite** signal an obstacle and the next response. You do not need a new sophisticated connector for every sentence; clarity comes from a well-chosen sequence.

When reporting a problem, include what help you need. **J'ai perdu ma carte. Je voudrais savoir où déclarer la perte.** The polite **je voudrais** is a fixed useful form of the conditional, which will be studied systematically later. You may use a helpful chunk before knowing its full paradigm if you understand its function and limits. It does not authorize a random mixture of present, future, and past endings.

For listening practice, first hear the report without its transcript and list only events. On the second pass, add locations and time references. Then transcribe one sentence and mark the auxiliary in one color and participle in another. If you miss **je suis descendu**, compare the complete sound group with **j'ai cherché**. Sound recognition often improves when you understand the grammatical alternatives that could plausibly occur.

## Future plans: aller plus an infinitive

The near future, often called **futur proche**, uses present **aller** plus an infinitive: **je vais partir, tu vas partir, il va partir, nous allons partir, vous allez partir, elles vont partir**. It commonly presents an intention, a planned development, or something connected to the present situation. “Near” is not a rigid calendar limit. **L'année prochaine, je vais étudier à Lille** can be natural when the plan is established now.

Only **aller** is conjugated. In **Nous allons réserver une chambre**, **allons** agrees with nous, while **réserver** remains an infinitive. Negate the conjugated verb: **Nous n'allons pas réserver ici**. Put a reflexive pronoun with its infinitive: **Je vais me reposer**. Later, when replacing **une chambre** with **la**, the object pronoun also belongs before the infinitive: **Nous allons la réserver**. The learner's question should be “Which verb owns this object?” rather than “Do all pronouns go at the very start?”

Present **aller** can also mean movement toward a destination: **Je vais à la gare**. In **Je vais prendre le train**, it helps construct the future. You can combine the meanings: **Je vais à la gare parce que je vais prendre le train**. Context and the following complement distinguish them. A preposition plus a place points to ordinary going; a following infinitive commonly supplies a planned action.

Use the form to make a real weekend plan: **Samedi, je vais retrouver une amie. Nous allons visiter un musée. Après, nous allons déjeuner près du fleuve. S'il pleut, nous allons prendre le bus.** The last sentence introduces a useful conditional pattern with **si + present** and a future meaning in the main clause. Do not put a future tense immediately after ordinary conditional **si**: **si tu viens**, not *si tu viendras*.

### A just-completed action and an imminent action

Present **venir de + infinitive** presents a recent completion: **Je viens de réserver une chambre** — “I have just booked a room”; **Nous venons d'arriver** — “We have just arrived.” Conjugate venir for the subject, retain de, and keep the following verb in the infinitive. De elides before an appropriate vowel sound. Compare ordinary movement **Je viens de Lyon**, “I come from Lyon,” where de introduces a place rather than an infinitive. The following complement tells you which construction is present.

**Être sur le point de + infinitive** means to be about to do something: **Le train est sur le point de partir**. It emphasizes imminence more strongly than a general plan such as **Je vais voyager cet été**. Neither construction is a new one-word verb tense; both combine familiar conjugated verbs with infinitives. On a small timeline, put **je viens de partir** just before now, **je suis sur le point de partir** just after now, and **je vais partir en août** at the planned future date. The actual distance meant by “just” depends on context, so it has no universal fixed number of minutes.

## The simple future and its practical boundaries

The **futur simple** adds **-ai, -as, -a, -ons, -ez, -ont** to a future stem. For many **-er** and **-ir** verbs, the stem is the infinitive: **je parlerai**, **nous finirons**. For many **-re** verbs, remove the final **e**: **je prendrai**, **vous attendrez**. These endings resemble forms of **avoir**, a historical connection that can help memory, but the modern result is one written word.

Common irregular stems include **être → ser-**, **avoir → aur-**, **aller → ir-**, **faire → fer-**, **venir → viendr-**, **voir → verr-**, **pouvoir → pourr-**, **vouloir → voudr-**, **devoir → devr-**, and **savoir → saur-**. Thus **je serai**, **tu auras**, **nous irons**, **elles pourront**. Practice the stem and ending as separate choices: desired verb gives **viendr-**; subject vous gives **-ez**; result **vous viendrez**.

The future can announce a prediction, promise, or scheduled consequence: **Le train arrivera à dix-huit heures**; **Je vous téléphonerai demain**; **Vous recevrez une confirmation**. The near future and simple future overlap; do not reduce them to “soon” versus “far away.” Compare **Attention, le verre va tomber !**, tied to an immediate visible development, with **Un jour, tu comprendras**, a prediction. Real choice also depends on register, context, and how the speaker presents the event.

In a future time clause with **quand**, French often uses future where English uses present: **Quand tu arriveras, nous dînerons** — “When you arrive, we will have dinner.” Contrast **Si tu arrives tôt, nous dînerons ensemble** — “If you arrive early, we will have dinner together.” Ordinary conditional **si** takes present here; future **quand** can take future. The conjunction changes the grammatical expectation. Learn the pair rather than a vague rule that all subordinate clauses use present.

## Instructions: the imperative is a conversation action

The imperative gives an instruction, invitation, or request without an expressed subject pronoun. It has familiar singular, nous, and vous forms: **Écoute**, **Écoutons**, **Écoutez**. With regular **-er** verbs, familiar singular normally drops the present-tense **-s**: **Tu regardes → Regarde !** The same happens in **Va !** from aller. A linking **-s** returns before **y** or **en** in relevant constructions: **Vas-y**, **Manges-en**.

Common irregular imperatives are **sois/soyons/soyez**, **aie/ayons/ayez**, and **sache/sachons/sachez**. You will meet **Veuillez patienter** — “Please wait” — as a polite service formula. Do not assume that an imperative is always rude; tone, relationship, wording, and situation matter. **Entrez, je vous en prie** is welcoming. **Vous pouvez tourner à gauche** may be a gentler instruction than a bare command in some conversations.

Negation surrounds the imperative: **Ne prenez pas cette rue**. In an affirmative command, object pronouns follow with hyphens: **Prenez-le**, “Take it.” In a negative command they return before the verb: **Ne le prenez pas**. With reflexive commands, compare **Lève-toi** and **Ne te lève pas**; **me/te** generally become **moi/toi** after an affirmative imperative, with special behavior before **en/y**. The full pronoun-order system will be developed in Chapter 04.

Give directions as a sequence with landmarks: **Sortez de la gare. Traversez la place. Tournez à droite après la pharmacie. Continuez tout droit pendant deux minutes. La mairie est à gauche, en face de la poste.** A listener can follow this because each instruction has an action and a recognizable reference point. “Go there, then turn” is grammatically possible but practically weak. Ask the listener to repeat the crucial turn if the route is complicated.

## Food and quantities: not every noun names a countable unit

Compare **un pain** and **du pain**. **Un pain** can refer to one loaf or a type of bread in context; **du pain** refers to some bread without counting units. The partitive articles are **du** before a masculine singular noun, **de la** before a feminine singular noun, and **de l'** before an appropriate vowel sound. **Des** commonly introduces plural quantities: **des pâtes**, **des légumes**. The exact article depends on how the speaker construes the noun in that message.

Use **Je voudrais du fromage et de la salade** for unspecified amounts. Compare **Je voudrais un fromage** if asking for a whole cheese or one variety, and **Je voudrais le fromage de la région** if identifying a particular cheese. English can hide these choices behind “cheese”; French makes you choose a determiner. Begin from meaning: category, specific referent, countable unit, or unspecified amount.

After many quantity expressions, use **de/d'**: **un kilo de pommes**, **une bouteille d'eau**, **beaucoup de travail**, **un peu de sel**, **trop de bruit**, **assez de temps**. Do not add a partitive after **beaucoup de**: *beaucoup du pain* is not the ordinary way to say a lot of bread. **Beaucoup du pain que tu as acheté** is a different, specific construction involving part of a defined set; it should not become your default quantity model.

An ordinary negative often changes indefinite or partitive articles to **de**: **Je mange du pain → Je ne mange pas de pain**; **J'achète des pommes → Je n'achète pas de pommes**. Definite preference retains its article: **Je n'aime pas le pain**. With **être**, identification retains the article: **Ce n'est pas de la soupe**. Contrast also matters: **Je ne bois pas du thé, je bois du café** corrects the kind of drink. These patterns make sense when you ask what the negative targets.

## Shopping as a complete interaction

At a market, begin with greeting and request: **Bonjour, je voudrais deux cents grammes de fromage, s'il vous plaît.** The seller may ask **Celui-ci ?**, “This one?” or **Vous en voulez combien ?**, “How much of it do you want?” You need not produce every form immediately to understand its role. Reply with a quantity: **Deux cents grammes, merci.** Then ask **Ça fait combien ?** or **Combien je vous dois ?** The latter is conversational; **Combien est-ce que je vous dois ?** is another option.

**Vendeur : Bonjour, vous désirez ? — Cliente : Bonjour. Je voudrais un kilo de tomates et une bouteille d'huile d'olive. — Vendeur : Vous voulez autre chose ? — Cliente : Oui, est-ce que vous avez du pain complet ? — Vendeur : Il n'y en a plus aujourd'hui. — Cliente : D'accord, ce sera tout. Je peux payer par carte ?**

The customer wants tomatoes, oil, and wholemeal bread; the bread has run out. **Il n'y en a plus** is a useful chunk meaning there is no more of it. Chapter 04 will unpack **y** and **en**; for now, connect the whole phrase to an unavailable product. **Ce sera tout** uses the simple future to close the order, “That will be all.” **Par carte**, **en espèces**, and **sans contact** cover common payment methods.

Practice a substitution with genuine constraints: a limited budget, an allergy, or an unavailable product. **Je suis allergique aux noix** conveys an allergy; **sans noix** means without nuts. Do not rely on a language exercise for medical safety: in a real transaction, clearly confirm ingredients with the person responsible. The language goal is an unambiguous request and confirmation, not memorizing one sentence without understanding the answer.

## Objects and the first useful pronouns

An object completes a verb's meaning. In **Je cherche la gare**, the station is what I am looking for. French **chercher** directly takes its object, unlike English “look for,” so do not add **pour** by translation. A **direct object** is attached without a preposition in the relevant verb construction. An **indirect object** is introduced by a preposition such as **à**. The French construction, not the English translation, determines the role.

Replace a previously identified direct object with **le**, **la**, or **les**: **Je cherche le musée → Je le cherche**; **Je cherche la gare → Je la cherche**; **Je cherche les clés → Je les cherche**. Before a vowel sound, **le/la** become **l'**: **Je l'attends**. These pronouns go before the conjugated verb in a simple statement. Their written form can look like an article, but a following verb reveals the pronoun's job.

For people receiving something through **à**, **lui** can mean to him or to her, and **leur** to them: **Je téléphone à ma sœur → Je lui téléphone**; **J'écris à mes parents → Je leur écris**. Do not use **la** because the English sentence mentions a woman: **téléphoner à** requires an indirect role. Some **à + person** constructions behave differently, as in **penser à elle**. You will build a verb-construction inventory rather than apply lui indiscriminately.

Other object pronouns include **me, te, nous, vous**. **Vous m'attendez ?** means “Are you waiting for me?” **Je vous parle** means “I am speaking to you.” The same surface pronoun can serve a direct or indirect role, so its spelling alone does not identify the construction. In a two-verb phrase, place the pronoun with the verb it completes: **Je vais les acheter**, “I am going to buy them.” Here **les** is the object of acheter, not of aller.

## Travel prepositions and a useful distinction about duration

For many feminine countries use **en**: **en France**, **en Belgique**. Many masculine countries take **au**: **au Canada**, **au Japon**. Plural country names take **aux**: **aux États-Unis**. Countries beginning with a vowel often take **en** regardless of masculine gender: **en Iran**. Cities usually take **à**: **à Paris**, **à Montréal**. Some place names have articles and their own conventions, so learn actual destinations as phrases.

Return or origin commonly uses **de/d'**, **du**, or **des** according to the name: **de France**, **du Canada**, **des États-Unis**, **de Paris**. Transport expressions include **en train**, **en voiture**, **en bus**, **à pied**, and **à vélo**; actual usage has some variation, especially with bicycle and motorbike phrases. You do not need a speculative geometric rule about being inside every vehicle. Store standard high-frequency combinations.

Compare **pendant**, **depuis**, **il y a**, and **dans**. **J'ai travaillé pendant deux heures** gives the duration of a completed activity. **Je travaille ici depuis deux ans** says the situation began two years ago and continues now; French uses present in this basic continuing situation. **J'ai commencé il y a deux ans** locates a beginning two years ago. **Je partirai dans deux heures** locates departure two hours from now. These four messages answer different time questions.

English “I have lived here for two years” can therefore become **J'habite ici depuis deux ans**. Translating it as **J'ai habité ici pendant deux ans** normally presents that residence as a completed period and may imply you no longer live here. That is not merely a stylistic difference. Draw an arrow continuing into now for **depuis** and a closed interval for completed **pendant**; the picture helps you preserve the intended relationship.

Make the contrast personal with four controlled messages. Choose a course that began last month and continues: **J'étudie le français depuis un mois**. Choose a completed study session: **J'ai étudié pendant une heure ce matin**. Locate its beginning relative to now: **J'ai commencé il y a trois heures**. Locate the next session relative to now: **Je vais recommencer dans deux heures**. Check that the invented clock times are compatible before practicing the sentences aloud. If it is noon and you began three hours ago, the beginning was nine; a one-hour session then ended at ten. A new session in two hours begins at fourteen hours. This small calculation exposes a real meaning difference that a list of translations can hide. Ask a partner to reconstruct the schedule from your four sentences, then change the starting time and repeat without reading.

## A travel narrative with practical consequences

**Samedi dernier, Élodie est partie de Nantes avec son frère. Ils ont pris un train pour Bordeaux. À la gare, ils ont acheté des sandwichs et une bouteille d'eau. Élodie a mis les billets dans son sac, mais son frère a oublié son portefeuille à la maison. Ils sont arrivés à midi et ils ont rejoint leur amie Inès devant la gare. L'après-midi, ils ont visité le centre-ville. Aujourd'hui, dimanche, ils vont louer des vélos si le temps reste beau. Ils rentreront lundi soir. Avant le départ, Inès leur dit : « Vérifiez les horaires et n'oubliez pas vos affaires ! »**

This account reports a completed outward journey and then shifts to the characters' plans. **Sont partis** and **sont arrivés** agree with a mixed group under traditional standard grammar. **Ont pris**, **ont acheté**, and **ont visité** use avoir without subject agreement. **Aujourd'hui, dimanche** establishes the current speaking date, while **lundi soir** identifies the planned return. **Leur** in **Inès leur dit** replaces **à Élodie et à son frère**. The command **n'oubliez pas** uses vous because Inès addresses both travelers.

Retell the story from Élodie's viewpoint: **Je suis partie… Nous avons pris… Mon frère a oublié…** Several forms change because the subject changes, while the basic events remain the same. Then move the whole story into an itinerary written before the trip: **Nous allons partir… Nous prendrons…** This is not a word-swapping exercise; you must also remove information that a traveler could not reasonably know in advance, such as an accidental forgotten wallet.

For a listening task, let the site read only the first five sentences. Type the French events, then an English account. Compare meaning before spelling: did you correctly identify who forgot the wallet? Next inspect auxiliary choice and accents. A transcript that is orthographically close but assigns the mistake to the wrong person has failed the communication task, even if an automatic string comparison happens to reward several matching words.

## Guided translation with explained alternatives

Translate: 1. Yesterday I bought two tickets. 2. My sister arrived at nine. 3. We did not find the station. 4. Tomorrow we are going to rent bicycles. 5. When you arrive, I will call you. 6. If it rains, we will take the bus. 7. Take this street and turn left. 8. Do not forget your passport. 9. I would like a bottle of water. 10. I have lived here for three months.

Models: **1. Hier, j'ai acheté deux billets.** Acheter uses avoir. **2. Ma sœur est arrivée à neuf heures.** Arriver uses être here, and the subject is feminine singular. **3. Nous n'avons pas trouvé la gare.** The definite object remains **la gare**; negation surrounds avons. **4. Demain, nous allons louer des vélos.** Simple future **nous louerons** is also possible, depending on how the plan is presented.

**5. Quand tu arriveras, je t'appellerai.** Future quand takes a future form; **te** elides before appeler. With polite address use **Quand vous arriverez, je vous appellerai**. **6. S'il pleut, nous prendrons le bus.** Si takes present in this ordinary future condition. **7. Prenez cette rue et tournez à gauche.** The vous imperative omits the subject. **8. N'oubliez pas votre passeport.** The possessive agrees with passeport and the address is polite singular or plural.

**9. Je voudrais une bouteille d'eau.** A quantity container is followed by **de**, elided before eau. **10. J'habite ici depuis trois mois.** The continuing residence uses present plus depuis. Compare the reverse translation **J'ai habité ici pendant trois mois**, “I lived here for three months,” which presents a completed period. An answer checker should explain the difference rather than merely mark both strings as past-time expressions.

Translate into English: **Elle a sorti les clés. Elle est sortie à huit heures. Je vais les acheter. Il n'y a plus de pain. Vous recevrez une confirmation demain.** Models: “She took out the keys.” “She went out at eight.” “I am going to buy them.” “There is no bread left.” “You will receive a confirmation tomorrow.” The first pair is a compact test of construction-based auxiliary choice; the third tests whether you link the object pronoun to its infinitive.

# 04 — Telling a story: background, sequence, and connections

**Level:** A2 toward B1. **Time:** 100 hours, divided into ten ten-hour units. **Prerequisites:** present-tense sentences, passé composé, future plans, basic quantities, and introductory object pronouns. This chapter develops a connected account: what the situation was, what changed, what had happened before, and how people and things relate. It also gives you practical language for travel disruptions and routine administration. CEFR alignment is an instructional judgment about tasks rather than an official inventory of prescribed structures.

Imagine explaining why you missed an appointment. “I arrived late” is an event. Your listener also needs the background: it was raining, the bus service was disrupted, and you had left home without the correct document. A useful narrative organizes these relationships. French gives you several tools for doing so, especially the imparfait, passé composé, plus-que-parfait, relative clauses, and pronouns. The aim is not to put every new form into every paragraph; it is to make the account easy to reconstruct.

## A narrative has a viewpoint

Start with **À huit heures, il pleuvait. J'attendais le bus. Soudain, mon téléphone a sonné.** “At eight, it was raining. I was waiting for the bus. Suddenly, my phone rang.” The first two sentences invite the listener to look inside a situation in progress. The last presents a bounded event that advances the sequence. This contrast is often more useful than the inaccurate shortcut “imparfait means long; passé composé means short.” A very long event can be presented as a completed whole.

Compare **J'ai travaillé pendant dix ans dans cette entreprise** and **À cette époque, je travaillais dans cette entreprise**. The first closes a ten-year period and presents it as a unit. The second places you inside the earlier situation, without asserting its endpoints. The duration of ten years does not force the imparfait. The speaker's viewpoint and the message's organization matter.

Now compare **Il était malade quand je suis arrivé** and **Il a été malade pendant trois jours**. The first gives a state already in place at arrival. The second presents a completed period of illness. **Être** can occur in either tense. Do not memorize “description verbs always take imparfait.” Instead ask whether you are opening a background window or reporting a bounded occurrence or period.

Time expressions help, but they are not automatic tense switches. **Hier** can introduce **Hier, il pleuvait** or **Hier, il a plu pendant deux heures**. **Souvent** frequently accompanies habits in the imparfait, but **Cette semaine, il m'a souvent appelé** can summarize several completed calls. A good learner uses markers as evidence while still checking what the sentence claims about the event.

## Forming the imparfait without guessing

Take the present **nous** form, remove **-ons**, and add **-ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient**. **Nous parlons → parl- → je parlais, tu parlais, il parlait, nous parlions, vous parliez, elles parlaient**. **Nous finissons → finiss- → je finissais**. **Nous prenons → pren- → je prenais**. This procedure gives you a reliable stem even for many verbs whose infinitive does not transparently reveal it.

The important exception is **être**, with stem **ét-**: **j'étais, tu étais, elle était, nous étions, vous étiez, ils étaient**. Distinguish the stem from the ending during practice. If the subject is vous and the verb is prendre, retrieve **pren-** then **-iez**, producing **vous preniez**. Saying a complete sentence such as **Vous preniez toujours le même bus** turns the chart into a usable relationship.

Spelling may preserve sound: **nous mangeons → je mangeais**, but **nous mangions**, because the i already supports the soft g. **Nous commençons → je commençais**, but **nous commencions**. Verbs with stems ending in i can show double written i in nous/vous: **nous étudiions**, **vous étudiiez**. Those spellings can look surprising while following the ordinary formation procedure. Do not remove a letter merely because the result appears visually unfamiliar.

Pronunciation separates written distinctions imperfectly. **Je parlais**, **tu parlais**, **il parlait**, and **ils parlaient** often share the same ending sound. The subject and context carry person and number. The **nous/vous** forms are more audibly distinct. Listen to phrases and subject–verb groups rather than treating every written ending as a separately pronounced syllable.

## Background, habits, and interrupted activities

For a repeated past routine, use the imparfait naturally: **Quand j'étais enfant, je passais les vacances chez mes grands-parents. Nous allions au marché le samedi.** “When I was a child, I spent the holidays at my grandparents' home. We went to the market on Saturdays.” The narrative presents an established pattern, not a counted list of completed visits. Add **d'habitude**, **à cette époque**, or **tous les étés** when helpful, without assuming the marker alone determines the tense.

For an activity in progress when something happened: **Je préparais le dîner quand tu as appelé**. The cooking forms the background; the call enters it. You can reverse the sentence order without changing that relationship: **Quand tu as appelé, je préparais le dîner**. A learner who chooses tense from sentence order will fail here. Sketch overlapping activity and event on a timeline instead.

Two simultaneous background activities can both use imparfait: **Pendant que je préparais le dîner, mon frère mettait la table**. A sequence of completed actions commonly uses passé composé: **J'ai préparé le dîner, puis mon frère a mis la table**. The first says the activities overlapped; the second presents one after the other. This is why tense is part of meaning rather than a decorative layer added after translation.

Some verbs change their practical interpretation with viewpoint. **Je connaissais cette personne** describes existing familiarity; **J'ai connu cette personne à Lyon** can mean I met or came to know that person in Lyon. **Je voulais partir** describes an intention; **J'ai voulu partir** presents a bounded attempt or decision, depending on context. Avoid rigid bilingual glosses that conceal these differences. Read enough surrounding text to see what the speaker is doing.

## A worked revision: from event list to coherent story

First draft: **Hier, j'ai quitté la maison. J'ai pris le bus. J'ai vu un accident. Je suis arrivé en retard.** This is understandable, but it leaves motives and circumstances unclear. Add background: **Hier, il pleuvait et la circulation était très lente. J'ai quitté la maison à huit heures. Comme le métro était fermé, j'ai pris le bus. Pendant que nous traversions le centre-ville, j'ai vu un accident. Je suis arrivé au bureau avec vingt minutes de retard.**

Now inspect each choice. **Il pleuvait** and **la circulation était très lente** establish the scene. **J'ai quitté** moves to a completed action. **Le métro était fermé** provides the reason already true when the choice was made. **Nous traversions** opens the activity in progress; **j'ai vu** marks the observation within it. **Je suis arrivé** gives the outcome. The story is better because the relationships are explicit, not simply because it contains more tenses.

Do not add an imparfait everywhere the English translation uses “was.” **I was born in Lille** is **Je suis né à Lille** or **Je suis née à Lille**. **I was invited** can involve a passive construction rather than an imparfait. English forms and French forms package meaning differently. Identify the event structure first; consult a model for constructions you have not learned rather than extend a one-word equivalence.

For a speaking version, shorten less relevant details and preserve the key causal chain. **Je suis arrivé en retard parce que le métro était fermé. J'ai pris le bus, mais il y avait beaucoup de circulation.** A spoken account should answer the listener's practical concern. You can add the accident if asked; good narrative competence includes deciding which details belong in the first telling.

## An earlier past: the plus-que-parfait

The **plus-que-parfait** places an event before an already established past reference point. Build it with imparfait **avoir** or **être** plus the past participle: **j'avais acheté**, **elle était partie**, **nous avions compris**. Auxiliary choice and participle agreement follow the same broad patterns as the passé composé. The difference is the auxiliary's tense and therefore the time relationship.

Compare **Quand je suis arrivé, elle est partie** with **Quand je suis arrivé, elle était partie**. The first typically presents her departure at or after my arrival in sequence; the second says she had already left before I arrived. A missing **déjà** does not remove the anterior relationship: the plus-que-parfait itself expresses it. Add **déjà** when emphasizing that the earlier event was completed by that point.

Work through **J'ai raté le train parce que j'avais noté une mauvaise heure**. The missed train is the narrative result, **j'ai raté**. Writing down the wrong time happened earlier, **j'avais noté**. The cause is therefore grammatically ordered before its consequence even though it appears later in the sentence. The tense helps the listener reconstruct chronology without requiring the storyteller to present every event in chronological order.

Negation surrounds the auxiliary: **Je n'avais pas reçu le message**. With a short adverb: **J'avais déjà réservé une chambre**. With être: **Mes amies étaient arrivées avant moi**. For pronominal verbs: **Elle s'était levée tôt**. Do not construct *elle avait été arrivée* for ordinary “she had arrived”; that inserts an unnecessary extra auxiliary and does not follow the compound tense pattern.

Use earlier-past forms only when a past reference point exists or is understood. If the whole message is simply “I bought bread yesterday,” **J'ai acheté du pain hier** is sufficient. A plus-que-parfait needs a relationship such as “I had bought bread before the guests arrived.” Learning a more advanced form does not make it preferable in every past sentence. Precision comes from choosing the form the temporal relationship requires.

## Direct and indirect objects: follow the French verb

In **J'ai donné le dossier à Samir**, **le dossier** is the direct object, and **à Samir** identifies the recipient. A direct object is connected without a preposition in this construction. An indirect object is connected through a preposition. French verb patterns determine these roles: **écouter quelqu'un** is direct, even though English “listen to someone” includes to; **téléphoner à quelqu'un** is indirect, even though English “phone someone” does not.

Replace direct objects with **le/la/les**: **J'ai lu le dossier → Je l'ai lu**; **J'ai trouvé les clés → Je les ai trouvées**. Replace certain **à + person** complements with **lui/leur**: **J'ai parlé à Samir → Je lui ai parlé**; **J'ai écrit à mes collègues → Je leur ai écrit**. **Lui** does not identify male versus female: it can replace à Samir or à Léa. **Leur** is a pronoun here and does not take a plural s.

Not all prepositional person complements become lui/leur. **Je pense à Léa → Je pense à elle**; **Je voyage avec Samir → Je voyage avec lui**. The stressed pronouns are **moi, toi, lui, elle, nous, vous, eux, elles**. They appear after many prepositions and for emphasis: **Moi, je préfère attendre**. Do not treat every English “to her” as a signal for lui. Learn **penser à quelqu'un**, **parler à quelqu'un**, and **parler de quelqu'un** as separate frames.

With two verbs, the object usually goes with its own verb: **Je peux le faire**, **Je vais lui téléphoner**, **Je voudrais les voir**. Contrast **Je lui promets de venir**: **lui** completes **promets**, because I promise to someone, while venir describes what I promise. This is a practical diagnostic method: expand the pronoun back into its full noun phrase and determine which verb needs it.

## Y: a place or a selected à-complement

**Y** often replaces a place complement: **Je vais à la mairie → J'y vais**; **Elle habite à Lyon → Elle y habite**; **Les documents sont dans le tiroir → Ils y sont**. It can stand for a location introduced by several prepositions, not only à. English may translate it as there, but that translation is a clue rather than a full definition. Identify the location recoverable from context.

Y also replaces certain **à + thing/idea** complements: **Je pense à ce problème → J'y pense**; **Je participe à la réunion → J'y participe**. For a specific person, ordinary careful French often uses a stressed pronoun instead: **Je pense à Marie → Je pense à elle**. Do not infer from **j'y pense** that y is a universal substitute for any phrase beginning with à.

In simple statements y goes before the verb: **Nous y allons demain**. In a compound tense it goes before the auxiliary: **Nous y sommes allés hier**. With an infinitive: **Nous allons y retourner**. Negation encloses the pronoun–verb group around the conjugated verb: **Nous n'y allons pas**. The pronoun has not changed its role; the surrounding verbal construction determines its position.

Practice a real exchange: **Tu vas à la mairie ce matin ? — Oui, j'y vais à dix heures. — Tu peux déposer ce document ? — Oui, je vais y déposer le document.** An ordinary speaker might then avoid repetition with a second pronoun, but first make the location relationship clear. Replacing every noun at once can make a beginner's sentence impossible to interpret even when the order is technically correct.

## En: quantities, sources, and selected de-complements

**En** often replaces a noun introduced by a partitive or indefinite quantity: **Tu veux du café ? — Oui, j'en veux**. **Tu as des billets ? — Oui, j'en ai**. With a number or quantity expression, keep that quantity: **J'ai trois billets → J'en ai trois**; **Je veux beaucoup de café → J'en veux beaucoup**. The number answers how many while en identifies what is being counted.

Compare **J'ai les billets → Je les ai** with **J'ai des billets → J'en ai**. The first refers to a definite known set; the second introduces an indefinite quantity. **J'ai deux billets → J'en ai deux** retains two. These contrasts explain why substituting le/les for every English “it/them” is insufficient. The original noun phrase's structure matters.

En can replace a source place introduced by de: **Je reviens de Paris → J'en reviens**. It also replaces selected **de + thing/idea** complements: **Je parle de ce projet → J'en parle**; **J'ai besoin de ce document → J'en ai besoin**. When referring clearly to a specific person, a stressed pronoun is often preferable or required by the construction: **Je parle de Léa → Je parle d'elle**. Actual usage includes variation, but the distinction is a sound careful-writing habit.

Place en before the conjugated verb or auxiliary: **J'en veux**, **J'en ai acheté**. With an infinitive: **Je vais en acheter**. Negative quantity: **Je n'en ai pas**, **Je n'en veux plus**. Do not add a second **de** mechanically: *j'en ai de trois* does not mean I have three of them. The noun has been replaced, while **trois** remains as the quantity.

## Two pronouns: order is a system

Before an ordinary conjugated verb, the common order is **me/te/se/nous/vous → le/la/les → lui/leur → y → en**. Not every slot can be filled together, and not every theoretical combination is natural. The chart describes ordering when compatible pronouns co-occur. **Il me le donne** means “He gives it to me”; **Je le lui donne** means “I give it to him/her.” Me belongs in the first group, whereas lui comes after le.

Work from the full sentence: **Je donne le formulaire à la conseillère**. Replace **le formulaire** with **le**. Replace **à la conseillère** with **lui**. The chart orders le before lui: **Je le lui donne**. In the past: **Je le lui ai donné**. In the negative: **Je ne le lui ai pas donné**. The pronouns stay together before the auxiliary; negation frames the conjugated element in its ordinary position.

With an infinitive: **Je vais le lui donner**. Both pronouns complete donner, so they sit before that infinitive. **Je veux vous en parler** means “I want to speak to you about it”: vous represents the person addressed and en the topic introduced by de. In a location-and-quantity phrase, **Il y en a trois** means “There are three of them.” This common expression combines y and en without requiring you to pronounce a separate word for each English component.

Affirmative imperative order differs: **Donnez-le-moi**, **Donnez-le-lui**, **Parlez-m'en**, **Allez-y**. The common order after an affirmative imperative begins with **le/la/les**, followed by **moi/toi/lui/nous/vous/leur**, then y and en; me/te forms occur before en/y as in **Donne-m'en**. In a negative imperative, return to the ordinary pre-verbal order: **Ne me le donnez pas**, **Ne lui en parlez pas**. Practice a few useful contrasts rather than trying to say a maximum-length pronoun chain.

## Past participle agreement: ask about grammatical roles

With **être**, ordinary intransitive participles agree with the subject: **Elles sont arrivées**. With **avoir**, a preceding direct object generally triggers agreement: **J'ai trouvé les clés → Les clés que j'ai trouvées → Je les ai trouvées**. In the original sentence, the direct object follows and no agreement appears on trouvé. In the relative and pronoun versions, it precedes, so feminine plural **trouvées** is written.

Now compare **J'ai parlé à mes voisines → Je leur ai parlé**. **Leur** is indirect, so it does not trigger direct-object agreement. The fact that it refers to women is irrelevant to this particular rule. The useful procedure is: identify the auxiliary; find the direct object if there is one; determine whether it precedes the participle; then apply the relevant rule. Advanced exceptions and infinitive constructions will require further refinement later.

Pronominal verbs need role analysis too. **Elles se sont lavées**: the women washed themselves, and se is a preceding direct object. **Elles se sont lavé les mains**: les mains is the following direct object, and se is indirect. **Elles se sont parlé**: parler à makes se indirect, so parlé does not agree. Some essentially pronominal verbs have their own agreement treatment; do not pretend one shortcut handles every case. These common contrasts establish the reasoning you need for a reference check.

In conversation, many endings are inaudible. This does not make agreement practice pointless; it means that dictation and writing require an analysis beyond raw sound. When a correction concerns only an inaudible ending, acknowledge that the oral message may have been intelligible while the written form needs work. Separating communication success from orthographic accuracy produces more useful feedback than calling the whole answer wrong.

## Relative clauses: identifying without starting over

A relative clause adds information about a noun. **J'ai un voisin. Ce voisin travaille à la mairie. → J'ai un voisin qui travaille à la mairie.** **Qui** is the subject of travaille inside the relative clause. It can refer to people or things: **un bus qui passe souvent**. Do not choose qui merely because the noun is a person; choose it because the relative element performs the subject's job.

For a direct object, use **que**: **J'ai rempli le formulaire. Vous avez envoyé ce formulaire. → J'ai rempli le formulaire que vous avez envoyé.** In the relative clause, vous is the subject and the form is what you sent. Que elides before a vowel: **le document qu'elle demande**. Because this direct object precedes an avoir participle, agreement may matter: **les lettres que j'ai reçues**.

**Où** refers to a place or time: **la ville où j'habite**, **le jour où je suis arrivé**. It is not the unaccented conjunction **ou**, “or.” **Dont** replaces a relationship involving de: **Je parle de ce dossier → le dossier dont je parle**; **Elle a besoin de cette attestation → l'attestation dont elle a besoin**. It can also express possession: **une personne dont le frère travaille ici**. Do not add a redundant de after dont.

Compare **la personne que je connais**, **la personne qui me connaît**, and **la personne dont je parle**. The same noun appears, but its job inside each relative differs: direct object, subject, or de-complement. Translate the underlying short sentences first if uncertain. This reconstruction method is slower initially but becomes much more reliable than choosing the relative from an English who/that translation.

## Comparing people, choices, and experiences

For adjectives and adverbs, use **plus…que**, **moins…que**, and **aussi…que**: **Ce trajet est plus long que l'autre**, **Elle parle moins vite que son frère**, **Cette chambre est aussi calme que la première**. For quantities, use **plus de…que**, **moins de…que**, and **autant de…que**: **J'ai autant de travail que toi**. The noun comparison needs de; the adjective comparison does not.

Distinguish **meilleur** and **mieux**. **Bon** is an adjective, and its comparative is **meilleur/meilleure/meilleurs/meilleures**: **Cette solution est meilleure**. **Bien** is commonly an adverb, and its comparative is **mieux**: **Elle comprend mieux**. **Ce restaurant est meilleur** judges the restaurant or its quality; **On mange mieux dans ce restaurant** judges how well one eats. Their English translations can both involve better, so identify the grammatical job.

The superlative uses a definite article: **le trajet le plus rapide**, **la meilleure solution**, **les chambres les moins chères**. Agreement remains necessary. To compare past and present, combine the system with tense: **Avant, le trajet était plus long; maintenant, le métro est plus rapide**. Avoid broad cultural claims based on one experience. **J'ai trouvé ce service plus pratique** makes the personal basis clearer than presenting an unsupported universal ranking.

**De plus en plus** and **de moins en moins** describe change: **Il y a de plus en plus de monde**, **Je fais de moins en moins d'erreurs**. **Plus…plus…** expresses linked change: **Plus je pratique, plus je comprends**. At this stage, use it with familiar present forms and a concrete relationship. You do not need to turn every comparison into an abstract argument; clear everyday judgments are the foundation for later nuanced evaluation.

## A document problem: reading and repairing an account

**Quand je suis arrivé à la mairie, il y avait déjà beaucoup de monde. J'avais pris rendez-vous une semaine plus tôt, mais je ne trouvais pas le message de confirmation. La conseillère m'a demandé le document que j'avais reçu par courrier. Je lui ai expliqué que je l'avais laissé à la maison. Elle était patiente et elle m'a indiqué une autre solution : je pouvais envoyer une copie numérique. J'en avais une dans mon téléphone. Je la lui ai envoyée, puis elle a vérifié mon dossier. Le rendez-vous a duré vingt minutes. En sortant, j'ai noté les pièces dont j'aurais besoin la prochaine fois.**

The account opens a scene with **il y avait**, then moves to the earlier booking with **j'avais pris**. **Je ne trouvais pas** describes a difficulty in progress. The request **m'a demandé** advances the events. **Le document que j'avais reçu** identifies the document and places its receipt earlier. **Je la lui ai envoyée** refers to feminine **une copie**, hence envoyée with a preceding direct object. **Dont j'aurais besoin** contains a conditional form that you can recognize as “would need”; the systematic conditional treatment follows later.

Retell the same problem without exposing personal data. Use a fictional appointment and a generic document. Administrative practice should teach the structure of a request rather than encourage publishing identity numbers or private records. A real message needs the recipient, purpose, relevant date, requested action, and a polite close. **Je vous écris au sujet de mon rendez-vous du 12 mai. Pourriez-vous me confirmer les documents nécessaires ?** is a useful model, with the conditional request understood as a polite chunk until its full treatment.

Now rewrite the account as a short practical email: **Bonjour, lors de mon rendez-vous de ce matin, vous m'avez demandé une copie du document. Je vous l'envoie en pièce jointe. Pourriez-vous me confirmer sa réception ? Merci pour votre aide. Cordialement, …** Do not include the entire narrative if the recipient needs only the attachment. The difference between a story and an administrative message is its communicative purpose, even when both use the same grammatical resources.

## Translation workshop and answer reasoning

Translate: 1. I was cooking when you called. 2. When I arrived, they had already left. 3. I gave the keys to my sister; I gave them to her yesterday. 4. Do you have three tickets? Yes, I have three. 5. The document that you sent is useful. 6. This is the town where I grew up. 7. The file I am talking about is on the table. 8. This solution is better, but it costs more. 9. We used to go there every summer. 10. Do not give it to me now.

Models: **1. Je préparais le dîner quand tu as appelé.** The preparation is the ongoing background. **2. Quand je suis arrivé/arrivée, ils étaient déjà partis** or **elles étaient déjà parties** according to the people. Both arrival and departure need appropriate être agreement. **3. J'ai donné les clés à ma sœur ; je les lui ai données hier.** Les precedes lui; données agrees with the preceding feminine plural object les clés.

**4. Tu as trois billets ? — Oui, j'en ai trois.** Retain the number after replacing the noun with en. **5. Le document que vous avez envoyé est utile.** Que is the direct object within the relative; document is masculine singular. **6. C'est la ville où j'ai grandi.** Où expresses the location. **7. Le dossier dont je parle est sur la table.** Parler de supplies the de-relationship. **8. Cette solution est meilleure, mais elle coûte plus cher.** Meilleure modifies the feminine noun solution; cher functions adverbially with coûter here.

**9. Nous y allions tous les étés.** A repeated past routine uses imparfait, and y replaces the destination. **10. Ne me le donne pas maintenant** with tu, or **Ne me le donnez pas maintenant** with vous. The negative command uses ordinary pre-verbal order. Compare affirmative **Donne-le-moi**, where order and forms change. If you can explain this contrast rather than merely recognize two strings, you have acquired a transferable rule.

Reverse translation: **J'avais oublié mon billet. Je lui en ai parlé. Les lettres que j'ai écrites sont sur le bureau. Il travaillait quand nous sommes entrés. Cette chambre est moins chère que l'autre.** Models: “I had forgotten my ticket.” “I spoke to him/her about it.” “The letters I wrote/have written are on the desk.” “He was working when we came in.” “This room is cheaper than the other.” Use context to choose a natural English tense without erasing the French event relationship.

## Review standards and the path forward

Use four 0–4 feedback scales: **temporal organization**, **reference clarity**, **grammatical control**, and **task completion**. A score of 3 means the listener can follow the story despite occasional repair; a score of 4 means the message is consistently clear within the practiced topic. In temporal organization, look for intelligible relationships rather than the largest number of tenses. In reference clarity, check whether every il, elle, le, lui, y, and en has a recoverable antecedent.

In grammatical control, separate an error that reverses chronology from a silent agreement error. Both deserve correction, but they have different communicative consequences. In task completion, judge whether the request or practical outcome is clear. A grammatically impressive email that never states what the recipient should do remains incomplete. This perspective prepares you for B1 tasks, where connected independent communication matters more than isolated sentence production.

Keep a correction log with a new example in a different context; that final transfer step tests whether the explanation has become usable.

## 1. Moving the reference point into the future

Consider three statements about a training course: *Je commence lundi* — “I start on Monday”; *Je vais commencer lundi* — “I am going to start on Monday”; and *Je commencerai lundi* — “I will start on Monday.” All can concern the future. French does not force every future event into the futur simple. The present often presents a scheduled arrangement, *aller* plus an infinitive often connects a future action to a current intention or visible situation, and the futur simple places the event in a future frame. Context matters more than a mechanical rule about the number of days remaining.

Build the regular futur simple from the infinitive and the endings **-ai, -as, -a, -ons, -ez, -ont**. With an infinitive ending in *-re*, remove the final *e*: *prendre → je prendrai*. Thus *nous choisirons une date* means “we will choose a date,” while *vous attendrez notre réponse* means “you will wait for our answer.” The ending identifies the subject, but French still normally requires the subject pronoun. Do not write an isolated *choisirons* as a complete ordinary sentence.

Several common verbs use special future stems. Learn them in useful pairs: *être → je serai disponible*; *avoir → j'aurai le temps*; *aller → j'irai au bureau*; *faire → je ferai le nécessaire*; *venir → je viendrai demain*; *pouvoir → je pourrai participer*; *vouloir → je voudrai probablement changer*; *devoir → je devrai partir*; *savoir → je saurai vendredi*; *voir → je verrai mon responsable*; *recevoir → je recevrai une confirmation*. The endings remain the same. *Il faudra réserver* uses the impersonal verb *falloir*: “it will be necessary to book.”

Work through “We will be able to send it to her tomorrow.” First identify the future ability: *nous pourrons*. The action that depends on ability remains infinitive: *envoyer*. The thing is a direct object, *le*, and the recipient is an indirect object, *lui*. Both belong to *envoyer*, so place them before that infinitive: **Nous pourrons le lui envoyer demain.** The future ending goes on *pouvoir*, not on every verb in the sentence. This construction lets you reuse the pronoun system from the preceding chapter in a longer message.

French uses the future after temporal conjunctions where English often uses the present: *Quand tu arriveras, je t'appellerai* — “When you arrive, I will call you.” The arrival is future in French even though English says “arrive.” Compare this with *si*, which behaves differently in a real condition. Keep the conjunction and meaning together when you learn the pattern rather than reducing everything to “after a connecting word.”

Forecasts can include honest uncertainty: *Je pense que le projet coûtera moins cher* — “I think the project will cost less”; *Il y aura peut-être un retard* — “There may be a delay.” *Peut-être* is an adverb, so it does not conjugate. Make a three-column plan of scheduled actions, intentions, and predictions. Say each item naturally before deciding which future expression fits it. Several answers may be defensible if the framing changes.

## 2. Open conditions: what happens if something happens?

An open condition treats its condition as a live possibility rather than an imaginary distance from reality. The central pattern is **si + present, future**: *Si tu confirmes aujourd'hui, nous réserverons la salle* — “If you confirm today, we will book the room.” The confirmation has not necessarily happened. The present in the *si* clause does not make it a present event; the construction gives it a possible future interpretation.

Take “If the train is late, I will let you know.” Start with the condition: *si le train est en retard*. Use present *est*, not future *sera*. Then build the consequence: *je te préviendrai*. The complete result is **Si le train est en retard, je te préviendrai.** A comma commonly separates a condition placed first. If the consequence comes first, the meaning remains: *Je te préviendrai si le train est en retard.*

The consequence need not be future. A general relationship can use two presents: *Si je dors mal, je suis moins patient* — “If I sleep badly, I am less patient.” A practical instruction can use the imperative: *Si tu as un problème, appelle-moi* — “If you have a problem, call me.” A modal present can express an available response: *Si vous êtes fatigué, vous pouvez faire une pause* — “If you are tired, you can take a break.” Identify whether the speaker states a habit, predicts a result, or issues an instruction.

Do not learn “never put the future after *si*” without its scope. It applies to this conditional use. In an indirect yes–no question, *si* means “whether”: *Je ne sais pas s'il viendra* — “I do not know whether he will come.” Here the future is grammatical because the clause reports a question, not an if–then condition. Diagnose the meaning by expanding it: does the sentence ask about an unknown answer, or does one event depend on another?

Conditions are useful for collaborative work because they allocate responsibility. Compare *Tu dois finir demain* — “You must finish tomorrow” with *Si tu termines la première partie demain, je pourrai relire le reste vendredi* — “If you finish the first part tomorrow, I will be able to review the rest on Friday.” The second sentence gives a dependency and a reason. It can still sound demanding, so tone, relationship, and context remain important.

Practice with an appointment. Write a confirmation, a contingency if the other person is late, and a contingency if you are late. Include *sinon* — “otherwise”: *Confirmez avant midi; sinon, nous proposerons le créneau à quelqu'un d'autre.* Notice that *avant midi* refers to a deadline, while *avant que* introduces a clause and will require a separate grammar treatment in the next chapter. Clear time expressions often prevent misunderstandings more effectively than elaborate verb forms.

## 3. Imagining a different present or future

Now compare an open offer with a remote hypothesis. *Si j'ai le temps, je viendrai* — “If I have time, I will come” leaves the possibility open. *Si j'avais le temps, je viendrais* — “If I had time, I would come” presents distance from the present situation. The speaker may lack time now or simply be exploring a less immediate possibility. The grammar signals a viewpoint; it does not mathematically measure probability.

The usual pattern is **si + imparfait, conditionnel présent**. You already know the imparfait endings. The conditional uses the **future stem plus imparfait endings**: *je travaillerais, tu travaillerais, il travaillerait, nous travaillerions, vous travailleriez, elles travailleraient*. The shared stem explains useful pairs: *je serai / je serais*, “I will be / I would be”; *nous pourrons / nous pourrions*, “we will be able to / we could.” In some accents *-ai* and *-ais* are audibly distinct and in others less distinct; syntax and context still carry the difference.

Construct “If I worked closer to home, I would spend less time travelling.” The hypothesis describes a different employment situation: *Si je travaillais plus près de chez moi*. The result is hypothetical: *je passerais moins de temps dans les transports*. Put them together: **Si je travaillais plus près de chez moi, je passerais moins de temps dans les transports.** *Chez moi* means at or around my home; *ma maison* would specifically name a house and might exclude someone who lives in a flat.

The common error *si j'aurais* comes from copying English “if I would.” For ordinary hypothetical conditions, select *si j'avais*. Then reserve *j'aurais* for the consequence: *Si j'avais une voiture, j'aurais davantage de liberté.* Both verbs come from *avoir*, but their jobs differ. Saying the clause roles aloud—condition, consequence—can be more helpful than copying a conjugation table twenty times.

The conditional also supports comparison without a *si* clause: *Le poste à Lyon serait plus intéressant, mais le logement coûterait plus cher* — “The job in Lyon would be more interesting, but housing would cost more.” The implicit condition is accepting that job. Make the condition explicit if your listener might misunderstand: *Si j'acceptais ce poste…* Give both an advantage and a limitation. B1 independence includes sustaining a simple explanation, not merely choosing an option.

In a discussion, ask *Qu'est-ce que tu ferais à ma place ?* — “What would you do in my position?” A useful answer can acknowledge missing information: *À ta place, je demanderais des précisions sur les horaires avant de décider.* Here *demander des précisions* means ask for clarification, and *sur* introduces the subject of those details. The construction lets you offer advice while making clear that the final decision belongs to the other person.

## 4. Tact, requests, and degrees of obligation

French politeness is not a single word added to a direct instruction. Verb choice, pronouns, intonation, explanation, and the listener's situation all contribute. *Donne-moi le dossier* can be perfectly normal between close colleagues, while *Pourriez-vous m'envoyer le dossier, s'il vous plaît ?* suits many first contacts. The conditional *pourriez* makes the request less direct. It does not literally express uncertainty about whether the recipient has the physical ability.

Useful request frames include *Je voudrais prendre rendez-vous* — “I would like to make an appointment”; *J'aimerais vous poser une question* — “I would like to ask you a question”; and *Serait-il possible de reporter la réunion ?* — “Would it be possible to postpone the meeting?” The last frame uses an impersonal construction, so the infinitive follows *de*. If you include a different subject inside a full clause, the grammar changes; the next chapter introduces *serait-il possible que…* with the subjunctive.

Advice ranges from a possibility to a strong recommendation: *Tu pourrais faire une pause* — “You could take a break”; *Tu devrais en parler à ton responsable* — “You should discuss it with your manager”; *Il faudrait vérifier le contrat* — “The contract should be checked / it would be necessary to check the contract.” Compare present *tu dois* and conditional *tu devrais*. The former usually states an obligation or a strong inference; the latter often offers advice. Neither replaces a professional judgment in health or legal matters.

Use a three-step refusal when a relationship matters: acknowledge the request, state a concrete limitation, and offer a workable alternative. *Merci d'avoir pensé à moi. Je ne pourrai pas terminer ce travail pour jeudi, car je serai en déplacement. En revanche, je pourrais vous envoyer une première version vendredi matin.* Translation: “Thank you for thinking of me. I will not be able to finish this work by Thursday because I will be away. However, I could send you a first version on Friday morning.” *Pour jeudi* gives a completion target; *pendant jeudi* would not express that deadline.

Be careful with dictionary equivalence. English “I want you to send it” cannot ordinarily become *je veux vous envoyer*; that means “I want to send you.” At this stage say *Pourriez-vous me l'envoyer ?* or *Je voudrais recevoir le document*. These are complete, accurate ways to communicate the goal while the next chapter teaches the construction with two different subjects. Choosing an available structure is a legitimate communication strategy, not a failure.

For speaking practice, perform the same request to a friend, a colleague, and a public office. Change *tu* to *vous*, but also adjust how much context you give. Record a version that is too abrupt, then repair it. Evaluate whether the repaired message names the action, deadline, reason, and next step. Politeness that hides the actual request can be as ineffective as a blunt demand.

## 5. Past alternatives, regret, and mixed time

Past decisions create a different problem: the event cannot now be changed. The standard counterfactual pattern is **si + plus-que-parfait, conditionnel passé**. *Si j'avais réservé plus tôt, j'aurais payé moins cher* — “If I had booked earlier, I would have paid less.” The first clause supplies the unrealized earlier action. The second imagines its unrealized consequence. This is not the same timeline as *si je réservais, je paierais*, which explores a present or future possibility.

Form the conditional past with **avoir or être in the conditional + past participle**. *J'aurais accepté* means “I would have accepted”; *elle serait partie* means “she would have left.” The choice of auxiliary follows the compound-tense rules already learned. With ordinary *être* verbs, the participle agrees with the subject: *elles seraient arrivées*. Pronominal verbs need more careful object analysis; chapter seven treats their difficult agreements in detail.

Build “If she had received the message, she would have come.” The condition uses *recevoir* in the plus-que-parfait: *Si elle avait reçu le message*. The consequence uses *venir*, which takes *être*: *elle serait venue*. The feminine *-e* matches *elle*. Thus **Si elle avait reçu le message, elle serait venue.** Do not add a conditional to the *si* clause merely because the whole scenario is imaginary.

Regret does not require a condition: *J'aurais dû vérifier l'adresse* — “I should have checked the address”; *Nous aurions pu demander de l'aide* — “We could have asked for help”; *J'aurais aimé rester plus longtemps* — “I would have liked to stay longer.” *J'ai dû partir* means “I had to leave,” while *j'aurais dû partir* means “I should have left.” The conditional auxiliary changes the interpretation substantially. Keep these expressions in contrastive pairs.

Time relationships can cross the pattern's two neat boxes. *Si j'avais accepté ce poste, j'habiterais maintenant à Toulouse* — “If I had accepted that job, I would now live in Toulouse.” The condition is a past choice, but the imagined consequence is a present situation, so the consequence uses the conditional present. Conversely, a present characteristic can explain a past alternative: *Si j'étais moins timide, j'aurais posé une question hier.* Understand the intended times before selecting forms.

The OQLF's discussion of the conditional past confirms the standard past-counterfactual pairing and notes literary alternatives. For this course, produce the ordinary modern pattern; recognize literary alternatives later when reading. See [Conditionnel passé et plus-que-parfait du subjonctif](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/24141/la-grammaire/le-verbe/temps-grammaticaux/conditionnel/conditionnel-passe-et-plus-que-parfait-du-subjonctif), inspected 13 November 2026. Your goal here is to explain a decision without confusing the real event with the imagined outcome.

## 6. Having something done and letting someone act

*Je répare mon vélo* says that I repair my bicycle. *Je fais réparer mon vélo* says that I have it repaired: I arrange the action, and someone else performs it. The construction is **faire + infinitive**. The infinitive names the action; *faire* carries person and tense: *je fais réparer*, *j'ai fait réparer*, *je ferai réparer*. The past participle *fait* stays invariable immediately before an infinitive: *les fenêtres que j'ai fait remplacer*.

Begin with a concrete repair: *Le mécanicien vérifie les freins.* If the owner arranges this, say *Je fais vérifier les freins par le mécanicien.* The brakes undergo checking, the mechanic performs it, and *je* arranges it. The agent can be omitted when irrelevant: *Je dois faire vérifier les freins*. This avoids the mistaken implication that you personally have mechanical expertise.

When a causative has a person who performs an intransitive action, a direct complement is possible: *Je fais entrer les visiteurs* — “I have the visitors come in / I show the visitors in.” If the infinitive already has a direct object, a performer may appear with *à* or *par*: *Je fais lire le message à mon collègue* or *par mon collègue*, depending on the intended construction and emphasis. At B1, an explicit *par* phrase often makes agency easier to follow. Pronoun-heavy causatives deserve additional study rather than being forced into every message.

*Laisser + infinitive* expresses allowing or not preventing: *Je laisse ma fille choisir son activité* — “I let my daughter choose her activity.” *Faire* and *laisser* therefore express different control: *Je le fais attendre* — “I make him wait”; *Je le laisse attendre* — “I leave him waiting / let him wait.” A small verb change can alter how a relationship sounds. Translate the situation, not just the two English words “have” or “let.”

Useful service expressions include *faire couper ses cheveux* — have one's hair cut; *faire traduire un document* — have a document translated; and *faire livrer un colis* — arrange for a parcel to be delivered. French often uses a definite article with body parts when possession is clear: *Je me fais couper les cheveux*. Learn this complete chunk. In *je me fais…*, *me* identifies the person affected or benefiting; it does not mean that the customer performs the haircut.

Role-play a repair call. Name the object, describe the symptom in ordinary language, ask about the estimate, and confirm the time. For example: *Mon ordinateur s'éteint tout seul. Je voudrais le faire examiner. Pourriez-vous me donner une estimation avant de changer une pièce ?* Avoid inventing technical vocabulary you do not understand; a clear symptom and sequence often communicate more effectively. Ask the listener to repeat the price, then summarize it yourself.

## 7. Reporting speech without losing the people

Direct speech preserves a speaker's words: *Léa dit : « Je suis disponible demain. »* Indirect speech embeds the information: *Léa dit qu'elle est disponible demain.* The pronoun changes because *je* referred to Léa inside her speech. Now the narrator refers to her as *elle*. *Que* introduces a reported statement and becomes *qu'* before a vowel sound. It is not translated as an object “which” here.

Start with present reporting verbs to isolate the changes: *Il explique qu'il travaille à distance* — “He explains that he works remotely”; *Elle affirme qu'elle a envoyé le dossier* — “She states that she sent the file.” The embedded tense expresses the actual relationship in time. A present reporting verb does not automatically require a tense change. Keep past events past and future events future if that is what the speaker means.

To report a yes–no question, use **demander si**: *Elle demande : « Est-ce que vous êtes libre ? » → Elle demande si nous sommes libres.* Do not keep *est-ce que* inside the indirect question. For a question word, keep the relevant word and use statement order: *Où habitez-vous ? → Il demande où nous habitons.* *Qu'est-ce que vous voulez ?* becomes *Il demande ce que nous voulons*. A question about the subject uses *ce qui*: *Qu'est-ce qui se passe ? → Il demande ce qui se passe.*

Reported requests commonly use **demander à quelqu'un de + infinitive**: *Il me demande de rappeler demain* — “He asks me to call back tomorrow.” The *me* is the person being asked, and the understood subject of *rappeler* is that same person. In a negative request, place *ne pas* together before the infinitive: *Elle nous demande de ne pas arriver en retard.* This is a productive alternative to more elaborate subordinate clauses.

With a past reporting verb, French often shifts the reference frame: *Elle a dit qu'elle était disponible* — “She said she was available”; *Elle a dit qu'elle viendrait* — “She said she would come.” Here the conditional can be a **future relative to a past viewpoint**, not an imaginary alternative. Chapter seven develops the full tense relationships. For now, distinguish the two uses by the reporting verb and timeline.

Time words may need adjustment. On Monday Léa says *Je viendrai demain*. When reporting on Friday, *Léa a dit qu'elle viendrait demain* would normally point to Saturday from the narrator's present, so clarify *le lendemain*, or use the actual day: *Léa a dit lundi qu'elle viendrait mardi*. Names and calendar dates are often safer than complicated transformations. Always preserve the real appointment rather than treating reported speech as a purely formal exercise.

## 8. Pronouns that keep a conversation coherent

Longer discussions repeat information, but repeating every noun can sound unnatural. Suppose a colleague asks, *Tu as parlé de ce problème à Nadia ?* A useful answer is *Oui, je lui en ai parlé.* *Lui* replaces *à Nadia* and *en* replaces *de ce problème*. Both appear before the compound auxiliary *ai*. The phrase does not require a new verb rule; it combines the verb's complement pattern with the established pronoun order.

For ordinary declarative clauses, keep the sequence **me/te/se/nous/vous → le/la/les → lui/leur → y → en**. This is a placement guide, not a command to use every position. *Je le lui donne* replaces a thing and a recipient. *Je vous en parlerai* replaces the listener and a topic. *Il y en a trois* means “there are three of them,” combining the expression *il y a* with quantity *en*. The actual combination must fit a meaningful underlying sentence.

Pronouns normally attach to the verb whose complements they replace. Compare *Je lui promets de venir* — “I promise him/her that I will come” with *Je promets de lui téléphoner* — “I promise to call him.” In the first, the recipient belongs to *promettre*; in the second, *lui* belongs to *téléphoner*. Ask yourself which action involves that person. Placement can change meaning, so avoid moving pronouns merely to make the first half of the sentence shorter.

For affirmative imperatives, the order changes and hyphens connect the pronouns: *Donnez-le-moi* — “Give it to me”; *Parlez-lui-en* — “Tell him/her about it.” In negative commands, ordinary preverbal placement returns: *Ne me le donnez pas*; *Ne lui en parlez pas*. If this becomes difficult during spontaneous speech, use a full noun or a shorter request first, then practice the compact version slowly.

Not every *à + person* becomes *lui*. *Penser à quelqu'un* normally uses a stressed pronoun for the person: *Je pense à elle*. *S'intéresser à quelque chose* can use *y*: *Je m'y intéresse*. For *de + person*, a stressed form can be useful when identifying an individual: *Je parle de lui*. Learn a verb together with its complement pattern and several real examples. English prepositions cannot reliably predict French pronouns.

Record a short mediation dialogue with three named people and two objects. Then replace only the nouns whose referents are clear. Listen without the written script: can you identify who *il*, *elle*, and *lui* refer to? If not, restore a name. Good pronoun use balances economy and clarity. Ambiguous perfection is less useful than a slightly repetitive message that everyone understands.

## 9. Reading a decision fairly

Read the following original text once for the decision and a second time for its reasons. Underline future commitments, conditional suggestions, and one unrealized past alternative. Do not translate every word before finding the central situation.

> **Un changement possible**
>
> Depuis plusieurs mois, Inès travaille dans une petite association. Elle aime son équipe, mais elle passe presque deux heures par jour dans les transports. Un organisme situé près de chez elle lui a proposé un entretien. Le poste serait mieux payé, mais le contrat ne durerait qu'un an.
>
> « Si j'accepte, je pourrai rentrer plus tôt et je serai moins fatiguée, explique-t-elle à son frère. En revanche, je perdrai peut-être la stabilité que j'ai aujourd'hui. » Son frère lui conseille de demander des précisions avant de prendre une décision. Il lui dit aussi qu'il ne choisirait pas uniquement en fonction du salaire.
>
> Inès se souvient de son premier emploi. Elle avait accepté très vite, sans poser assez de questions. Si elle avait mieux compris les horaires, elle aurait négocié une organisation différente. Cette fois, elle préparera une liste : les missions, l'accompagnement, les possibilités de formation et le renouvellement du contrat.
>
> Le lendemain, elle téléphone à l'organisme. « Pourriez-vous m'envoyer une description détaillée du poste ? J'aimerais également savoir si les horaires sont fixes. » La responsable lui répond qu'elle lui enverra le document dans la journée. Inès ne promet rien encore. Elle veut disposer d'informations suffisantes pour comparer les deux situations.

**Full translation.** For several months, Inès has worked in a small nonprofit organization. She likes her team, but she spends nearly two hours a day travelling. An organization near her home has offered her an interview. The job would be better paid, but the contract would last only one year. “If I accept, I will be able to get home earlier and I will be less tired,” she explains to her brother. “On the other hand, I may lose the stability I have today.” Her brother advises her to request details before making a decision. He also tells her that he would not choose solely on the basis of salary. Inès remembers her first job. She had accepted very quickly without asking enough questions. If she had understood the hours better, she would have negotiated a different arrangement. This time she will prepare a list: duties, support, training opportunities, and contract renewal. The next day she calls the organization. “Could you send me a detailed job description? I would also like to know whether the hours are fixed.” The manager replies that she will send her the document during the day. Inès does not promise anything yet. She wants sufficient information to compare the two situations.

Notice the different statuses of information. The interview offer is presented as an event, the benefits of accepting are conditional on a choice, and contract length is described from the viewpoint of a possible new job. *Ne…que* means “only,” not “not”; chapter seven revisits that restriction. *Depuis plusieurs mois* with the present places the employment across the interval from the past into now. Translating it with an English present perfect does not require a French compound past.

Answer in French: What does Inès value in her current job? Which risk concerns her? What has she learned from the earlier job? Why is the indirect question *si les horaires sont fixes* different from the conditional *si j'accepte*? A sound answer uses the text without inventing motives. For example: *Elle apprécie son équipe et la stabilité de son emploi. Elle craint un contrat court. Elle a appris à demander des renseignements avant de décider.*

## 10. Translation workshop with reasoning

Translate the English items into French, then translate your French back into English without looking. Several natural versions are possible. The models show one coherent choice, not the only correct sentence.

1. If you send me the estimate today, I will answer tomorrow.
2. If I had more experience, I would apply for this job.
3. You should discuss it with her before signing.
4. We could postpone the meeting until Friday.
5. If they had warned us, we would have arrived earlier.
6. I should have asked for a written confirmation.
7. She is having her bicycle repaired.
8. He lets his children choose their clothes.
9. The receptionist asks whether we have an appointment.
10. She asks us not to forget the documents.
11. I will be able to send it to you after the meeting.
12. If I had taken that course, I would understand this software better now.

**Explained answers.** 1. *Si vous m'envoyez le devis aujourd'hui, je vous répondrai demain.* The open condition takes the present; the promised reply takes the future. 2. *Si j'avais plus d'expérience, je poserais ma candidature à ce poste.* Hypothetical present: imparfait plus conditional. 3. *Tu devrais en parler avec elle avant de signer.* *En* replaces the topic, and *avec elle* makes the person explicit; *lui en parler* is also possible. 4. *Nous pourrions reporter la réunion à vendredi.* The conditional suggests an option. 5. *S'ils nous avaient prévenus, nous serions arrivés plus tôt.* The imagined arrival uses *être*; adjust agreement for the group's composition. 6. *J'aurais dû demander une confirmation écrite.* The conditional past of *devoir* conveys a missed desirable action.

7. *Elle fait réparer son vélo.* She arranges the repair. 8. *Il laisse ses enfants choisir leurs vêtements.* The children perform *choisir*. 9. *La personne à l'accueil demande si nous avons rendez-vous.* Reported yes–no question, so no *est-ce que*. 10. *Elle nous demande de ne pas oublier les documents.* Negative infinitive *ne pas oublier*. 11. *Je pourrai vous l'envoyer après la réunion.* Here *vous* precedes *le*, which elides to *l'* before *envoyer*. 12. *Si j'avais suivi cette formation, je comprendrais mieux ce logiciel maintenant.* A past condition has a present consequence; *suivre une formation* is the usual collocation.

Now translate these into English: *Je lui ai dit que je viendrais si j'avais le temps.* *Vous pourriez le faire livrer chez vous.* *Si elle avait accepté, elle serait partie en septembre.* *Je ne sais pas s'il sera disponible.* Respect uncertainty in the first item; do not convert every conditional into a definite promise. The models are “I told him/her that I would come if I had time”; “You could have it delivered to your home”; “If she had accepted, she would have left in September”; and “I do not know whether he will be available.” The last *si* means whether, which permits the future.

## Listening, speaking, and the decision portfolio

For dictation, select ten companion sentences without displaying their French text. Listen once at normal speed for meaning, once more while writing, and a final time to check small words. Write an English meaning separately. Compare *je serai* and *je serais* in full sentences, but do not assume every voice distinguishes them equally. Mark an accent error separately from a wrong tense and a missing content word. These require different repairs.

For speaking, choose between two plausible training courses. Give a two-minute explanation with three criteria, one open condition, one hypothetical alternative, and a clear next action. Ask a partner to introduce a changed price or unavailable date. Respond to that new information rather than reciting the prepared monologue again. If working alone, draw a new constraint from a list after recording your first version and record a genuine update.

Write a 180–220-word email requesting information before a decision. Include a polite opening, a specific request, a deadline with a reason, and an alternative if the deadline is impossible. Then write a 120-word private reflection about a past decision using one counterfactual. Read both aloud: unclear reference often becomes easier to notice in speech. Keep a correction record with the original phrase, the repair, and a new example of the same pattern.

Use four dimensions, each scored from zero to four: **task completion** (are the requested facts and next action present?); **coherence** (can the listener follow conditions and time?); **language control** (do verb forms and pronouns preserve meaning?); **interaction** (can you clarify, react, and repair?). ## 1. Why an attitude can change a verb

Compare *Je sais qu'elle vient* — “I know that she is coming” with *Je veux qu'elle vienne* — “I want her to come.” In the first, the speaker presents her coming as known information. In the second, the speaker presents it as desired. The change from *vient* to *vienne* is not simply a change from reality to unreality. She may in fact be coming in both situations. The grammatical frame tells us how the speaker uses the event.

**Indicative** and **subjunctive** are names for verb moods. A mood is a family of forms associated with how a clause is presented. The indicative includes familiar forms such as the present and compound past. The subjunctive has its own present and past forms in ordinary modern usage. Do not confuse mood with time: a present subjunctive can refer to a future event, as in *Je veux qu'elle vienne demain* — “I want her to come tomorrow.”

The most useful first pattern is a main clause expressing an attitude plus **que + subject + subjunctive**: *Il faut que tu partes* — “You need to leave”; *Je suis contente que tu sois là* — “I am happy that you are here.” In the second example, the person's presence is real and known, yet the emotion frame takes the subjunctive. This is why “subjunctive means something unreal” is an unreliable general rule.

The two-clause construction becomes especially useful when the subjects differ. *Je veux partir* means “I want to leave”: the same person wants and leaves, so the infinitive is natural. *Je veux que tu partes* means “I want you to leave”: the subject of *vouloir* is *je* and the subject of *partir* is *tu*. English can use “you to leave,” but ordinary French requires a finite subordinate clause here. *Je veux tu partir* and *je veux que tu partir* do not build that relationship correctly.

Work from meaning to structure. To say “It is important that the students understand the instructions,” start with the evaluation *Il est important*. Add *que* and the new subject *les étudiants*. Then choose the subjunctive of *comprendre*: **Il est important que les étudiants comprennent les consignes.** The written form *comprennent* happens to resemble the indicative present for this subject. A grammatical contrast can be present even when the spelling is identical.

In reading, circle the expression that introduces the subordinate clause before labeling the verb. The trigger may be a wish, emotion, evaluation, conjunction, or a viewpoint in a relative clause. A list of triggers is a useful memory aid, but understanding the whole construction prevents you from putting a subjunctive after every occurrence of *que*. *Je sais que*, *je pense que* in a straightforward assertion, and *il est évident que* normally introduce indicative clauses.

## 2. Building the present subjunctive

For many verbs, take the present indicative **ils/elles** stem, remove **-ent**, and add **-e, -es, -e, -ions, -iez, -ent**. From *ils parlent* obtain *que je parle, que tu parles, qu'il parle, que nous parlions, que vous parliez, qu'elles parlent*. The *que* is a convenient study cue; the actual sentence supplies its own appropriate frame. Say *Il faut que nous parlions*, not a detached table alone.

For verbs with differing stems, the *nous* and *vous* subjunctive commonly use the stem of the present indicative *nous* form. Thus *venir* gives *que je vienne*, *que tu viennes*, *qu'il vienne*, but *que nous venions*, *que vous veniez*, then *qu'ils viennent*. Similarly: *que je prenne / que nous prenions*; *que je boive / que nous buvions*. Learn the audible contrasts in a phrase rather than assuming every subjunctive is a regular *-e* ending attached to the infinitive.

Two very frequent verbs need full attention. **Être:** *que je sois, que tu sois, qu'il soit, que nous soyons, que vous soyez, qu'elles soient*. **Avoir:** *que j'aie, que tu aies, qu'il ait, que nous ayons, que vous ayez, qu'ils aient*. Many forms sound alike, so the subject and sentence frame matter. In writing, *il ait* is not *il est*, and *qu'ils soient* is not *qu'ils sont*.

Other high-value forms include *que je fasse* from *faire*, *que je puisse* from *pouvoir*, *que je sache* from *savoir*, *que je veuille* from *vouloir*, and *que j'aille* from *aller*. Their *nous* and *vous* forms include *fassions/fassiez*, *puissions/puissiez*, *sachions/sachiez*, *voulions/vouliez*, and *allions/alliez*. *Falloir* is chiefly impersonal: *qu'il faille*. Begin with the forms you need for real messages, but keep the whole paradigm available as a reference.

Negation surrounds the conjugated verb in a simple tense: *Je préfère que tu ne viennes pas trop tard* — “I prefer you not to come too late.” Object pronouns remain before it: *Il faut que tu me le rendes* — “You need to give it back to me.” In a compound form, they precede the auxiliary: *Je suis heureux que vous l'ayez reçu*. Mood changes the verb form, not the established position of the pronouns.

Practice building a sentence through four visible stages: intention, subjects, frame, form. For “We need her to be able to participate,” intention is necessity, subjects are *nous* and *elle*, a useful frame is *Il faut que*, and the required form is *puisse*: **Il faut qu'elle puisse participer.** If you need to emphasize “we need,” say *Nous avons besoin qu'elle puisse participer*. Avoid translating every English “need” with a single French construction regardless of what follows.

## 3. Wishes, requirements, and emotional reactions

Frequent desire frames are *vouloir que*, *souhaiter que*, *préférer que*, and *aimer mieux que*. *Le professeur souhaite que les étudiants participent* means “The teacher would like the students to participate.” The teacher's wish does not guarantee participation. Compare *Le professeur souhaite participer*, where the teacher is the person who wants to participate. The presence or absence of a second subject changes the clause structure.

Requirements include *il faut que*, *il est nécessaire que*, *il est important que*, and *exiger que*. They differ in social force: *Nous souhaitons que vous répondiez* expresses a wish; *Nous exigeons que vous répondiez* is a demand. Accurate grammar does not neutralize an aggressive verb. For a collaborative tone, explain the practical reason: *Il est important que chacun reçoive les documents avant la réunion pour pouvoir se préparer.*

Emotional expressions include *être content que*, *être surpris que*, *regretter que*, and *avoir peur que*. *Je suis surprise qu'il connaisse déjà ce livre* means “I am surprised that he already knows this book.” The grammatical gender of *surprise* describes the speaker; the subjunctive *connaisse* describes the embedded event. Separate the two agreement decisions. If the speaker is masculine, write *surpris*, while *connaisse* does not change because of that speaker's gender.

Take “We are delighted that you can join us.” Begin with *Nous sommes ravis*, agreeing the adjective with the speakers. Add the emotion's clause *que vous…*. The subordinate verb is *pouvoir* in the subjunctive, *puissiez*: **Nous sommes ravis que vous puissiez vous joindre à nous.** *Se joindre à* means join a group or activity. The reflexive *vous* belongs to the infinitive and remains before *joindre*.

An infinitive often makes a shared-subject sentence lighter: *Je suis contente de participer* — “I am happy to participate”; *Je regrette d'avoir refusé* — “I regret having refused.” The *de* belongs to these adjective or verb constructions. Do not insert *de* automatically before every infinitive after every verb: *je veux participer* has none. Learn a frame as a unit, including its preposition.

Some constructions describe another person's behavior through **de + infinitive** even though English prefers a clause: *Je vous remercie d'être venu* — “Thank you for coming.” Here the grammar is an established thanking pattern. This reminds us that “different subjects always require *que*” is only a guide for the wish structures under discussion, not a universal rule for all French verbs. A good language notebook stores constructions, not slogans that overreach.

In your project unit, negotiate five requirements for an exhibition, course, or community event. Distinguish a necessary condition from a preference. For each sentence, give a practical consequence if it is not met. For example, *Il faut que l'entrée soit accessible; sinon, certaines personnes ne pourront pas participer.* You are learning to connect grammar to decisions that matter rather than treating moods as a set of arbitrary replacements.

## 4. Doubt, belief, and changing a viewpoint

Straightforward affirmative belief usually takes the indicative: *Je pense qu'elle a raison* — “I think she is right”; *Je crois qu'il viendra* — “I believe he will come.” Doubt commonly takes the subjunctive: *Je doute qu'il vienne* — “I doubt he will come”; *Il est possible qu'elle ait raison* — “It is possible that she is right.” The language marks how the speaker presents the proposition, not an externally measured probability.

Negative or interrogative belief expressions allow important nuance. *Je ne pense pas qu'il soit prêt* is a normal way to express “I do not think he is ready,” with the readiness not asserted. In some contexts the indicative appears when the speaker focuses on a proposition treated as established or echoes an assertion. At this level, use the common subjunctive pattern after a straightforward negative judgment, then notice authentic variation without deciding that every exception is an error.

Questions depend on their stance. *Pensez-vous qu'il soit prêt ?* can withhold commitment and invite evaluation. *Vous pensez qu'il est prêt ?* can ask for confirmation of the other person's belief. Spoken intonation and context matter. A multiple-choice exercise must supply enough context to prefer one reading; isolated “think” questions without context can exaggerate certainty about a flexible choice.

**Espérer** is a useful contrast. In an affirmative statement it normally takes the indicative, including the future: *J'espère qu'elle réussira* — “I hope she will succeed.” Desire does not automatically mean subjunctive after every verb with a hopeful meaning. By contrast, *Je souhaite qu'elle réussisse* uses the subjunctive. Store these two sentences together so the lexical construction, not an English category alone, guides your choice.

Contrast probability frames: *Il est probable qu'il viendra* normally uses the indicative in an affirmative assertion; *Il est possible qu'il vienne* uses the subjunctive. *Il est certain qu'il viendra* asserts certainty; *Il n'est pas certain qu'il vienne* withholds it. The presence of a negative changes how the information is presented. Do not choose only by scanning for the adjective while ignoring its negation.

A relative clause can use the subjunctive when the referent is sought, hypothetical, or selected through an evaluative perspective: *Je cherche un livre qui soit accessible aux débutants* — “I am looking for a book that is accessible to beginners.” Compare *J'ai un livre qui est accessible aux débutants*, which describes an identified book. The first does not assert that a suitable book has been found. A superlative may also favor the subjunctive: *C'est le meilleur film que j'aie vu cette année*. This presents an assessment; indicative uses are possible when factual description is foregrounded.

For practice, take one claim such as *ce programme est utile* and frame it five ways: knowledge, belief, doubt, desire, and emotion. Explain the difference in English before selecting the verb. That explanation reveals whether you understand the message or are merely matching a memorized word to a mood.

## 5. Time inside the subjunctive and after conjunctions

Modern everyday French uses the present subjunctive for an event concurrent with or later than the main situation: *Je suis contente qu'elle soit ici* — “I am happy that she is here”; *Je serai contente qu'elle vienne demain* — “I will be happy for her to come tomorrow.” There is no separate ordinary future subjunctive. A future time expression can locate the event while the verb remains present subjunctive.

The **past subjunctive** expresses an event viewed as completed: subjunctive **avoir/être + past participle**. *Je suis contente qu'elle ait terminé* — “I am happy that she has finished”; *Je regrette qu'ils soient partis* — “I regret that they have left.” Use the auxiliary required by the verb and the same relevant participle agreement principles as in other compound tenses. The main point is completion relative to the viewpoint, not an absolute requirement that the calendar date be yesterday.

Compare *Il faut que tu finisses avant vendredi* with *Il faut que tu aies fini avant vendredi*. The first requires finishing before Friday; the second foregrounds the finished result by that deadline. Both can concern the future. Build the second carefully: requirement *il faut*, subordinate subject *tu*, auxiliary *aies*, participle *fini*. Do not use the indicative *as* inside this subjunctive frame.

Several conjunctions normally take the subjunctive: *pour que* and *afin que* for purpose; *avant que* for before; *jusqu'à ce que* for until; *bien que* and *quoique* for although; *à condition que* for provided that; and *sans que* for without a particular event. *Je parle lentement pour que tout le monde comprenne* means “I speak slowly so that everyone understands.” The intended understanding is a goal, not a guaranteed result.

When the subject is shared, use an appropriate infinitive structure: *Je parle lentement pour être compris* — “I speak slowly to be understood”; *Je relis avant d'envoyer le message* — “I reread before sending the message.” Different subjects require the full clause: *Je relis le message avant que mon collègue l'envoie*. *Avant que* commonly has an optional *ne* in formal writing; that *ne* is not ordinary negation. Chapter seven explains it.

**Après que** takes the indicative in the standard written norm: *Après qu'elle a présenté les résultats, nous avons discuté* — “After she presented the results, we discussed them.” For a completed future event, *Après qu'elle aura présenté les résultats, nous discuterons*. Many speakers use a subjunctive by analogy with *avant que*, so you will hear it; produce the indicative in careful writing. The OQLF's [conjunction reference](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/24238/la-grammaire/le-verbe/modes/subjonctif/subjonctif-ou-indicatif-apres-les-subordonnants-composes), inspected 13 November 2026, distinguishes these constructions.

*De sorte que* illustrates why meaning matters: *Il a parlé clairement, de sorte que nous avons compris* reports an actual consequence and uses the indicative. *Il parlera clairement de sorte que nous comprenions* presents a purpose and uses the subjunctive. This is a relationship between clauses, not a property of the three words alone. During reading, label whether the second event is reported as a result or intended as a goal.

## 6. Relative pronouns: reconstruct the missing relationship

Relative clauses let you identify or describe a noun without repeating it: *Voici l'étudiante. Elle a organisé la visite. → Voici l'étudiante qui a organisé la visite.* The relative pronoun *qui* replaces the subject inside the second clause. *Voici le livre. J'ai lu ce livre. → Voici le livre que j'ai lu.* Here *que* replaces the direct object. Begin from the two simple clauses whenever a longer sentence becomes confusing.

**Dont** represents a relationship introduced by **de** in many common constructions. *Nous parlons de ce film. → C'est le film dont nous parlons.* *Elle a besoin de ce document. → Voici le document dont elle a besoin.* The English rendering may be “that,” “which,” “about which,” or another phrase. The French choice follows *parler de* or *avoir besoin de*, not the English relative word.

*Dont* also expresses possession: *Je connais une chercheuse. Les travaux de cette chercheuse sont intéressants. → Je connais une chercheuse dont les travaux sont intéressants.* The relation *de cette chercheuse* has already been expressed by *dont*. Therefore do not write *dont ses travaux*, which repeats the possession. Use *dont les travaux*. The article belongs to *travaux* while *dont* establishes whose work it is.

With many other prepositions use **lequel, laquelle, lesquels, lesquelles**, agreeing with the antecedent. *La table sur laquelle j'ai posé le livre* — “the table on which I put the book”; *Les raisons pour lesquelles nous avons changé d'avis* — “the reasons why we changed our minds.” Agreement follows *table* or *raisons*, not the subject *je* or *nous*. When the antecedent is a person, *qui* after a preposition is often natural: *la collègue avec qui je travaille*.

Contractions with **à** are *auquel, à laquelle, auxquels, auxquelles*. With **de** they are *duquel, de laquelle, desquels, desquelles*. For “the project in which I am interested,” reconstruct *je m'intéresse à ce projet*: **le projet auquel je m'intéresse**. For “the room near which we waited,” reconstruct *près de cette salle*: **la salle près de laquelle nous avons attendu**. A multiword preposition such as *près de* cannot generally be replaced by *dont* while leaving *près* stranded.

Distinguish *ce qui*, *ce que*, and *ce dont* when there is no ordinary noun antecedent. *Ce qui m'intéresse, c'est la méthode* — “What interests me is the method”: *ce qui* is subject. *Ce que je veux comprendre, c'est la méthode* — “What I want to understand is the method”: *ce que* is direct object. *Ce dont j'ai besoin, c'est d'un exemple* — “What I need is an example”: *avoir besoin de* requires the *de* relationship. Do not choose by translating all three as “what.”

In writing practice, combine six pairs of clauses, then reverse each result into its two originals. If you cannot reconstruct the underlying preposition, your relative choice may be a guess. This reversible procedure is also a useful editing technique for long academic sentences. A relative clause should add a clear relationship rather than conceal a missing grammatical connection.

## 7. Describing processes with the passive

An active clause begins from the performer: *Le comité sélectionne les candidats* — “The committee selects the candidates.” A passive clause begins from the affected participants: *Les candidats sont sélectionnés par le comité* — “The candidates are selected by the committee.” The passive is **être + past participle**, with the participle agreeing with the grammatical subject. The tense is carried by *être*: *sont sélectionnés*, *ont été sélectionnés*, *seront sélectionnés*.

Walk through the transformation. Identify the active direct object *les candidates* in *Le comité a sélectionné les candidates*. Make it the passive subject. Keep the event in the past by using *ont été*. Add the participle and agree it with feminine plural *candidates*: **Les candidates ont été sélectionnées par le comité.** Do not confuse *ont été sélectionnées* with *étaient sélectionnées*, which changes the temporal viewpoint or may describe a state.

The agent often follows **par**: *Le bâtiment a été conçu par une architecte locale*. Some constructions use **de**, especially with states or conventional expressions: *Elle est appréciée de ses collègues*. Learn these as collocations rather than replacing every *par* with *de*. The agent can be omitted when unknown or irrelevant: *Les résultats seront publiés lundi*. That is useful in process descriptions, but repeated agentless passives can hide responsibility in an argument.

Only a suitable direct-object construction normally allows this transformation. *On téléphone aux candidats* cannot become *les candidats sont téléphonés*, because *téléphoner à* has an indirect complement. Say *On téléphone aux candidats* or change the verb appropriately: *Les candidats sont contactés par téléphone*. The second works because *contacter quelqu'un* takes a direct object. Do not let English passive patterns override a French verb's complement structure.

French frequently uses **on** instead of a passive: *On examine les demandes en juin* — “Applications are examined in June / They examine applications in June.” It can be lighter in speech. A pronominal construction can express a general process: *Ce formulaire se remplit en ligne* — “This form is filled in online.” Context decides whether *se* is reflexive or has a passive-like interpretation; forms do not map one-to-one across languages.

Explain a museum loan in four stages: the work is chosen, the condition is checked, transport is arranged, and the public is informed. Use both passive and *on*. Then ask who is responsible at each stage. If that matters, name the agent. Effective informational prose makes the process easy to follow and the actors visible when their roles affect the reader's decisions.

## 8. Participles, adjectives, and the gérondif

The present participle is often formed from the present *nous* stem plus **-ant**: *nous parlons → parlant*, *nous finissons → finissant*, *nous prenons → prenant*. Important exceptions are *étant*, *ayant*, and *sachant*. A verbal present participle is invariable: *des étudiants travaillant à temps partiel* — “students working part-time.” It can have its own complements and describes an action associated with the noun.

An adjective derived from a verb behaves as an adjective and agrees: *des activités intéressantes* — “interesting activities.” Compare *des personnes convainquant leurs voisins* — “people convincing their neighbors,” where the verbal participle has a direct object, with *des arguments convaincants* — “convincing arguments,” where the adjective describes the arguments. Some adjective and participle spellings differ. Do not assume every word ending in *-ant* has the same grammatical role.

The **gérondif** uses **en + present participle**: *en parlant*, *en lisant*, *en prenant le train*. It often expresses simultaneous action, means, or a related circumstance. *J'écoute un podcast en marchant* — “I listen to a podcast while walking”; *On apprend en pratiquant* — “We learn by practising.” English “by” and “while” represent different relations that French can express through the same form, so use context to interpret it.

The understood subject of the gérondif normally matches the subject of the main clause. *En entrant dans la salle, j'ai vu le professeur* means that I entered and I saw. The misleading *En entrant dans la salle, le téléphone a sonné* grammatically suggests the telephone entered the room. Repair it with a full temporal clause: *Quand je suis entré dans la salle, le téléphone a sonné.* This is a subject problem, not a conjugation problem.

**Tout en + participle** can emphasize simultaneity or a contrast: *Elle travaille tout en suivant une formation* — “She works while also taking a course”; *Il critique la méthode tout en reconnaissant son utilité* — “He criticizes the method while acknowledging its usefulness.” Do not treat *tout* here as the subject or translate it mechanically as “everything.” It modifies the relationship between the actions.

A compound participle can place an action earlier: *Ayant terminé son travail, elle est partie* — “Having finished her work, she left”; *Étant arrivée en avance, elle a attendu dehors* — “Having arrived early, she waited outside.” These are more common in formal writing than everyday conversation. The participle retains its appropriate auxiliary and agreement. If a sentence becomes awkward, use *comme elle avait terminé* or *après avoir terminé* instead. Condensation is a stylistic choice, not a compulsory mark of advanced ability.

## 9. Reading: a study is not a wish

Read this original report. Identify what the team observed, what it hopes to investigate, what participants felt, and what the project still cannot establish. These categories prevent you from presenting an aspiration as a research finding.

> **Une bibliothèque qui change ses horaires**
>
> Une bibliothèque municipale a proposé, pendant trois mois, une ouverture jusqu'à vingt et une heures deux soirs par semaine. L'équipe souhaitait que les habitants qui travaillent tard puissent utiliser les services sans se presser. Avant que l'expérience commence, un questionnaire a été distribué aux usagers. Les personnes auxquelles il a été envoyé ont été invitées à préciser leurs habitudes et leurs difficultés.
>
> Pendant l'expérience, le nombre d'entrées a augmenté le mardi soir. Plusieurs étudiants ont expliqué qu'ils appréciaient le calme et les tables sur lesquelles ils pouvaient installer leurs documents. D'autres visiteurs sont venus emprunter des livres en rentrant du travail. Une mère a dit qu'elle était contente que sa fille ait découvert un endroit agréable pour réviser.
>
> Ces observations sont encourageantes, bien que les résultats ne permettent pas de tirer toutes les conclusions. L'équipe n'a pas comparé la bibliothèque avec un établissement similaire dont les horaires seraient restés inchangés. Il est donc possible que d'autres facteurs aient contribué à la hausse de fréquentation.
>
> Pour la prochaine étape, la responsable demande que les données soient recueillies de manière plus régulière. Elle espère qu'une comparaison plus précise aidera la ville à décider. « Nous ne cherchons pas seulement une idée qui paraisse séduisante, explique-t-elle. Nous cherchons une organisation qui réponde aux besoins réels des habitants, sans que le personnel doive accepter des horaires imprévisibles. »

**Full translation.** For three months a municipal library offered opening until nine in the evening twice a week. The team wanted residents who work late to be able to use the services without rushing. Before the experiment began, a questionnaire was distributed to users. The people to whom it was sent were invited to describe their habits and difficulties. During the experiment, the number of visits increased on Tuesday evenings. Several students explained that they appreciated the quiet and the tables on which they could spread out their documents. Other visitors came to borrow books on their way home from work. A mother said she was happy that her daughter had discovered a pleasant place to revise. These observations are encouraging, although the results do not permit every conclusion to be drawn. The team did not compare the library with a similar establishment whose hours would have remained unchanged. Other factors may therefore have contributed to increased attendance. For the next stage, the manager asks for data to be collected more regularly. She hopes that a more precise comparison will help the city decide. “We are not only looking for an idea that looks attractive,” she explains. “We are looking for an arrangement that meets residents' real needs without staff having to accept unpredictable hours.”

The report includes more than one reason for a subjunctive. *Souhaitait que…puissent* presents a wish. *Bien que…ne permettent pas* presents a concession. *Il est possible que…aient contribué* presents an uncertain completed contribution. *Demande que…soient recueillies* presents a requested action in the passive. In *soient recueillies*, *soient* marks the mood and *recueillies* agrees with *données*. Separate those decisions rather than treating the whole expression as a single memorized block.

The report does not prove that longer hours caused every extra visit. Write a two-sentence summary preserving that limitation. A model is *La fréquentation a augmenté pendant une expérience d'ouverture en soirée. Cependant, les données ne suffisent pas à établir que ce changement explique à lui seul la hausse observée.* Then give one practical recommendation beginning *Il serait utile que…*. This combines the conditional's tact with the subjunctive's evaluation frame.

## 10. Translation and editing workshop

Translate these into French before looking at the models. Mark the expression that governs each clause, then identify whether the embedded event is presented as asserted, desired, evaluated, or uncertain.

1. I want the students to ask questions.
2. We are happy that you are here.
3. She hopes that the course will be useful.
4. It is possible that they have already left.
5. You need to finish before Friday.
6. We speak slowly so that everyone can understand.
7. This is the document that I need.
8. Here is the researcher whose work I admire.
9. The project in which she is interested is international.
10. The applications will be examined by a committee.
11. I learned these words by reading short articles.
12. After she has presented the results, we will discuss them.

**Models and explanations.** 1. *Je veux que les étudiants posent des questions.* Different subjects require the wish clause, and *poser une question* is the collocation. 2. *Nous sommes heureux que vous soyez ici.* Emotion takes the subjunctive although the presence is real. 3. *Elle espère que la formation sera utile.* Affirmative *espérer* normally takes the indicative. 4. *Il est possible qu'ils soient déjà partis.* Possibility frame plus past subjunctive; the completed action uses *être*. 5. *Il faut que vous finissiez avant vendredi.* *Avant vendredi* is a prepositional phrase and does not itself introduce another verb. 6. *Nous parlons lentement pour que tout le monde puisse comprendre.* The purpose has a different subject, grammatically singular *tout le monde*.

7. *C'est le document dont j'ai besoin.* Reconstruct *j'ai besoin de ce document*. 8. *Voici la chercheuse dont j'admire les travaux.* The missing *de* relationship belongs to *les travaux de la chercheuse*, even though *admirer* itself takes a direct object. 9. *Le projet auquel elle s'intéresse est international.* Reconstruct *s'intéresser à ce projet*. 10. *Les candidatures seront examinées par un comité.* Feminine plural subject requires *examinées*. 11. *J'ai appris ces mots en lisant de courts articles.* The person learning also reads. 12. *Après qu'elle aura présenté les résultats, nous en discuterons.* Standard *après que* takes the indicative; the future perfect marks completion before the future discussion. *Discuter des résultats* permits *en*; *discuter les résultats* also exists with a different direct-object construction.

Repair the errors in these sentences: *Je souhaite qu'il vient.* *Je suis content que tu es là.* *La collègue dont son bureau est à côté vient demain.* *Les candidates ont été choisi.* *En lisant le rapport, les erreurs m'ont surpris.* Models: *Je souhaite qu'il vienne*; *Je suis content que tu sois là*; *La collègue dont le bureau est à côté vient demain*; *Les candidates ont été choisies*; *En lisant le rapport, j'ai été surpris par les erreurs.* The final repair makes the reader of the report the subject of the main clause.

Now translate from French into English: *Je cherche quelqu'un qui puisse nous aider.* *Je connais quelqu'un qui peut nous aider.* *Il est probable qu'elle viendra.* *Il faut que les dossiers aient été envoyés avant lundi.* The first seeks a suitable person without asserting one has been identified; the second identifies someone with the ability. The third presents her coming as probable. The fourth requires the files to have been sent before Monday: the subjunctive auxiliary *aient*, passive *été*, and agreement *envoyés* all have separate jobs.

## Listening and speaking with grammatical purpose

For a listening cycle, choose eight companion sentences containing *soit*, *ait*, *puisse*, *fasse*, and *vienne*. Hide the written French, listen, type the sentence, and provide an English meaning. Then group your errors: a missing trigger, a mistaken subject, an unfamiliar verb form, or an inaudible written ending. The last category often needs orthographic review rather than more volume or slower speech. Re-listen to the full phrase after checking so that the repair returns to meaningful language.

For speaking, propose a community workshop. You must explain its audience, desired outcome, two practical requirements, one uncertainty, and the way participants will be selected. Use at least one indicative assertion and explain why it is asserted. Then answer a skeptical question: “How do you know people will attend?” A defensible answer distinguishes hope from evidence: *Nous espérons qu'ils viendront, mais il faut que nous vérifiions la demande avant de réserver une grande salle.*

In partner work, one person represents organizers and the other participants. Organizers request a schedule; participants express constraints. Use *je préférerais que*, *il faudrait que*, and *à condition que* when their meanings fit, rather than inserting them merely to meet a quota. Repeat the discussion with a changed condition, such as a smaller budget or no evening access. Adaptation demonstrates a stronger command than a perfectly memorized first conversation.

## The chapter project and a realistic checkpoint

Write a 250–300-word proposal for an educational or cultural activity. Include a concise description, evidence of a need, two requirements, one limitation, and a next step. Use a relative clause to identify a resource, a passive or *on* construction to explain a process, and a purpose clause. Then edit every embedded clause: who is its subject, what relation introduces it, and which mood is justified? If an answer is unclear, split the sentence and rebuild it.

Present the proposal for three minutes and take two follow-up questions. Ask the listener to summarize your main recommendation. If their summary differs, decide whether the problem came from grammar, organization, vocabulary, or an unstated assumption. Revise the relevant cause. Correcting every accent while ignoring an unclear recommendation is poor prioritization.

Score task completion, coherence, grammatical control, and interaction from zero to four, using the scale from chapter five. Add a qualitative check: did you distinguish what is known, what is desired, and what remains uncertain? Aim for a clear independent performance with recoverable errors, then repeat the task later with new content. These local scores document growth; they are not an official CEFR assessment.

Before continuing, explain five mood choices in your own words, reconstruct three complex relatives as simple clauses, and repair one dangling gérondif. # 07 · Argument and precise prose

**Working level:** B2. **Study budget:** 100 hours, course hours 601–700. **Prerequisites:** chapters five and six, especially time relationships, reported speech, subjunctive frames, relative clauses, passive voice, and participles. This chapter helps you build a defensible argument, report another person's position fairly, and edit the grammatical details of careful French.

An argument is not a pile of impressive connecting words. It is a relationship between a claim, reasons, evidence, limitations, and a response to a reasonable objection. French grammar helps make those relationships explicit. A reader should know what you assert, where the information comes from, what remains uncertain, and why your recommendation follows. The same skills support work messages, public discussion, study, and examination tasks.

## 1. An argument the reader can inspect

Begin with a question small enough to discuss concretely: should a town extend its library's opening hours? *Il faut prolonger les horaires* — “Opening hours should be extended” is a claim, but it does not yet explain why. Add a reason: *de nombreux habitants travaillent pendant les heures d'ouverture actuelles* — “many residents work during the current opening hours.” Add evidence if available: a local survey, attendance figures, or a clearly described observation. Do not invent a statistic to make the paragraph sound authoritative.

A useful paragraph can follow **claim → reason → example → limitation → implication**. *La bibliothèque devrait ouvrir plus tard deux soirs par semaine. Cette mesure permettrait aux habitants qui travaillent en journée d'utiliser ses services. Par exemple, une personne qui termine à dix-huit heures pourrait emprunter un livre sans prendre un congé. Il faudrait toutefois vérifier le coût et les disponibilités du personnel. Une expérimentation limitée serait donc préférable à une extension immédiate de tous les horaires.*

Translation: “The library should open later two evenings a week. This measure would allow residents who work during the day to use its services. For example, someone who finishes at six could borrow a book without taking time off. However, costs and staff availability would need to be checked. A limited trial would therefore be preferable to an immediate extension of all opening hours.” The paragraph does not claim that one example proves demand. It shows a plausible benefit and proposes a way to investigate the uncertainty.

Notice how grammar supports the argument. *Devrait* recommends rather than reports a current obligation. *Permettrait* describes an anticipated consequence. *Qui travaillent* identifies the intended group. *Il faudrait* introduces a requirement before action. *Donc* marks a conclusion drawn from those considerations. Each form has a job. Adding *en outre*, *néanmoins*, and *par conséquent* without clear relationships would not make the reasoning stronger.

Use **selon** to identify a source: *Selon le rapport, la fréquentation a augmenté* — “According to the report, attendance increased.” The phrase attributes the information; it does not independently verify it. Use **d'après** similarly for a source or basis: *D'après les chiffres disponibles…* Say *à mon avis* for a personal view and *dans ce cas* for a conclusion tied to a particular situation. These signals prevent the reader from confusing observation, inference, and opinion.

Practice by writing a paragraph without any connector beyond *parce que*, *mais*, and *donc*. Ask whether each sentence actually follows from the previous one. Then introduce a more precise connector only when it clarifies the relationship. Good B2 prose can be plain. Accuracy and relevance matter more than the number of formal expressions displayed.

## 2. Cause is not the same as consequence

**Parce que** introduces a cause or reason that answers why: *Le service ferme plus tôt parce que deux employés sont absents*. **Puisque** presents a reason treated as known or accepted: *Puisque vous avez déjà les documents, nous pouvons commencer*. **Comme**, at the beginning of a clause, can supply background cause: *Comme le bâtiment est fermé, la réunion aura lieu en ligne*. The same word *comme* has other uses, so inspect the whole sentence.

Prepositional expressions take a noun phrase rather than a finite clause. **À cause de** often introduces an unwelcome cause: *à cause d'une panne*. **Grâce à** commonly credits a beneficial cause: *grâce au soutien des bénévoles*. **En raison de** is more neutral or formal: *en raison des travaux*. Their evaluations can be used ironically, but start with ordinary meanings. A phrase such as *grâce à la pollution* will sound intentionally surprising unless the context explains the favorable effect.

Build “The event was postponed because of the storm.” Name the event and completed passive: *L'événement a été reporté*. Add the noun cause: **L'événement a été reporté à cause de la tempête.** To use a full clause, say *parce qu'une tempête était annoncée*. Do not write *à cause que* in standard edited prose. Regional uses exist, but this course's production target is widely understood careful French.

Consequence can use **donc**, **alors**, **par conséquent**, or **c'est pourquoi**. *Le bâtiment est fermé; la réunion aura donc lieu en ligne.* *C'est pourquoi* generally links to the result as a new clause: *Le bâtiment est fermé. C'est pourquoi nous avons changé de lieu.* Match punctuation to the structure rather than joining a page of independent clauses with commas. A semicolon can separate closely related independent clauses, while a full stop is often the clearest choice.

Degree-result constructions include **si + adjective/adverb + que** and **tellement + adjective/adverb + que**: *Le débat était si long que plusieurs participants sont partis* — “The debate was so long that several participants left.” With nouns, use *tellement de*: *Il y avait tellement de bruit que nous n'entendions rien*. With *tel*, agreement matters: *une telle différence que…*, *de tels écarts que…*. Choose a simple structure you can control before expanding it.

Distinguish a reported consequence from a purpose. *Elle a expliqué la règle, de sorte que nous avons compris* reports understanding as a result. *Elle explique la règle pour que nous comprenions* presents understanding as the goal. The first takes an indicative result; the second takes the subjunctive after *pour que*. An intended effect may fail, while a reported effect is presented as having occurred. That distinction is central to evaluating proposals and studies.

When discussing data, avoid automatically inferring cause from two things occurring together. *La fréquentation a augmenté après le changement* states sequence; *la fréquentation a augmenté grâce au changement* attributes a favorable cause. If evidence is limited, write *Le changement a peut-être contribué à cette hausse*. The cautious wording is more accurate because it identifies an inference rather than disguising it as an observation.

## 3. Conceding a point and defining a purpose

A concession acknowledges something that could count against the main claim while maintaining the claim. **Bien que + subjunctive**: *Bien que cette mesure soit coûteuse, elle pourrait être utile* — “Although this measure is costly, it could be useful.” **Même si + indicative**: *Même si cette mesure est coûteuse, elle pourrait être utile*. *Même si* can also introduce hypothetical situations with the appropriate indicative tense. Do not put the subjunctive after it merely because English says “even if.”

**Malgré + noun** provides a shorter structure: *Malgré son coût, cette mesure pourrait être utile*. *Malgré que* exists in usage and is treated differently by reference works and registers; for your own careful writing here, choose the uncontroversial *bien que* with a clause or *malgré* with a noun. You do not need a disputed construction to make a precise concession.

Contrastive connectors include *cependant*, *pourtant*, *toutefois*, and *néanmoins*. They overlap but can emphasize different relationships. *Pourtant* often highlights an unexpected contrast: *Le service est gratuit; pourtant, il reste peu utilisé*. *Toutefois* often introduces a qualification: *La proposition est intéressante. Toutefois, son financement reste incertain*. Read the sentence aloud and identify exactly what expectation is being challenged or limited.

Opposition compares contrasting facts: *Tandis que certains habitants souhaitent une ouverture le soir, d'autres préfèrent le dimanche* — “While some residents want evening opening, others prefer Sunday.” Here *tandis que* is contrastive, not merely temporal. **En revanche** can introduce a compensating contrast: *Le bâtiment est petit; en revanche, il est bien situé*. **Au contraire** contradicts or reverses a preceding idea: *Ce service n'est pas inutile; au contraire, il répond à un besoin réel*. They are not interchangeable decorative words.

Purpose with a shared subject uses **pour/afin de + infinitive**: *La ville consulte les habitants pour mieux comprendre leurs besoins*. A different subject commonly uses **pour que/afin que + subjunctive**: *La ville consulte les habitants pour que chacun puisse s'exprimer*. The city consults, and each person expresses an opinion. The clause structure makes that change of subject explicit.

Negative purpose can use *pour ne pas*: *Nous limitons la durée pour ne pas fatiguer les participants* — “We limit the duration so as not to tire participants.” With another subject: *Nous faisons une pause pour que les participants ne soient pas trop fatigués*. Keep negation with the verb whose action you negate. *Nous ne faisons pas une pause pour…* can instead deny the making of a pause or challenge its purpose, depending on context.

Write a balanced pair of paragraphs. In the first, give the strongest reasonable objection to your preferred policy. In the second, answer it without pretending it has disappeared. Begin *Il est vrai que…*, then *Cependant…*. A fair concession preserves the opponent's actual claim; it does not replace that claim with an easier, exaggerated version. Grammatical sophistication cannot repair an unfair summary.

## 4. Reported speech and a moving timeline

The key question in reported speech is “From which time are we viewing the events?” Suppose on Monday a manager says *Je suis occupée, j'ai reçu votre dossier et je vous répondrai mercredi*. When reporting later with a past reporting verb, say *Elle a dit qu'elle était occupée, qu'elle avait reçu notre dossier et qu'elle nous répondrait mercredi*. The three embedded verbs represent simultaneity, earlier completion, and later action relative to Monday's statement.

This gives a useful ordinary pattern: present becomes **imparfait**, passé composé becomes **plus-que-parfait**, future becomes **conditional present**, and future perfect becomes **conditional past** when the viewpoint shifts to a past reporting moment. *Elle a dit : « J'aurai terminé avant midi. » → Elle a dit qu'elle aurait terminé avant midi.* The conditional here marks future completion relative to her earlier statement. It does not necessarily signal an unreal condition.

Do not treat the pattern as an instruction to change every verb blindly. If the narrator explicitly maintains a statement's current validity, the present can remain: *Le professeur a expliqué que l'eau bout à cent degrés à la pression atmosphérique normale*. The general claim is presented as still valid, with its physical condition specified. *Il m'a dit qu'il habite à Lyon* can emphasize his continuing residence; *qu'il habitait à Lyon* situates it at the time of the statement. Context and intended viewpoint guide the choice.

Pronouns and possessives require the same attention as tense. *Nadia m'a dit : « Je t'enverrai mon document. »* becomes *Nadia m'a dit qu'elle m'enverrait son document*. *Je* becomes *elle*, *te* refers to the narrator and becomes *me*, and *mon* becomes *son*. *Son* agrees with masculine singular *document*, not with Nadia's gender. The owner is identified by the sentence context.

Time and place words can shift: *aujourd'hui → ce jour-là*, *demain → le lendemain*, *hier → la veille*, *ici → là* when the reporting perspective changes. These are not mandatory replacements regardless of context. If you report the same morning in the same room, *ici* may still be accurate. If the date matters operationally, use the date: *Elle a dit le 4 mai qu'elle répondrait le 6 mai*. A clear calendar reference can prevent mistakes that elegant grammatical transformation would leave unresolved.

Indirect questions use statement order: *Il a demandé où nous habitions*, not *où habitions-nous*. A yes–no question uses *si*: *Elle a demandé si nous avions reçu le message*. An instruction often uses *de + infinitive*: *Il nous a demandé de patienter*. For a negative instruction: *Elle nous a demandé de ne pas ouvrir la pièce jointe*. Identify the speech act—statement, question, or request—before choosing the reporting structure.

The OQLF's [temporal value of the conditional](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/24138/la-grammaire/le-verbe/temps-grammaticaux/conditionnel/valeur-temporelle-du-conditionnel), inspected 13 November 2026, is a useful reference for the future-from-a-past-viewpoint interpretation. Practice with a three-column timeline: original speech date, embedded event date, and reporting date. If those dates are clear, the tense decision becomes a meaningful representation rather than a puzzle about labels.

## 5. Distinguishing voices and degrees of commitment

Reporting verbs communicate stance. *Dire* is relatively neutral. *Affirmer* presents a confident assertion, *reconnaître* acknowledges something, *préciser* adds specificity, *souligner* emphasizes, and *prétendre* can suggest that a claim is disputed or doubtful. If a journalist simply reported something, translating the verb as *prétendre* may unfairly make the journalist sound suspicious. Choose a verb that matches the actual speech act.

Compare *Le rapport montre que…* with *Le rapport suggère que…*. The first presents stronger evidential support, while the second indicates a tentative interpretation. If you have only a short abstract or a secondhand summary, do not pretend to know exactly what the full report demonstrates. Language practice is an opportunity to develop honest sourcing: *D'après le résumé disponible, les auteurs estiment que…* makes your access and attribution clearer.

The conditional can report information that the speaker does not confirm: *Le projet coûterait deux millions d'euros* — “The project reportedly would cost / is said to cost two million euros.” This **conditional of reported information** differs from the conditional in an explicit hypothetical and from future-in-the-past. Context usually identifies which use is intended. Add a source when possible: *Selon le journal local, le projet coûterait…*. This form should not be used to spread an invented rumor.

Separate direct quotation from paraphrase. Direct quotation preserves the actual wording and needs an identifiable source. A paraphrase uses your own wording while preserving the point: *La responsable estime que la période d'essai est trop courte*. Do not put your improved or simplified sentence in quotation marks as if the person said it exactly. In the learning portfolio, use fictional sources clearly labeled as fictional if you need a dialogue without relying on a real article.

For an oral summary, try a three-part frame: *Le premier intervenant soutient que… Le second reconnaît…mais estime que… Pour ma part…*. The final marker separates your view from the preceding views. Give each person a fair reason before evaluating it. When someone challenges your summary, use repair language: *Si je vous ai bien compris…*, *Vous voulez dire que… ?*, *Je reformule…*. Interaction includes revising your interpretation, not merely defending the first version.

Listen to a short human interview and take notes in two columns: speaker's claim and supporting reason. Add a third column for your own questions after listening. Then summarize without looking at a transcript. Check whether you accidentally turned a possibility into a certainty, omitted a condition, or attributed your own inference to the speaker. Those are meaning errors even if every verb ending is correct.

## 6. Completed infinitives and concise sequencing

The **past infinitive** uses **avoir or être + past participle**: *avoir compris*, *être arrivé*, *s'être trompé*. It expresses an action viewed as completed relative to another point. *Je suis content d'avoir participé* — “I am happy to have participated”; *Après avoir lu le rapport, elle a posé une question* — “After reading the report, she asked a question.” The infinitive's subject is understood from the construction, so it must remain clear.

Build “After sending the files, we called the client.” The sending and calling have the same subject, *nous*. Use *après avoir envoyé les fichiers*, then the main clause: **Après avoir envoyé les fichiers, nous avons appelé le client.** In a compound infinitive, a direct object following the participle does not trigger agreement with *avoir*. If the direct object pronoun precedes, agreement applies where required: *Après les avoir envoyés, nous avons appelé le client*, with *les* referring to masculine plural *fichiers*.

With *être*, agreement follows the appropriate subject relationship: *Après être arrivée, elle a téléphoné* — “After arriving, she called.” The final *-e* marks a feminine speaker or subject in this example. For a pronominal verb: *Après s'être reposée, elle s'est remise au travail*. Harder pronominal participles require direct-object analysis, explained below. Do not replace every *être* participle agreement with an unqualified “agree with the subject” rule.

The negative infinitive often places *ne pas* before the whole compound: *Il regrette de ne pas avoir répondu* — “He regrets not having replied.” *Merci de ne pas avoir oublié* — “Thank you for not having forgotten.” The pronoun stays with the auxiliary: *Je regrette de ne pas lui avoir répondu*. In speech, *ne* may disappear, but retain it in careful writing. A local grammar checker that accepts the sentence cannot decide whether the regret is sincere or contextually appropriate.

An infinitive can prevent an overloaded chain of *que* clauses, but only when the subject relation is valid. *Après avoir lu le rapport, les conclusions semblent fragiles* incorrectly makes the conclusions appear to have read the report. Repair *Après avoir lu le rapport, je trouve les conclusions fragiles*, or *Après la lecture du rapport, les conclusions semblent fragiles*, with a suitable context identifying the evaluator. The underlying actor must remain accessible.

Practice converting pairs of clauses in both directions: *Elle a terminé son exposé. Elle a répondu aux questions. → Après avoir terminé son exposé, elle a répondu aux questions.* Then explain why a pair with different subjects cannot use the same shortcut. This reversible exercise reveals whether condensation preserves meaning. Advanced writing is not measured by how many subjects you can omit.

## 7. Participle agreement: find the direct object first

With **avoir**, the ordinary past participle agrees with a **preceding direct object**, not with the subject. *J'ai lu les lettres* has the object after the participle, so *lu* remains unchanged. *Les lettres que j'ai lues* has *que* representing feminine plural *lettres* before the participle, so write *lues*. Ask “I read what?” to identify the object, then ask where that object appears. Perform both steps; a nearby feminine noun alone is not enough.

Pronouns make the same relationship compact. *Tu as reçu les invitations ? — Oui, je les ai reçues.* *Les* represents feminine plural *invitations* and precedes *ai reçues*. In *Je leur ai répondu*, *leur* represents an indirect recipient because one *répond à quelqu'un*. There is no direct object represented by *leur*, so *répondu* does not agree with it. Learn the verb's complement pattern before deciding on an ending.

With an ordinary **être** passive or nonpronominal compound, the participle agrees with the subject: *Les portes sont fermées*; *Les étudiantes sont arrivées*. But pronominal verbs require more discrimination. *Elles se sont lavées* has *se* as the direct object: they washed themselves. *Elles se sont lavé les mains* has *les mains* as the following direct object and *se* as the indirect possessor or beneficiary, so *lavé* remains unchanged.

Compare reciprocal actions: *Elles se sont vues* because *voir quelqu'un* is direct, but *elles se sont parlé* because *parler à quelqu'un* is indirect. Another contrast is *elles se sont écrit* versus *les lettres qu'elles se sont écrites*. In the first, the recipients are indirect and no preceding direct object controls agreement. In the second, *que* represents preceding feminine plural *lettres*, so *écrites* agrees with those letters. The subjects are identical; the object structure changes the spelling.

Some verbs are essentially pronominal or have a pronominal meaning that cannot be analyzed as an ordinary direct/indirect object pattern. They generally agree with the subject: *elles se sont souvenues de la réunion*, *elles se sont enfuies*. A few expressions have special behavior, such as invariable *se rendre compte*: *elles se sont rendu compte de l'erreur*. Keep a reference for these lexical cases rather than forcing every verb into a simplified slogan.

The OQLF's [occasionally pronominal participle agreement](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/22942/la-grammaire/le-verbe/accord-du-participe-passe/avec-lauxiliaire-etre/accord-du-participe-passe-des-verbes-occasionnellement-pronominaux), inspected 13 November 2026, supports this direct-object analysis. The chapter's examples are original. For an editing routine, underline the verb, reconstruct its nonpronominal complement pattern when appropriate, label the direct object, and mark whether it comes before the participle. Consult a reference for a special verb rather than guessing from the auxiliary alone.

Before an infinitive, additional distinctions arise. *Les chanteuses que j'ai entendues chanter* has preceding singers who perform *chanter*, so agreement applies. *Les chansons que j'ai entendu chanter* has songs being sung, so *entendu* remains invariable in this construction. *Fait* immediately before an infinitive is invariable: *les robes qu'elle a fait retoucher*. For *laissé + infinitive*, traditional and rectified conventions differ; the 1990 rectifications permit invariability. Use a consistent convention and consult a reference when editing for a particular institution.

Many of these endings are silent. Dictation therefore requires grammatical reasoning as well as sound recognition. Do not infer that an inaudible written distinction is unimportant in an edited document, or that a speaker must articulate an extra consonant that ordinary French leaves silent. Speaking and orthographic editing draw on overlapping but different skills.

## 8. Negation, restriction, and formal questions

**Ne…que** expresses restriction, usually “only”: *La réunion ne durera qu'une heure* — “The meeting will last only one hour.” The restricted element follows *que*. *Je n'ai parlé qu'à Nadia* means I spoke only to Nadia. *Je n'ai fait que parler à Nadia* means all I did was speak to Nadia. Moving the focus can change the claim. Do not translate a sentence with *ne…que* as an ordinary negative.

**Ne…pas que** commonly means “not only”: *Il n'y a pas que le prix à considérer* — “Price is not the only thing to consider.” Compare *Il n'y a que le prix à considérer* — “Only the price needs to be considered.” A single *pas* reverses the restriction's implication. Also distinguish *ne…plus* — “no longer,” *ne…jamais* — “never,” *ne…rien* — “nothing/not anything,” and *ne…personne* — “no one/not anyone.” The second element provides much of the negative meaning.

An **expletive ne** does not negate the event. *Je crains qu'il ne pleuve* means “I fear that it will rain,” not “I fear that it will not rain.” *Je crains qu'il ne pleuve pas* expresses the fear that it will not rain. Expletive *ne* appears especially in careful written language after some fear expressions, *avant que*, *à moins que*, and certain comparisons. It is often omitted in everyday speech without changing the affirmative event's meaning.

Compare *Elle est plus compétente que je ne le pensais* — “She is more competent than I thought.” The *ne* does not turn *pensais* negative. In *Partons avant qu'il ne soit trop tard*, the meaning is “Let us leave before it is too late.” You can produce *avant qu'il soit trop tard* as well. The OQLF's [expletive ne reference](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/22467/la-syntaxe/la-negation-et-la-restriction/constructions-avec-un-ne-expletif), inspected 13 November 2026, distinguishes this optional element from negation.

Formal yes–no questions can invert a pronoun subject: *Avez-vous reçu le rapport ?* With a noun subject, repeat it through a pronoun: *Le comité a-t-il reçu le rapport ?* The linking *-t-* occurs where needed between a verb ending in a written vowel and *il/elle/on*: *a-t-il*, *parle-t-elle*. Do not add it after an existing *t*: *vient-il*, not *vient-t-il*. Hyphens are part of the written construction.

Question words require their proper complement relationship: *À qui avez-vous répondu ?* because *répondre à*; *De quoi parle ce rapport ?* because *parler de*. Formal inversion is not compulsory in all speech. *Est-ce que vous avez reçu le rapport ?* and conversational rising intonation are normal in suitable contexts. Inside an indirect question, return to statement order: *Je voudrais savoir si le comité a reçu le rapport*. A common editing error mixes direct and indirect patterns into *je voudrais savoir a-t-il reçu*.

## 9. Reading a public debate without adopting every claim

The following original text describes a fictional debate. No numerical or institutional claim in it is presented as a real local finding. Read first for the disagreement, then label each speaker's reasons and uncertainties.

> **Faut-il supprimer les impressions automatiques ?**
>
> Une université envisage de ne plus imprimer automatiquement les documents distribués en cours. Selon la direction, cette mesure permettrait de réduire le gaspillage et de dégager un budget pour l'accessibilité numérique. Les documents resteraient disponibles en ligne et les étudiants qui en auraient besoin pourraient demander une version papier.
>
> Lors d'une réunion, une représentante étudiante a reconnu que certaines impressions finissaient à la poubelle sans avoir été lues. Elle a toutefois souligné que tous les étudiants ne disposaient pas d'un appareil adapté. Elle a demandé comment les personnes qui rencontrent des difficultés de lecture à l'écran seraient accompagnées. « Il ne suffit pas que le fichier existe, a-t-elle ajouté. Il faut qu'il soit réellement utilisable. »
>
> Un enseignant a expliqué qu'il avait déjà réduit le nombre de pages distribuées. Après avoir interrogé son groupe, il avait conservé une courte synthèse imprimée et publié les exercices supplémentaires en ligne. Cette organisation lui avait paru satisfaisante, bien qu'il n'ait pas encore évalué ses effets à long terme.
>
> La direction a proposé une période d'essai. Elle a précisé que les économies éventuelles seraient mesurées et que les demandes de documents papier ne seraient pas facturées aux étudiants. Plusieurs participants ont accepté ce principe, à condition que les procédures restent simples. D'autres ont demandé une consultation plus large avant qu'une décision définitive ne soit prise.
>
> Le débat ne porte donc pas uniquement sur la quantité de papier. Il concerne aussi l'accès aux ressources, le temps de travail des enseignants et la manière d'évaluer une politique. Une expérimentation pourrait éclairer la décision, pourvu que ses critères soient définis à l'avance et que les difficultés rencontrées soient effectivement prises en compte.

**Full translation.** A university is considering no longer automatically printing documents handed out in classes. According to management, the measure would reduce waste and free up a budget for digital accessibility. Documents would remain available online, and students who needed them could request a paper version. At a meeting, a student representative acknowledged that some printouts ended up in the bin without being read. She nevertheless emphasized that not all students had a suitable device. She asked how people who experience difficulty reading on a screen would be supported. “It is not enough for the file to exist,” she added. “It has to be genuinely usable.” A teacher explained that he had already reduced the number of pages distributed. After asking his group, he had kept a short printed summary and published additional exercises online. This arrangement had seemed satisfactory to him, although he had not yet evaluated its long-term effects. Management proposed a trial period. It stated that any savings would be measured and that students would not be charged for paper documents. Several participants accepted this principle provided that procedures remained simple. Others requested wider consultation before a final decision was taken. The debate therefore is not solely about paper quantity. It also concerns access to resources, teachers' work time, and how a policy is evaluated. A trial could inform the decision provided that its criteria were defined in advance and difficulties encountered were actually taken into account.

The text distinguishes a proposed benefit from a measured outcome. *Permettrait* and *pourrait* remain conditional because the proposed system has not established its effects. *Elle a demandé comment…seraient accompagnées* is an indirect question with future-in-the-past. *Avant qu'une décision…ne soit prise* has expletive *ne*, not negation. *Sans avoir été lues* is a past passive infinitive whose participle agrees with the understood feminine plural printouts.

Write a neutral 90-word summary before giving your opinion. Include the proposed change, the central accessibility concern, and the trial's conditions. Do not claim that every student opposes the measure or that printing has already ended. Then write a 90-word response recommending one criterion for evaluation. A clear recommendation could consider timely access, not only the number of printed pages.

## 10. Translation, correction, and an argument portfolio

Translate into French: 1. Although the proposal is useful, its cost remains uncertain. 2. Since you already have the report, we can begin. 3. They changed the schedule so that everyone could participate. 4. She said that she had sent the documents. 5. He said that he would answer the next day. 6. After reading the article, I changed my opinion. 7. The letters that they sent each other are long. 8. They spoke to each other yesterday. 9. The meeting will last only one hour. 10. I fear that the project will be delayed. 11. The committee reportedly has accepted the proposal. 12. I would like to know whether the applications have been examined.

**Explained models.** 1. *Bien que la proposition soit utile, son coût reste incertain.* Subjunctive after the concession frame. 2. *Puisque vous avez déjà le rapport, nous pouvons commencer.* A reason treated as accepted. 3. *Ils ont changé les horaires pour que tout le monde puisse participer.* Purpose, not an asserted result; singular *tout le monde*. 4. *Elle a dit qu'elle avait envoyé les documents.* The sending precedes the past statement. 5. *Il a dit qu'il répondrait le lendemain.* Conditional as future-in-the-past. 6. *Après avoir lu l'article, j'ai changé d'avis.* Shared subject and completed infinitive; *changer d'avis* is the collocation.

7. *Les lettres qu'ils se sont envoyées sont longues.* Preceding direct object *que*, representing feminine plural letters, controls *envoyées*. 8. *Ils se sont parlé hier.* The reciprocal participants are indirect complements of *parler à*, so no agreement on *parlé*. 9. *La réunion ne durera qu'une heure.* Restriction means only. 10. *Je crains que le projet soit retardé* or *ne soit retardé*. Optional expletive *ne* does not reverse the event. 11. *Le comité aurait accepté la proposition.* Add the actual source in real writing; the conditional signals unconfirmed reporting here. 12. *Je voudrais savoir si les candidatures ont été examinées.* Indirect question has statement order; passive participle agrees with feminine plural *candidatures*.

Correct these: *Bien que le coût est élevé, nous acceptons.* *Elle a demandé où habitions-nous.* *Les informations que j'ai reçu sont utiles.* *Elles se sont téléphonées.* *La réunion ne durera pas qu'une heure* when the intended meaning is “only one hour.” Repairs: *Bien que le coût soit élevé…*; *Elle a demandé où nous habitions*; *Les informations que j'ai reçues…*; *Elles se sont téléphoné* because *téléphoner à* is indirect; *La réunion ne durera qu'une heure*. Explain each repair before copying it.

For listening, use ten companion sentences with the written text hidden, then a two-minute human report. Type the French and an English meaning for the sentence task. For the report, take notes rather than attempting a complete transcript. Identify an attribution, a concession, and a proposed consequence. Check whether an unfamiliar connector caused you to reverse the speaker's position. Replay that relationship in its whole sentence, not as an isolated word.

For speaking, deliver a four-minute recommendation on a manageable issue: a workplace schedule, library service, university resource, or local transport change. State a claim, provide two reasons and one concrete example, concede a real limitation, and propose a next step. A partner should ask two skeptical questions. Use clarification when needed and revise a point if the objection exposes missing evidence. Reasonable adaptation is a strength.

Write a 300–350-word argument, then edit in separate passes. First check the argument and source attribution. Next check subjects, clause connections, and times. Then check direct objects and participle agreement. Finally check accents, punctuation, and repeated vocabulary. Separate passes reduce the chance that a small spelling issue distracts you from a missing reason. Keep your first and final versions to make improvement visible.

Use the four dimensions from previous chapters—task completion, coherence, language control, interaction—each from zero to four. Add a written note on evidential honesty: did you preserve uncertainty and represent opposing views fairly? A useful milestone is a clear argument that another reader can summarize accurately, with errors that do not repeatedly distort relationships. This is evidence of progress on these tasks, not an official proficiency verdict.

The next stage expands the vocabulary, register, and discourse flexibility needed for demanding B2 and C1 work. Continue revisiting these structures through authentic reading and conversation. Grammar becomes durable when you use it to solve new communication problems, notice the result, and repair what did not work.

# Chapter 08 — Register, cohesion, and literary reading

**Stage:** B2 toward C1. **Study allocation:** 100 hours, in ten ten-hour units. **Prerequisites:** ordinary past and future tenses, conditional and subjunctive choices, object pronouns, relative clauses, and paragraph argument. The stage label describes this course's teaching sequence; it is not an official grammar checklist.

You can now express an opinion. The next problem is making an opinion belong to a situation. A message to a friend, a complaint to a company, an interview, and an essay can communicate similar facts while organizing attention differently. Advanced French is not a collection of unusually long words. It is control over what a listener already knows, what you want to emphasize, how directly you make a request, and what relationship your wording establishes.

## 1. One event, three social settings

Imagine that a train was cancelled and you arrived late. To a friend you might say, **« Mon train a été supprimé, du coup je suis arrivé en retard. »** — My train was cancelled, so I arrived late. In an ordinary professional message: **« Mon train ayant été supprimé, je suis arrivé en retard. »** — As my train had been cancelled, I arrived late. In a formal request: **« L'annulation de mon train m'a empêché d'arriver à l'heure prévue. »** — The cancellation of my train prevented me from arriving at the scheduled time.

Follow the changes. The first version presents two events in speech order. *Du coup* is a frequent conversational consequence marker, but repeating it throughout an essay makes the relations sound undifferentiated. The second version turns the cause into a participial clause. Its subject, *mon train*, differs from the subject of *je suis arrivé*; this is an absolute construction with its own subject. The third makes *annulation* the grammatical subject and frames lateness as a consequence of a named cause. None is inherently more truthful. Each puts the event into a different social and informational frame.

Register is a set of choices, not an on/off switch. A colloquial word inside otherwise formal prose can be deliberate quotation. A respectful conversation can use short sentences. Begin by identifying audience, purpose, medium, and stakes. If you write to someone whose preferred style you do not know, clear neutral French is usually a more dependable starting point than ceremonious language. **« Pourriez-vous m'indiquer la marche à suivre ? »** is useful and natural; a chain of elaborate politeness formulas can conceal the actual request.

Practice by rewriting **« Je peux pas venir, j'ai un truc à régler »** twice. A neutral version is **« Je ne peux pas venir, car j'ai une démarche à effectuer. »** A professional version might be **« Un engagement personnel m'empêche d'assister à la réunion. »** Notice that *un truc* does not always mean *une démarche*. You must know what the speaker means before translating vagueness into precision. Do not invent a medical appointment merely because it sounds appropriate.

## 2. Spoken grammar and the written record

In ordinary conversation, the first part of **ne...pas** is often absent: **« Je sais pas »** for **« Je ne sais pas. »** You need to recognize that pattern when listening. In edited standard writing, keep *ne* unless you are representing speech or deliberately choosing an informal voice. This is a distinction between usage settings, not evidence that ordinary speakers have forgotten grammar.

Several sound reductions make familiar words difficult to identify. **« Il y a »** may be heard with a very reduced beginning. **« Je suis »** can be compressed in rapid speech. **« Tu as »** is often heard and informally written as **« t'as »**. These are listening targets before they are production targets. Start with a complete careful version, hear a naturally spoken version, then identify which sounds were reduced. Do not train by reading invented phonetic spellings as if every speaker used the same reductions.

The pronoun **on** deserves particular care. In **« On part demain »**, it may mean *we*. The verb remains singular: *on part*, not *on partons*. Adjectives and participles can reflect the people intended: two women saying **« On est arrivées »** can use feminine plural agreement in contexts where *on* stands for them. In a formal report, replacing every *nous* with *on* may create avoidable uncertainty about who performed an action. **« Nous avons interrogé trente personnes »** identifies the authors' action more directly than **« On a interrogé trente personnes. »**

Spoken French also tolerates structures that require punctuation or reformulation in writing. **« Le dossier, je l'ai envoyé hier »** is a normal dislocation. It is not the same as accidentally writing two unrelated subjects. In an email, **« J'ai envoyé le dossier hier »** may be enough. In an interview transcript, retaining the dislocation preserves the speaker's organization of attention.

For listening work, record a short neutral paragraph yourself, or use the site's French voice, and then compare it with an authentic conversational extract from a lawful source. Synthetic speech can model words but may not reproduce turn-taking, overlap, or regional reduction. Mark what you actually heard rather than treating a prediction as a transcription. Revisit the passage after seeing the text and listen again without the text.

## 3. Emphasis: choosing the answer to an implicit question

Consider **« Léa a envoyé le rapport hier. »** The whole sentence can answer *What happened?* To answer *Who sent it?*, say **« C'est Léa qui a envoyé le rapport. »** To answer *What did Léa send?*, say **« C'est le rapport que Léa a envoyé. »** To answer *When?*, say **« C'est hier que Léa a envoyé le rapport. »** The construction splits the sentence into an emphasized element and the clause that identifies its role.

Use **qui** when the highlighted element is the subject of the following verb. In **« C'est Léa qui a envoyé... »**, Léa performs the sending. Use **que** for an emphasized direct object or many adverbial elements. In **« C'est le rapport que Léa a envoyé »**, Léa is still the subject, and *le rapport* is what she sent. This choice follows grammar, not whether the emphasized item is a person: **« C'est Léa que j'ai appelée »** uses *que* because I called Léa.

With plural nouns, standard edited usage commonly has **« Ce sont les résultats qui comptent »**. You will also encounter *c'est* before a plural in conversation. Do not let that listening observation erase agreement in your own careful prose. First-person emphasis gives **« C'est moi qui ai envoyé le rapport »**: the relative subject represents *moi*, so *ai* is first person. **« C'est vous qui avez raison »** follows the same reasoning.

Another useful structure is **« Ce qui m'inquiète, c'est le délai »** — What worries me is the deadline. *Ce qui* is the subject of *inquiète*. Compare **« Ce que je crains, c'est un retard »** — What I fear is a delay. Here *je* is the subject of *crains*, and *ce que* supplies its object. **« Ce dont nous avons besoin, c'est de temps »** follows *avoir besoin de*. Build the underlying clause first: *nous avons besoin de temps*. Then preserve the preposition relationship.

Emphasis becomes weak when every sentence insists on itself. If your paragraph contains six successive **« C'est...que... »** sentences, restore some neutral sentences and reserve emphasis for a real contrast. Read the paragraph aloud and ask which question each emphasized structure answers. If you cannot name the contrast, the simpler sentence may communicate more clearly.

## 4. Dislocation, reference, and topic continuity

**« Ce projet, nous le terminerons en juin »** places *ce projet* first and resumes it with *le*. The initial noun phrase establishes the topic; the clause gives news about it. A right dislocation reverses the arrangement: **« Nous le terminerons en juin, ce projet. »** This often sounds conversational and can add clarification or emotional coloring. The structure is not a device for avoiding object-pronoun rules. The pronoun still has to match its role.

Compare **« Cette question, j'y ai réfléchi »** and **« Cette question, je l'ai étudiée. »** *Réfléchir à une question* requires an indirect relationship represented by *y*. *Étudier une question* takes a direct object represented by *la*, shortened to *l'* before a vowel. The second past participle agrees with its preceding direct object: *étudiée*. Starting with a topic does not determine the pronoun; the verb's construction does.

Cohesion also depends on avoiding ambiguous reference. **« Nadia a parlé à Claire après son entretien »** does not tell us whose interview is meant. Agreement cannot solve this because both women can be represented by *son*. Write **« après l'entretien de Nadia »** if that distinction matters. Similarly, **« La mairie a consulté l'association. Elle demande une réponse »** leaves *elle* unclear unless context strongly favors one institution. Repeating a noun can be more elegant than forcing a reader to guess.

Anaphora means referring back to something already introduced. Useful forms include *ce constat*, *cette démarche*, *ces résultats*, and *une telle décision*. They do more than avoid repetition: each labels the previous material. If you call a person's tentative suggestion **« cette décision »**, you have changed its status. **« Cette hypothèse »** acknowledges uncertainty; **« ce fait »** presents something as established. At C1, reference words can carry an argument quietly, so choose them with the same care as verbs.

Write a six-sentence paragraph about a proposed library renovation. Underline every pronoun and demonstrative. Draw an arrow from each to its intended antecedent. If two antecedents compete, revise. Then read only the first words of each sentence. A sequence such as *La bibliothèque... Ce bâtiment... Sa rénovation... Cette dépense...* shows a planned movement from object to action to cost. That movement helps a reader follow your reasoning without excessive signposting.

## 5. Nominalization: useful compression with a cost

Nominalization turns an action or property into a noun: *réduire → la réduction*, *décider → la décision*, *fiable → la fiabilité*. Compare **« La ville a réduit les tarifs, ce qui a augmenté la fréquentation »** with **« La réduction des tarifs a entraîné une hausse de la fréquentation. »** The second sentence packages the initial action into a noun phrase, allowing the writer to discuss its effect as a single topic.

Work through the transformation carefully. First identify the action, *réduire les tarifs*. Name it, *la réduction des tarifs*. Then decide whether to preserve the actor: *la réduction des tarifs par la ville*. Finally choose a relation, *a entraîné*. Do not assume the nominalized version automatically proves causation. If the evidence shows only simultaneous change, write **« a coïncidé avec une hausse »** or **« semble avoir contribué à une hausse »**. Grammar can package a claim; it cannot validate that claim.

Nominalization can hide responsibility. **« Une erreur a été commise lors de la transmission »** omits who made the mistake and what was transmitted. Sometimes the actor is unknown or irrelevant. In a troubleshooting report, **« Le service a transmis une version incomplète du fichier »** may be more useful. Choose opacity only when it serves a legitimate purpose, not because abstract nouns sound advanced.

French noun phrases can accumulate prepositions: **« l'évaluation de l'efficacité de la mise en œuvre du dispositif »**. This is grammatically possible but difficult to process. Unpack it as **« Nous avons évalué si le dispositif avait été mis en œuvre efficacement »** if that matches the intended meaning. Notice the potential ambiguity: evaluating whether implementation was effective differs from evaluating whether the policy itself works. Rewriting should expose the distinction rather than merely shorten the phrase.

Practice both directions. Transform **« Les habitants participent davantage parce que les réunions ont lieu le soir »** into **« La tenue des réunions en soirée favorise la participation des habitants »**. Then explain what changed: *favorise* suggests contribution, while the original *parce que* asserts a cause. A close translation might retain *s'explique par*. Next unpack **« Le report de l'ouverture a suscité l'inquiétude des commerçants »** as **« Les commerçants se sont inquiétés lorsque l'ouverture a été reportée »**. Both versions work, but each places a different element at the center of attention.

## 6. Negation and indefinites without accidental contradictions

Basic **ne...pas** denies a proposition. Other negatives specify what is absent: **« Personne n'a répondu »** — Nobody replied; **« Je n'ai rien reçu »** — I received nothing; **« Il ne vient jamais »** — He never comes; **« Nous n'avons plus de temps »** — We have no time left. In these standard forms, *pas* is not added automatically. **« Je n'ai pas rien reçu »** can express a marked double negation, but it does not mean the ordinary *I received nothing*.

Position follows function and tense. **« Je n'ai rencontré personne »** places *personne* after the participle. **« Je n'ai rien compris »** places *rien* before it. **« Personne n'a rien dit »** is a normal combination meaning nobody said anything; the negatives work together rather than cancelling each other into a positive statement. **« Aucun participant n'a répondu »** uses *aucun* with a noun and usually a singular verb here. Use context to distinguish absence from low quantity: *peu de participants* means some participants, but few.

**Ne...que** restricts rather than negates: **« Je n'ai lu que l'introduction »** means I read only the introduction. The restriction's position matters. **« Seule Léa a lu le rapport »** means Léa alone read it. **« Léa a seulement lu le rapport »** may contrast reading with analyzing or acting on it. **« Léa n'a lu que le rapport »** restricts what she read. Translate *only* by identifying its scope before choosing a French position.

Indefinites require similar care. **« Chaque étudiant a reçu un dossier »** distributes one dossier to each student. **« Tous les étudiants ont reçu un dossier »** makes a claim about the whole group. **« Quelques étudiants »** means some students; **« certains étudiants »** often suggests a selected or unspecified subset. **« Quelque étudiant »** is not the normal singular equivalent of *some student* in everyday prose. Learn useful constructions as chunks rather than mechanically matching English words.

A particularly useful distinction is **« tout autre »**. **« Une tout autre solution »** means an entirely different solution; *tout* functions adverbially. **« Toute autre solution serait trop coûteuse »** means any other solution would be too costly; *toute* determines *solution*. Test a paraphrase before editing agreement. At this level, a small word can reorganize the whole claim.

## 7. Building vocabulary through families, constructions, and collocations

Knowing *fiable* helps with *la fiabilité*, but a word family is not a promise of identical meaning. *Sensible* usually means sensitive, not sensible; *sensé* can mean sensible or reasonable. *Actuellement* means currently; *éventuellement* means possibly or if the opportunity arises, not usually eventually. **« Nous pourrons éventuellement reporter la réunion »** proposes a possibility. **« Nous reporterons finalement la réunion »** reports an eventual outcome after reconsideration.

Store vocabulary with its construction. Write **« contribuer à quelque chose »**, **« dépendre de quelque chose »**, **« disposer de ressources »**, and **« remédier à un problème »**. An isolated verb gives you meaning without the grammatical connection needed to use it. Build two examples for a new verb: one concrete and one abstract. **« Le couvercle résiste à la chaleur »** and **« Cette hypothèse résiste à la critique »** show a reusable relationship without pretending that every metaphor is predictable.

Collocations are combinations preferred by usage. French commonly has **« prendre une décision »**, **« mener une enquête »**, **« tirer une conclusion »**, and **« porter atteinte à un droit »**. Translating each word separately may produce understandable but unnatural phrases. Learn the noun with its usual action and one alternative when meaning changes: *prendre une décision* is making a decision; *revenir sur une décision* is reconsidering or reversing it; *appliquer une décision* is implementing it.

Idioms need register and situation notes. **« Mettre la main à la pâte »** means to help with practical work, not necessarily cooking. **« Avoir du pain sur la planche »** means having plenty of work ahead. They can enliven ordinary conversation, but placing three food idioms in a formal complaint may sound forced. First paraphrase an idiom in neutral French. If you cannot explain the situation in plain language, memorizing the colorful version has not yet produced usable knowledge.

For each reading session, collect at most a manageable set of high-value items. Record headword, gender if relevant, construction, meaning in that passage, a sentence you understand, and a contrasting near-synonym. Review recognition and production separately. Recognizing *néanmoins* in an article does not mean you can place it naturally in an oral answer. This chapter's bank is a curated starting set; independent reading must continue to expand your vocabulary beyond any finite course list.

## 8. Literary past tenses as a reading system

In much written narrative, **passé simple** performs a role that ordinary conversation commonly assigns to **passé composé**: it advances the chain of completed events. **« Elle ouvrit la fenêtre, aperçut un homme et recula »** — She opened the window, caught sight of a man, and stepped back. The imperfect supplies background: **« Il pleuvait et la rue était vide »**. First identify the narrative function; then resolve the unfamiliar form.

Common endings help. Regular *-er* verbs give *je parlai, tu parlas, il parla, nous parlâmes, vous parlâtes, ils parlèrent*. Many other verbs follow patterns in *-is* or *-us*: *il finit, il prit, il voulut, il eut*. *Être* gives *il fut*; *venir* gives *il vint*. Some forms coincide with another tense: **« il finit »** can be present or passé simple. The surrounding narrative establishes the reading. Do not pronounce a written ending you have not checked merely by analogy with present-tense spelling.

The **passé antérieur** combines the passé simple of an auxiliary with a past participle: **« Quand elle eut terminé, elle sortit »** — When she had finished, she went out. The finishing is anterior to the event that follows. With an *être* verb: **« Dès qu'ils furent arrivés, la discussion commença. »** In an oral account, a natural contemporary counterpart is **« Après avoir terminé, elle est sortie »**. Some varieties also use compound forms such as *quand elle a eu fini*; these are not required for the course's neutral production target. Do not mechanically substitute one auxiliary form everywhere; natural contemporary phrasing depends on context.

The practical goal is recognition, not theatrical conversation. In a novel, underline verbs that move the plot and circle imperfect verbs that build the scene. Convert five lines into an oral account using appropriate contemporary tenses. You are translating narrative perspective as well as morphology. If every verb becomes passé composé, you may lose background or habitual meaning. If every verb becomes imperfect, events may stop advancing.

Write a tiny narrative: **« La porte était entrouverte. Amine frappa, attendit quelques secondes, puis entra. Sur la table reposait une lettre. »** Explain each choice: *était* establishes a state, *frappa/attendit/entra* advance successive events, and *reposait* introduces an object as part of the scene. This functional explanation is more valuable than reciting an ending table without understanding the story.

## 9. Older subjunctive forms: recognizing time and style

Modern standard French generally uses the present or past subjunctive after a past reporting verb when a subjunctive is required: **« Elle souhaitait qu'il vienne »** and **« Elle regrettait qu'il soit parti »**. Literary writing may instead use the imperfect or pluperfect subjunctive. **« Elle souhaitait qu'il vînt »** is a literary counterpart of the first sentence. **« Elle regrettait qu'il fût parti »** is a literary counterpart of the second.

The point is not that an event suddenly becomes imperfect in aspect because its subjunctive is called *imparfait*. The form participates in an older or literary sequence of tenses. Identify the main verb's meaning, whether the subordinate event is simultaneous, later, or already completed, and whether the passage is using a literary register. Then paraphrase into current neutral French before translating into English.

Several recognizable third-person singular forms are valuable: *qu'il fût* from *être*, *qu'il eût* from *avoir*, *qu'il vînt* from *venir*, *qu'il pût* from *pouvoir*, and *qu'il fît* from *faire*. The circumflex can distinguish these from visually similar passé simple forms: **« il fut »** versus **« qu'il fût »**. Context still matters; do not decide an entire sentence from one accent without examining the conjunction and governing expression.

Consider **« Bien qu'il eût compris le danger, il poursuivit sa route »**. *Bien que* marks concession and governs the subjunctive. *Eût compris* indicates prior understanding; *poursuivit* advances the narrative. A contemporary paraphrase is **« Bien qu'il ait compris le danger, il a poursuivi sa route »**. English can say *Although he had understood the danger, he continued on his way*. That translation expresses anteriority directly, even though English does not mirror the French subjunctive morphology.

Do not imitate these forms in an everyday email to display proficiency. Use them when studying literature, translating a deliberately historical voice, or analyzing an author's style. For production, dependable control of current constructions serves a broader range of real situations. Your reading notebook can have a separate recognition column so rare forms enrich comprehension without displacing the language you need frequently.

## 10. Original reading: a place, a memory, a proposal

Read the following original passage once for its main movement, then again for reference and register.

> Lorsque Mina revint dans la petite ville où elle avait grandi, la gare lui parut plus basse qu'autrefois. Ce n'était pas le bâtiment qui avait changé, mais la place qu'il occupait dans son souvenir. Enfant, elle y voyait une frontière : au-delà des rails commençait un monde auquel elle ne connaissait pas encore de nom.
>
> La mairie envisageait désormais de transformer l'ancienne salle d'attente en atelier partagé. Certains habitants y voyaient une dépense inutile ; d'autres estimaient qu'un lieu vacant devait servir à quelque chose. Mina, elle, hésitait. Elle tenait à la mémoire du lieu sans vouloir en faire un musée immobile. Ce qu'elle redoutait, ce n'était pas le changement, c'était l'effacement.
>
> Au cours de la réunion, elle proposa que les futurs usagers recueillent les récits des voyageurs. On pourrait les écouter en travaillant, ou les lire près des fenêtres donnant sur les voies. Ainsi, la transformation ne signifierait pas la disparition du passé. Encore faudrait-il que l'association chargée du projet dispose du temps nécessaire pour le mener à bien.

The first paragraph says that when Mina returned, the station seemed lower than before. The building had not changed; its place in her memory had. As a child she had seen it as a frontier, beyond which began a world she could not yet name. The second presents a current plan to convert the former waiting room into a shared workshop. Residents disagree. Mina wants to preserve memory without freezing the place. The third describes her proposal to collect travelers' stories, with a final reservation about the association's time and resources.

Trace the tense sequence. *Revint* and *parut* are passé simple events or perceptions in the narrative frame. *Avait grandi* and *avait changé* refer to an earlier layer. *Envisageait*, *voyaient*, and *estimait* describe the situation and attitudes. *Proposa* moves the meeting forward. The subordinate **« que les futurs usagers recueillent »** uses the subjunctive after a proposal; it does not report that collection has already occurred.

Now trace cohesion. *Y* in *y voyait* refers to the station as a meaningful place; *y voyaient une dépense* means saw in the proposal an expense. *Le lieu* and *le passé* are related but not interchangeable. **« Ce qu'elle redoutait... »** presents the important distinction between change and erasure. **« Encore faudrait-il que... »** introduces a necessary condition with a reserved, somewhat formal tone: the plan sounds attractive, but a practical requirement remains.

Translate the final sentence carefully: *Still, the association responsible for the project would need to have enough time to carry it through.* A literal *Again it would be necessary* would miss the reservation. Translation requires the discourse function, not just dictionary substitutions. For speaking, summarize Mina's position in ninety seconds without copying the passage. Preserve both her support for transformation and her concern about memory.

## 11. Guided transformations with reasoned answers

Translate into French: (1) It is the timetable that worries us. (2) I have only read the first chapter. (3) Nobody told us anything. (4) This proposal, we have already discussed it. (5) Once she had closed the door, she left. Use a literary narrative for the last item.

Possible answers are: **« C'est le calendrier qui nous inquiète. »** The emphasized noun is the subject of *inquiète*, hence *qui*. **« Je n'ai lu que le premier chapitre. »** The restriction applies to the object. **« Personne ne nous a rien dit. »** *Personne* is the subject, *nous* the indirect object, and *rien* the direct object. **« Cette proposition, nous en avons déjà discuté. »** This answer uses *discuter de cette proposition*; **« nous l'avons déjà discutée »** can correspond to transitive *discuter une proposition*, but constructions and register differ, so the intended verb pattern must be stated. **« Quand elle eut fermé la porte, elle partit. »** The closing precedes departure in a literary past sequence.

Translate into English: (1) **« Ce qu'il a besoin, c'est de méthode. »** is intentionally faulty. Repair it before translating. (2) **« Le dossier, elle l'a relu attentivement. »** (3) **« Une tout autre explication est possible. »** (4) **« Toute autre réponse serait prématurée. »** (5) **« Bien qu'il fût tard, ils poursuivirent la discussion. »**

For item one, use **« Ce dont il a besoin, c'est de méthode »** — What he needs is a methodical approach. The underlying construction is *avoir besoin de quelque chose*. The relative form must retain that relationship through *dont*, rather than using direct-object *que*. Another natural answer is **« Il a besoin d'une méthode »** if the intended meaning is a particular method. That noun choice changes the emphasis slightly: *de méthode* can refer to systematic working habits, while *d'une méthode* introduces a method. Item two means *The file: she reread it carefully*, usually naturalized as *She reread the file carefully*. Item three means an entirely different explanation is possible. Item four means any other answer would be premature. Item five means although it was late, they continued the discussion; *fût* marks literary subjunctive style.

Revision exercise: change **« Suite à l'annulation de la réunion, on a fait une discussion sur le problème et pris une décision de report »** into a clear neutral sentence. One answer is **« Après l'annulation de la réunion, nous avons discuté du problème et décidé de reporter le projet. »** You must identify what was postponed; if the source does not say, keep the object open or ask for it. Clarity is not permission to invent missing facts.

# Chapter 09 — Academic and professional French

**Stage:** C1 development. **Study allocation:** 100 hours in ten ten-hour units. **Prerequisites:** tense and mood control, complex clauses, argument, register, and reference from Chapters 01–08. You will combine several sources, distinguish fact from inference, write useful professional documents, and sustain an appropriately qualified position. C1 is a pattern of performance across tasks, not a list of impressive expressions.

The recurring situation is a proposal to extend a community learning center's opening hours. You receive figures, opinions, constraints, and incomplete evidence. Your job is to explain what can be concluded, what remains uncertain, and what someone should do next. The examples are fictional and the figures are deliberately small enough to inspect. They teach language and reasoning; they are not policy research.

## 1. A claim has a source, a scope, and a strength

Start with **« Le centre ferme trop tôt »** — The center closes too early. Who says this, for whom is closing time too early, and compared with what need? A resident's opinion is not the same kind of evidence as attendance records. A stronger professional account might be **« Selon les personnes interrogées en soirée, l'horaire actuel limite l'accès aux ateliers »** — According to the people interviewed in the evening, the present timetable limits access to workshops.

Now examine what the qualification does. *Selon* attributes the statement. *Les personnes interrogées en soirée* identifies the group. *Limite l'accès* describes an effect without claiming that access is impossible. The sentence does not imply that everyone in the neighborhood agrees. If you remove the qualifier and write **« Les habitants ne peuvent pas accéder aux ateliers »**, you have changed a bounded report into a general claim.

French has many tools for expressing strength. **« Les données montrent que... »** presents a result as supported by data. **« Les données suggèrent que... »** is more tentative. **« Il est possible que... »** introduces a possibility and takes the subjunctive in this construction: **« Il est possible que cet horaire convienne mieux aux salariés. »** **« Il semble que... »** often allows different mood choices according to how the proposition is presented; for dependable formal production when expressing uncertainty, **« Il semble que l'effet soit limité »** is a useful pattern. Do not treat mood as a mechanical percentage scale of certainty.

Distinguish *probable* from *possible*, *nécessaire* from *souhaitable*, and *efficace* from *efficient*. Something can achieve its aim without doing so at low cost. **« Cette mesure est souhaitable »** expresses a judgment about desirability; **« Cette mesure est nécessaire »** claims it is required. If your evidence supports only preference, selecting the stronger adjective overstates the conclusion.

Exercise: qualify **« Les cours du soir améliorent les résultats »** in three ways. A report of association is **« Dans cet échantillon, la participation aux cours du soir est associée à de meilleurs résultats. »** A cautious explanation is **« Les cours du soir pourraient contribuer à cette amélioration. »** An attributed opinion is **« D'après plusieurs participants, les cours du soir les aident à progresser. »** Each is grammatically accurate, but they express different evidence. Choose the one the source actually supports.

## 2. Reporting speech without laundering an opinion into fact

Reporting verbs classify what someone is doing. **« La directrice indique que les locaux sont disponibles »** reports information. **« Elle estime que l'extension serait utile »** reports a judgment. **« Elle affirme que les coûts resteront stables »** presents an assertion, not independent verification. **« Elle reconnaît que le budget est limité »** adds the idea of acknowledgment. Avoid using *démontrer* when a source merely asserts something; the verb would give the argument a strength it has not earned.

Indirect speech also changes viewpoint. Direct speech on Monday: **« Nous ouvrirons demain »**. A later narrative can say **« La directrice a annoncé lundi que le centre ouvrirait le lendemain »**. *Demain* becomes *le lendemain* because the time anchor moved. The future becomes conditional as future viewed from a past reporting point. If you write an immediate news summary while tomorrow is still the relevant day, **« Elle a annoncé que le centre ouvrira demain »** may be appropriate. Sequence of tenses serves time and viewpoint, not arbitrary word replacement.

For a prior event: **« Elle explique qu'elle a consulté l'équipe »**. From a past narrative frame: **« Elle a expliqué qu'elle avait consulté l'équipe »**. The plus-que-parfait locates the consultation before the explanation. For a continuing truth, a present may be retained when the writer treats it as still valid: **« Le responsable a rappelé que le bâtiment est accessible aux personnes à mobilité réduite. »** If you use an imperfect, the sentence may stay inside the historical frame without necessarily declaring the fact false today.

Questions require reconstruction. **« Pourquoi le centre ferme-t-il à dix-huit heures ? »** becomes **« Les habitants demandent pourquoi le centre ferme à dix-huit heures. »** Indirect questions do not retain direct-question inversion. A yes/no question becomes *si*: **« Ils demandent si une ouverture tardive est possible. »** Distinguish this *si* from a hypothetical condition. You can say **« Je me demande s'il viendra »** because this is an indirect question about future arrival; the familiar rule excluding future after conditional *si* does not apply to every appearance of the word.

Practice by interviewing a partner for five minutes. Write six reported statements, two reported questions, and one sentence that explicitly marks disagreement between speakers. Ask the partner to verify whether your account preserves their degree of certainty. That verification is a language task: a grammatically elegant summary can still misrepresent what someone said.

## 3. Synthesis is organized by questions, not by documents

A summary condenses one source. A synthesis combines relevant information from several sources around a common problem. Suppose source A describes increased demand for evening workshops, source B lists staffing costs, and source C gives residents' concerns about noise. Writing one paragraph beginning *Le document A dit...*, then one for B, then one for C is easy, but it often leaves the reader to perform the actual comparison.

Begin with a question: **« À quelles conditions une ouverture en soirée serait-elle utile et réalisable ? »** — Under what conditions would evening opening be useful and feasible? Create a small evidence table. One row concerns demand, another staffing, another neighborhood impact. Place relevant material from every source into those rows. This reveals agreement, tension, and gaps before you write sentences.

Use a neutral introductory frame: **« Les documents portent sur l'élargissement des horaires d'un centre de quartier. Ils font apparaître une demande réelle, tout en soulignant des contraintes de personnel et de voisinage. »** The phrase *une demande réelle* needs support in the fictional sources. If only two comments mention demand, use *une demande exprimée par certains usagers*. Then organize the body by the shared questions: who would benefit, what resources would be needed, and which safeguards might address concerns.

Link sources precisely. **« Les deux enquêtes convergent sur... »** means the two surveys converge on something. **« En revanche, elles divergent quant à... »** identifies disagreement about a particular point. **« Le second document apporte un éclairage complémentaire »** says it adds another angle. Complementary does not mean contradictory. **« Cette estimation doit être rapprochée de... »** invites comparison without claiming identity. Avoid *contrairement* when sources simply discuss different dimensions.

In a neutral synthesis, do not smuggle in your opinion through adjectives. **« Le projet courageux de la mairie se heurte aux plaintes exagérées des voisins »** sides with one group. A neutral version is **« Le projet de la mairie suscite des réserves chez certains riverains »**. If your task later asks for a recommendation, separate that recommendation from the source account so the reader can see where evidence ends and judgment begins.

Keep a traceability note while drafting: mark the source behind each factual sentence, even if the final task does not use formal citation. If you cannot identify a source for a number, remove it or label it as an assumption. A synthesis is successful when the new organization helps the reader understand the relationship among sources without changing what any source actually says.

## 4. Original source pack and a worked synthesis

The following short documents are original practice material. They describe a fictional center and are not findings about a real community.

**Document A — Usager survey.** **« Sur les soixante personnes ayant répondu au questionnaire du centre, trente-six souhaitent une ouverture après dix-huit heures au moins un jour par semaine. Vingt-quatre de ces trente-six personnes déclarent travailler à temps plein. Le questionnaire a été proposé à l'accueil pendant deux semaines. Il ne permet donc pas de connaître directement l'avis des personnes qui ne fréquentent jamais le centre. »**

Translation: Of sixty questionnaire respondents, thirty-six want opening after six at least once weekly; twenty-four of those thirty-six report full-time employment. The questionnaire was offered at reception for two weeks, so it does not directly establish the opinions of people who never visit the center. The fraction thirty-six out of sixty is sixty percent of respondents, not sixty percent of all residents. Keep the denominator attached to the claim.

**Document B — Staffing note.** **« Une soirée supplémentaire nécessiterait la présence de deux agents pendant trois heures. Le coût estimé est de cent quatre-vingts euros par soirée, hors éventuels frais de sécurité. L'équipe recommande une expérimentation de huit semaines avant toute modification durable. Elle précise que les heures correspondantes ne peuvent pas être ajoutées sans réorganiser le planning. »**

Translation: One additional evening would require two staff members for three hours. The estimated cost is 180 euros per evening, excluding possible security costs. The team recommends an eight-week trial before a lasting change and says the hours require schedule reorganization. Eight evenings at this estimate give 1,440 euros before excluded costs. Do not convert an estimate into a guaranteed final budget.

**Document C — Neighborhood association message.** **« L'association n'est pas opposée à une ouverture tardive. Elle souhaite toutefois que les activités bruyantes se terminent avant vingt heures et qu'un bilan soit communiqué aux riverains. Selon plusieurs membres, les départs groupés devant l'entrée principale constituent une gêne plus importante que les activités elles-mêmes. »**

Translation: The association does not oppose late opening. It wants noisy activities to end before eight and a review to be shared with neighbors. Several members say groups leaving through the main entrance are more bothersome than the activities. **« N'est pas opposée »** is not equivalent to enthusiastic support. **« Selon plusieurs membres »** must remain an attributed assessment.

A possible synthesis begins: **« L'élargissement des horaires répond à une demande exprimée par une majorité des répondants, notamment parmi les personnes qui travaillent à temps plein. La portée de l'enquête reste néanmoins limitée, puisqu'elle a été menée à l'accueil du centre. La faisabilité du projet dépendrait surtout de la réorganisation du travail et de la maîtrise des nuisances. »** Continue: **« L'équipe propose une expérimentation de huit semaines, dont le coût de personnel est estimé à 1 440 euros, hors sécurité. De son côté, l'association de quartier demande des horaires adaptés aux activités bruyantes et un bilan partagé. Une phase d'essai permettrait ainsi d'évaluer simultanément la fréquentation, les besoins de personnel et les effets sur le voisinage. »**

The final sentence is a reasoned synthesis of the proposed trial, not a source quotation. It stays within the information provided. If a task demands strictly source-bound reporting, phrase it as the trial's potential purpose rather than claiming it has already proved effective. Compare your version against the three documents and highlight any detail you accidentally strengthened or omitted.

## 5. Argument, counterargument, and a recommendation that survives scrutiny

An argument connects a claim to reasons. A recommendation adds an action under specified conditions. **« Il faut ouvrir plus tard parce que c'est mieux »** repeats a preference without explaining who benefits or what tradeoff is acceptable. A stronger sentence is **« Une ouverture hebdomadaire jusqu'à vingt heures permettrait à certains salariés de participer aux ateliers, à condition que le planning soit réorganisé sans réduire les services essentiels de la journée. »** It identifies beneficiaries, expected benefit, and a constraint.

Build paragraphs in four movements: claim, reason or evidence, interpretation, and consequence. For example: **« Une expérimentation limitée paraît préférable à une modification immédiate et définitive. En effet, l'enquête porte uniquement sur les personnes déjà présentes au centre. Elle établit une demande parmi les répondants, mais ne permet pas de prévoir avec certitude la fréquentation en soirée. Un essai de huit semaines fournirait donc des données plus directement liées au nouvel horaire. »** Each sentence does a different job. The paragraph does not need a connector at the start of every clause.

Present the strongest reasonable objection. **« On pourrait objecter que le coût reste élevé pour un nombre encore incertain d'usagers »** is a plausible concern. Reply by acknowledging the true part: **« Cette réserve est fondée : le coût par participant dépendra de la fréquentation réelle. C'est pourquoi le bilan devrait fixer un seuil de participation et examiner d'autres modalités, telles qu'une ouverture sur inscription. »** Do not answer a cost objection by merely repeating that education is important. Address the relationship the objection raises.

Use concession accurately. **« Certes, la mesure entraîne une dépense supplémentaire ; elle peut toutefois répondre à un besoin aujourd'hui mal couvert. »** *Certes* signals an admitted point, and *toutefois* introduces the balancing consideration. **« Même si la demande est réelle, les moyens restent limités »** accepts a premise hypothetically or concessively without requiring the subjunctive. **« Bien que la demande soit réelle... »** takes the subjunctive. The two constructions can communicate similar arguments while following different grammatical patterns.

End with an operational recommendation. Specify action, responsible actor if known, time frame, and review criterion. **« Je recommande que la direction organise une soirée d'essai par semaine pendant huit semaines et publie un bilan portant sur la fréquentation, les coûts et les remarques des riverains. »** *Recommander que* governs the subjunctive here. If responsibility has not been assigned, write **« Il conviendrait de désigner une personne chargée du bilan »** rather than pretending the association already agreed to perform it.

## 6. Professional email: make the next action easy

A useful professional email answers why you are writing, what the reader needs to know, and what response you request. A meaningful subject helps: **« Objet : proposition d'expérimentation des horaires du centre »**. Begin with the relevant context, not a long explanation of your feelings about writing. **« À la suite de notre échange du 12 novembre, je vous transmets une proposition d'ouverture en soirée. »** This identifies the previous exchange and the attachment's role.

An original model follows:

> Madame,
>
> À la suite de notre réunion, je vous adresse une proposition d'expérimentation des horaires du centre. Elle prévoit une ouverture jusqu'à vingt heures un soir par semaine pendant huit semaines.
>
> Le coût de personnel est estimé à 1 440 euros pour l'ensemble de la période, hors éventuels frais de sécurité. Avant de confirmer le dispositif, il conviendrait de vérifier les possibilités de réorganisation du planning et de consulter les riverains sur les modalités de sortie.
>
> Pourriez-vous m'indiquer si cette proposition peut être examinée lors de la prochaine réunion de direction ? Je reste disponible pour préciser les critères du bilan.
>
> Cordialement,
>
> Samira Laurent

The model is formal enough for an unfamiliar professional contact without being ceremonial. *Je vous adresse* means I am sending you; *une adresse* as a noun has a different role. *Il conviendrait de* proposes a prudent next step. The question requests a specific response: whether the proposal can be considered at the next meeting. It does not ask the reader vaguely to *give feedback* without indicating the decision needed.

For disagreement, distinguish firmness from aggression. **« Je ne peux pas confirmer cette estimation en l'état, car les frais de sécurité n'y figurent pas »** states a limitation and its reason. **« Votre estimation est absurde »** attacks the work without helping repair it. If you need urgency, give the real deadline and consequence: **« Une réponse avant vendredi nous permettrait de préparer le planning de décembre. »** Do not fabricate urgency with *très urgent* in every subject line.

Learn attachment and follow-up language: **« Vous trouverez le tableau en pièce jointe »**, **« Je vous remercie pour votre retour »**, **« Sauf erreur de ma part, je n'ai pas encore reçu la version révisée »**. The last phrase softens a reminder by allowing that you may have missed something; it is not a substitute for checking your records. After writing, remove any sentence that does not help the recipient understand, decide, or act.

## 7. Reports, minutes, and the difference between a decision and a discussion

A report can analyze; meeting minutes primarily record. Their purposes overlap, but they are not identical. **« Les participants ont évoqué une ouverture le jeudi »** records a discussion. **« Les participants ont décidé d'ouvrir le jeudi »** records a decision. **« La direction examinera la possibilité d'une ouverture le jeudi »** records a future action. Confusing these forms can create practical commitments that nobody made.

Use explicit headings in a short professional report: context, observations, analysis, recommendations, and next steps. Under observations, preserve data and attribution. Under analysis, explain what follows and what does not. Under recommendations, state your judgment with conditions. A short table can be more effective than a paragraph listing ten dates or costs, but the table still needs clear units and definitions.

A minute entry might read: **« Horaires : l'équipe propose une expérimentation de huit semaines. La direction demandera une estimation complémentaire des frais de sécurité avant le 20 novembre. Aucune décision définitive n'a été prise concernant le jour d'ouverture. »** The final sentence prevents a proposal from being mistaken for an agreed schedule. If the responsible actor or date is unknown, mark it as to be assigned in your working notes and resolve it before issuing final minutes; do not publish a fabricated certainty.

Passive voice can help when the result matters more than the actor: **« Les réponses ont été classées par thème »**. An active version is more useful when accountability matters: **« L'équipe d'accueil a classé les réponses par thème »**. With a passive compound tense, agreement follows the subject: *les réponses ont été classées*. With active *avoir*, **« L'équipe a classé les réponses »** has no agreement with a following object; **« Les réponses que l'équipe a classées »** has a preceding direct object and agreement.

Professional nouns have constructions worth learning: *un compte rendu de réunion*, *un rapport sur la fréquentation*, *une réponse à une demande*, *un engagement envers les usagers*, *une hausse de la fréquentation*. The preposition often expresses the relationship more precisely than an English noun sequence. Translate *a community center staffing cost estimate* by rebuilding that relationship, for example **« une estimation des coûts de personnel du centre de quartier »**, then check that *du centre* refers to the intended noun.

## 8. Complex time, mood, and conditions in a real recommendation

Advanced sentences are useful when several events depend on one another. **« Si le financement était confirmé, nous pourrions lancer le dispositif en janvier »** sets a hypothetical present or future condition. **« Si le financement avait été confirmé plus tôt, nous aurions pu lancer le dispositif en septembre »** describes an unreal past. A mixed condition is possible: **« Si nous avions obtenu les fonds en septembre, le centre serait déjà ouvert le soir »**. The past condition explains a present consequence.

Do not use conditional morphology merely because the English sentence contains *would*. After conditional *si*, **« si nous aurions »** is not the standard pattern for the intended hypothetical condition. But **« Je voudrais savoir si nous aurions assez de personnel dans ce cas »** contains an indirect question embedded in a larger hypothetical scenario; the syntax is different. Identify what *si* is doing before applying a slogan about tense.

Purpose and prior conditions require careful time relationships. **« Nous devons réorganiser le planning pour que l'équipe puisse assurer la permanence »** uses the subjunctive after *pour que*. **« Une fois que le planning aura été validé, nous informerons les usagers »** uses the future anterior for a future completed step before another future action. **« Avant que le planning soit validé, évitons d'annoncer une date définitive »** uses the subjunctive after *avant que*. An optional *ne* can appear in formal usage after *avant que* without adding negation; do not confuse it with *ne...pas*.

For a completed prerequisite with an infinitive: **« Après avoir consulté les riverains, la direction publiera sa décision »**. The understood subject of *avoir consulté* is *la direction*. **« Après avoir consulté les riverains, la décision sera publiée »** leaves the decision as the apparent subject of the introductory infinitive, producing an illogical attachment. Repair it with an active main clause or a subordinate clause: **« Après que la direction aura consulté les riverains, la décision sera publiée. »**

The construction **« après que »** takes the indicative in standard careful usage because it presents the event as accomplished or treated as such in the time relation. You will hear subjunctive forms in actual usage; recognize them without letting exposure erase your edited production target. In an exam or formal report, use a well-controlled alternative if the sequence feels uncertain: **« Une fois la consultation terminée, la direction publiera sa décision. »** Clarity can be achieved by choosing a construction you command fully.

## 9. Reading disciplinary language without pretending to be a specialist

C1 includes handling complex material in familiar areas and developing strategies for unfamiliar domains. It does not mean knowing every legal, medical, engineering, or philosophical term. When a text contains *la portée*, determine whether it means scope, range, significance, or a literal bearing in that context. When it contains *un dispositif*, ask whether it refers to a mechanism, an arrangement, or a policy scheme. Broad words often become precise through their surrounding nouns and verbs.

Build a domain notebook around relationships. For economics, distinguish *le chiffre d'affaires* from *le bénéfice*: revenue is not profit. For research, distinguish *une hypothèse*, *une méthode*, *un résultat*, and *une interprétation*. For public policy, distinguish *une mesure*, *sa mise en œuvre*, and *son évaluation*. You can understand the structure of an argument even when some technical nouns need lookup. First identify what is being measured or claimed, then fill the lexical gaps.

Watch qualifiers in quantitative prose: **« environ »**, **« près de »**, **« au moins »**, **« au plus »**, **« respectivement »**, **« dans l'ensemble »**, and **« toutes choses égales par ailleurs »**. The last phrase signals a comparison holding other factors constant conceptually or analytically; it is not a magic guarantee that they actually were controlled. If a text says **« au moins trente personnes »**, translating it as *about thirty people* removes the lower-bound meaning.

Learn to paraphrase a definition. **« La fréquentation désigne ici le nombre de visites, et non le nombre de personnes distinctes »** tells you what the measure counts. One person visiting three times contributes three visits. If you summarize that as three people, the grammar may be perfect while the information is wrong. At this level, language comprehension includes respecting definitions, scope, and denominators.

For an unfamiliar specialist text, use a three-pass method. First read titles and opening sentences to locate the question. Next read for the main relationships, marking a small number of terms essential to those relationships. Finally verify those terms in a reliable domain source and reread the decisive paragraphs. Do not interrupt every sentence for a dictionary search; equally, do not invent a meaning because a word resembles English. Write a plain-language account, then compare it against the original to find what your paraphrase omitted or strengthened.

## 10. Francophone variation and respectful adaptation

French is used in many communities, and variation occurs within each of them. Accent, vocabulary, politeness routines, and administrative terminology can differ by region, generation, profession, and situation. A useful learner asks **« Quel terme emploie-t-on dans ce contexte ? »** rather than ranking every unfamiliar expression as incorrect. At the same time, learning a local expression does not make it suitable in every setting.

Some differences are easy to observe. A mobile phone may be called *un portable* in many French contexts and *un cellulaire* in many Canadian contexts. *Septante* and *nonante* are used in some francophone regions where other speakers use *soixante-dix* and *quatre-vingt-dix*. These examples are not complete maps of regional usage; check the audience and locality rather than assigning one form to everyone in a country. Professional terminology can differ for legal or institutional reasons, not just preference.

Pronunciation variation affects listening. Do not define comprehensibility as copying a single prestige accent. Train your ear with a range of speakers and genres, beginning with clear recordings and adding unscripted exchanges. Keep a note of a sound pattern you hear, but avoid caricaturing it in your own speech. Your production goal is clear, consistent, socially appropriate French; adaptation can include recognizing local words without imitating every phonetic feature.

Politeness also depends on relationship. *Tu* can signal familiarity, solidarity, or a local institutional norm; *vous* can mark respect, distance, or plural reference. If someone invites you to use *tu*, the invitation changes the relationship, but you still need appropriate professional content. When uncertain, **« Préférez-vous que l'on se tutoie ? »** asks about preference respectfully. Do not infer an entire culture's attitude from one workplace exchange.

For this unit, compare two lawful public recordings from different francophone settings on the same broad topic. Note three shared structures and three differences you can actually document. Separate accent, vocabulary, genre, and speaker-specific style. A newsreader and a street interview differ in more than geography. Finish by writing a neutral summary that both audiences could understand, then identify two words you would adapt for a particular local recipient after checking a suitable reference.

## 11. Guided translation and editing with answers

Translate into French: (1) According to the respondents, the timetable is too limited. (2) The figures suggest that demand may increase. (3) If the team had been consulted, the error could have been avoided. (4) Once the budget has been approved, we will publish the timetable. (5) She asks whether the center will be open tomorrow. State the reporting time for the last item.

Possible answers: **« Selon les répondants, les horaires sont trop restreints. »** Plural *les horaires* naturally refers to opening hours. **« Les chiffres laissent penser que la demande pourrait augmenter. »** This keeps a cautious interpretation. **« Si l'équipe avait été consultée, l'erreur aurait pu être évitée. »** The feminine singular *consultée* agrees with *l'équipe* in the passive, and *évitée* agrees with *l'erreur*. **« Une fois que le budget aura été approuvé, nous publierons les horaires. »** Future anterior marks prior completion in the future. **« Elle demande si le centre sera ouvert demain »** works if the reporting present and *tomorrow* share the current time anchor.

Translate into English: **« Les résultats doivent être interprétés avec prudence, dans la mesure où l'échantillon ne comprend que les usagers actuels. »** A suitable answer is *The results should be interpreted cautiously, since the sample includes only current users.* Here *dans la mesure où* supplies the reason or limiting condition; literal *in the measure where* is not English. **« Cette réserve ne remet pas en cause l'intérêt du projet »** means *This reservation does not call the value of the project into question.* It does not mean the project has no risk.

Repair **« La directrice demande quand est-ce que nous pourrons commencer »** in formal indirect speech. Use **« La directrice demande quand nous pourrons commencer. »** Repair **« Après avoir étudié les réponses, une décision sera prise »** by naming the actor: **« Après avoir étudié les réponses, la direction prendra une décision. »** If the actor is unknown, use **« Une décision sera prise après l'étude des réponses. »** That solution avoids the dangling understood subject, though it still leaves responsibility unnamed.

Finally revise **« Soixante pour cent des habitants veulent une ouverture tardive »** using Document A. A faithful answer is **« Soixante pour cent des répondants souhaitent une ouverture tardive au moins un jour par semaine. »** Add **« Le questionnaire ayant été proposé à l'accueil, ce résultat ne représente pas nécessairement l'ensemble des habitants. »** This correction demonstrates why source handling and grammar belong together. Precision includes retaining the population, the request's scope, and the sampling limitation.

# Chapter 10 — C1 integration and independent mastery

**Stage:** C1 integration. **Study allocation:** 100 hours in ten ten-hour units. **Prerequisites:** the grammar, vocabulary strategies, discourse control, and source handling developed throughout the course. Your task is to make these resources available while listening, interacting, reading, and writing under realistic constraints. Finishing 1,000 hours is evidence of sustained study, not an automatic certificate of proficiency.

This chapter combines precision with flexibility. Precision means choosing a tense, pronoun, word, or source claim for a reason. Flexibility means finding another route when the first formulation fails. A speaker who can elegantly repair a misunderstanding often communicates more successfully than a speaker who has memorized a perfect monologue but cannot respond to a question.

## 1. What advanced independence looks like

Imagine attending a public discussion about converting a former factory into a cultural center. You need to follow speakers with different viewpoints, identify the actual disagreement, ask a relevant question, and later explain the issue to someone who was absent. None of those tasks can be reduced to recalling a verb table. They draw on listening, inference, vocabulary, discourse, and social judgment at the same time.

Define progress through observable performances. Can you identify a speaker's main claim and a qualification? Can you distinguish an estimate from a commitment? Can you summarize disagreement without caricaturing either side? Can you maintain a conversation when an unfamiliar expression occurs? Can you revise an essay so that a reader can follow it without reconstructing missing logical steps? These questions give study a practical direction.

A useful error log separates three categories. A **knowledge gap** means you do not know the needed form or meaning. A **retrieval gap** means you know it in slow work but cannot access it during a task. A **monitoring gap** means you produced an error you could have noticed. Treat them differently. A knowledge gap needs instruction and examples; a retrieval gap needs repeated varied use; a monitoring gap needs a manageable checking routine. Copying every correction ten times does not diagnose which problem you have.

For example, if you write **« les mesures que nous avons pris »**, ask whether you know why *prises* is required. If not, revisit preceding direct-object agreement. If you can explain it immediately but missed it under time pressure, add a final pass that looks only for relative *que* before compound verbs. If you keep making the error while speaking, practice short meaningful sequences: **« la mesure que nous avons prise »**, **« les mesures que nous avons prises »**, and **« nous avons pris ces mesures »**. The contrast trains the actual decision.

Keep ambitious goals without turning every conversation into a test. A demanding reading can reveal new gaps for years. That is normal in any language, including one's first. The aim is a durable ability to learn from those gaps and continue communicating accurately enough for the situation. This course's vocabulary bank is a foundation for that system, not a claim to contain every word you will ever encounter.

## 2. Advanced agreement as a sequence of questions

Agreement becomes less intimidating when you identify grammatical relationships before looking at endings. Begin with the noun: what is its gender and number? Identify the adjective or participle that relates to it. Then identify the construction controlling agreement. Do not decide solely from the nearest noun; proximity is not the same as dependency.

With **être** in ordinary passive or intransitive compound constructions, agreement generally follows the subject: **« Les candidates sont arrivées »**, **« Les propositions ont été retenues »**. With **avoir**, find whether there is a direct object before the participle: **« Elles ont retenu les propositions »** versus **« Les propositions qu'elles ont retenues »**. In the second, *que* represents *les propositions* and comes before *ont retenues*. The feminine plural ending follows that preceding direct object.

Pronominal verbs require analysis. **« Elles se sont lavées »** treats *se* as the direct object: they washed themselves. **« Elles se sont lavé les mains »** has *les mains* as a following direct object; *se* is indirect, so *lavé* does not agree with *elles* through *se*. **« Les mains qu'elles se sont lavées »** has a preceding direct object and agreement with *les mains*. **« Elles se sont parlé »** has no agreement because *parler à quelqu'un* takes an indirect object. For verbs whose pronominal form has a particular lexical behavior, consult a dependable reference rather than extending one slogan to every verb.

The participle **fait** before an infinitive is invariable in **« les robes qu'elle a fait refaire »**. She caused the dresses to be remade; the construction is *faire + infinitive*. Other participle-plus-infinitive patterns can depend on who performs the infinitive's action, and spelling recommendations concerning *laissé* have variation across references. The practical skill is recognizing when a construction requires a targeted reference check. You do not gain precision by guessing confidently in an edge case.

Agreement can also reveal meaning. **« La plupart des participants sont arrivés »** uses a plural verb because the expression refers to multiple participants. **« Une partie des participants a répondu »** and plural agreement in some contexts can reflect different grammatical or semantic emphasis; for important edited prose, consider rewriting as **« Plusieurs participants ont répondu »** or **« Une partie du groupe a répondu »** if that preserves the intended quantity. A rewrite that removes ambiguity may be more useful than defending a borderline form.

## 3. Spelling, punctuation, and ambiguity in final revision

Advanced editing is not searching for every possible defect at once. Use focused passes. First check content and organization: is every necessary idea present and does the conclusion follow? Then check clause structure and reference. Finally check morphology, spelling, accents, and punctuation. If you begin by polishing commas in a paragraph that must be rewritten, you spend attention on work that will disappear.

Homophones need grammatical tests. **« Ces mesures »** points to particular measures; **« ses mesures »** identifies measures associated with a person. **« C'est »** can be expanded as *cela est* in suitable contexts; **« s'est »** belongs to a pronominal verb such as *elle s'est trompée*. **« Leur décision »** uses a possessive determiner; **« je leur réponds »** uses an indirect-object pronoun that does not become *leurs*. Explain the function before choosing the spelling.

Accents carry lexical and grammatical distinctions. **« Ou »** offers an alternative; **« où »** relates to place or, in some constructions, time. **« Du »** can be an article or contraction; **« dû »** is the masculine singular participle of *devoir*. **« Il a dû partir »** means he had to leave or must have left, depending on context. A missing accent may be tolerated in a quick message, but in a formal document it can force a reader to solve an avoidable ambiguity.

Punctuation helps the reader recover structure. **« Les habitants qui travaillent le soir seront consultés »** selects a subgroup: residents who work in the evening. **« Les habitants, qui travaillent le soir, seront consultés »** adds an explanatory relative and can imply that the relevant residents work in the evening generally. Do not insert commas merely where you happened to breathe. Ask whether the relative identifies the noun or adds extra information about an already identified group.

Dangling modifiers create unintended actors. **« En arrivant à la gare, le train était déjà parti »** attaches *en arrivant* awkwardly to *le train*, as if the train arrived at the station while already departed. Repair it: **« Quand je suis arrivé à la gare, le train était déjà parti. »** If you intend a general observation, **« À mon arrivée à la gare, le train était déjà parti »** also works. In each case, the temporal relationship remains while the actor becomes clear.

Finish by reading aloud at a natural pace. A sentence that repeatedly forces you to restart may contain too many embedded relationships. Divide it, then restore explicit links. Shortening is not automatically simplification of thought; sometimes two sentences express a complex relation more accurately because the reader can inspect each step.

## 4. Lexical precision: near-synonyms have different jobs

Knowing several words for an idea is useful only if you know their differences. **« Une difficulté »** is a difficulty; **« une contrainte »** is a constraint; **« un obstacle »** blocks progress; **« un enjeu »** is what is at stake or an important issue, depending on context. Calling every problem *un enjeu* can make prose vague. **« Le manque de personnel constitue une contrainte »** identifies a resource limit. **« L'enjeu est de garantir l'accès au service »** identifies the goal or stake.

Similarly, **« efficace »** concerns achieving an intended result, **« rentable »** concerns financial return, and **« pertinent »** concerns relevance or appropriateness. **« Une réponse pertinente »** addresses the question. **« Une mesure efficace »** works toward its aim. **« Une activité rentable »** produces a favorable financial result. A policy can be effective but not profitable; a profitable service can fail to address a public need. Choose the adjective after identifying the dimension of evaluation.

Verb choice can expose hidden assumptions. **« Prouver »** claims proof; **« indiquer »** points toward information; **« laisser entendre »** suggests an implication; **« prétendre »** often distances the writer from a claim or questions it. **« Le rapport prétend que... »** is not a neutral equivalent of *the report states that*. If you intend neutral attribution, use *indique*, *précise*, or *affirme* as appropriate. Tone can change through one reporting verb.

Learn families through contrasts. *Améliorer* is to improve something; *s'améliorer* is to improve or get better. **« Le centre améliore ses services »** has an actor changing services. **« Les services s'améliorent »** presents improvement without naming the agent. *Augmenter* can be transitive or intransitive: **« La direction augmente les tarifs »** versus **« Les tarifs augmentent »**. The grammatical pattern determines where responsibility appears.

For every high-value new word, create a mini-entry with a definition in simple French, one natural collocation, a contrasting word, and a personal example. For *une répercussion*: *une conséquence qui se fait sentir ailleurs ou plus tard*; *avoir des répercussions sur*; contrast with *une cause*; **« Le changement d'horaire a des répercussions sur l'organisation familiale. »** Review by reconstructing the relationship, not merely guessing an English gloss. At C1, flexible explanation often matters more than having the rarest available synonym.

## 5. Translation as reconstruction of meaning

Translation begins by understanding the source as a whole. Identify who acts, what happens, when, with what certainty, and for what audience. Then express that meaning in the target language using its own ordinary constructions. Only after that compare details. Starting with one-to-one word substitution often preserves the surface while distorting the message.

Consider **« Il aurait refusé de signer l'accord »**. Depending on context, the conditional can report unconfirmed information: *He is said to have refused to sign the agreement* or *He reportedly refused to sign it*. In a hypothetical past context it could mean *He would have refused to sign the agreement*. The morphology alone does not decide. Read the surrounding sentences and identify whether the writer is reporting a claim or imagining an alternative past.

English *since* can express time or cause. *Since Monday* gives **« depuis lundi »**. *Since the office is closed, we will return tomorrow* gives **« Puisque le bureau est fermé, nous reviendrons demain »** if the closure is presented as an accepted reason. *Because* can be *parce que*; *as* can have several roles; *while* can express simultaneity or contrast. Translate the relationship before choosing the connector.

French tenses may require a structural change in English. **« Cela fait trois ans que je travaille ici »** becomes *I have been working here for three years*. **« Je viens de terminer »** becomes *I have just finished*. **« Il devait arriver à huit heures »** may mean *He was supposed to arrive at eight*, but context can also suggest a scheduled future-in-the-past. Do not memorize one English equivalent as the only possible meaning of *devoir*.

Preserve register without imitating awkwardness. **« Nous vous saurions gré de bien vouloir nous transmettre le document »** is a formal request. A functional English translation might be *We would be grateful if you could send us the document*. Word-for-word translation would be unnatural. Conversely, translating a casual **« Tu peux m'envoyer ça ? »** as an elaborate formal request changes the relationship. Matching pragmatic force matters alongside informational accuracy.

After drafting, back-check in a disciplined way. Underline numbers, negatives, restrictive expressions, comparisons, and modal verbs in the source; locate their counterparts in the translation. Check names and time anchors separately. Then read the target text without looking at the source and ask whether it sounds coherent. A translation can pass the detail check while remaining clumsy, or sound elegant while dropping a restriction. Both checks are necessary.

## 6. Fast listening: decode, infer, and verify

Rapid speech feels like a wall when you try to recognize every written word in sequence. Actual listening uses rhythm, chunks, context, and selective attention. First establish the situation: who is speaking, to whom, for what purpose, and about which issue? That frame helps predict possible meanings, but prediction must remain a hypothesis until the sound supports it.

Use a three-listen routine. On the first listen, write only the topic, stance, and two supporting points. On the second, target details such as dates, contrast markers, and qualifications. On the third, transcribe a short difficult section exactly as heard. If a reliable transcript exists, compare afterward and explain the mismatch: unknown word, reduction, liaison, segmentation, or attention. Simply replaying the same extract many times without diagnosing the difficulty can train frustration rather than comprehension.

Segmentation errors are especially revealing. A familiar sequence may be heard as a different set of words because boundaries are not acoustically separated. Link the sound to meaning and grammar together. If you hear something like **« ils ont »**, distinguish it from **« ils sont »** through the consonant and the sentence's structure. If a final consonant appears through liaison, identify the phrase that licenses it. Do not assume every written final consonant must be pronounced.

Take notes in compressed meaning rather than full sentences. For a speaker saying **« L'ouverture tardive serait utile, mais elle suppose une réorganisation du personnel »**, note *benefit evening opening / condition staffing reorganization*. Then reconstruct the argument in French afterward. Trying to transcribe an entire fast discussion often makes you miss the next point. Use abbreviations you understand and distinguish the speaker's claims from your own reactions.

Synthetic TTS in the companion is valuable for repeated word and sentence practice, slower playback, and dictation. It does not replace authentic multi-speaker listening, emotional tone, overlap, or genre conventions. Use lawful interviews, discussions, lectures, and news reports as additional material. Rotate topics and voices while retaining some repeated listening so that progress includes both transfer and detailed improvement.

For a listening test, hide the model sentence, listen, type the French, and write an English rendering. Score transcription and meaning separately. Missing an accent, choosing the wrong tense, and misunderstanding a negation are different errors. The companion can compare against accepted strings and show the reference; a human review remains useful for a valid translation the bank did not anticipate. A mismatch is a prompt to inspect meaning, not automatic proof that every word of your answer is wrong.

## 7. Pragmatics, implied meaning, and humor

People do not always state their purpose directly. **« Il fait un peu froid ici »** can be a comment about temperature or an indirect request to close a window. Context determines which interpretation is plausible. A meeting participant saying **« C'est une proposition ambitieuse »** may be praising the plan, warning about feasibility, or doing both. Do not assign sarcasm merely because a phrase permits it.

Look for converging evidence: prior turns, intonation, facial expression when available, relationship, and practical circumstances. If uncertainty matters, ask a clarifying question. **« Quand vous dites “ambitieuse”, pensez-vous surtout au budget ou au calendrier ? »** invites the speaker to specify the concern without accusing them of irony. **« Si je comprends bien, vous êtes favorable au principe, mais vous avez des réserves sur la mise en œuvre »** checks a balanced interpretation.

Humor often depends on shared knowledge, polysemy, understatement, exaggeration, or a mismatch between expectation and outcome. A joke may fail in translation because its wording carries two meanings that the target language cannot reproduce. Explain the mechanism before attempting an equivalent. If a play on *vol* uses both flight and theft, an English version may require a different wordplay or an explanatory note. The right choice depends on whether the goal is literary translation, conversation, or analysis.

Be cautious with unfamiliar slang and taboo language. Understanding an expression in a film does not establish when you can use it appropriately. Record its approximate force, relationship, and setting. If a speaker teases a close friend, copying the phrase to a new colleague may change it from affectionate to insulting. Recognition can progress faster than production safely and usefully.

Practice pragmatic repair with three responses to a vague criticism. First, a clarifying response: **« Quel aspect vous paraît le plus fragile ? »** Second, acknowledgment plus focus: **« Je comprends la réserve sur les coûts ; voici comment nous pourrions la traiter. »** Third, a boundary: **« Je peux discuter du contenu de la proposition, mais j'aimerais que nous évitions les remarques personnelles. »** These are linguistic resources for maintaining a productive exchange, not formulas guaranteed to resolve every social situation.

## 8. Speaking: plan lightly, interact fully, repair clearly

A useful presentation has a question, a position, reasons, and a consequence. For a five-minute talk, plan a short opening, two developed points, one objection, and a conclusion. Write keywords, not a complete script, for practice. A script can support early work, but reading it aloud does not test spontaneous retrieval or interaction. Gradually reduce the notes while preserving the structure.

Useful openings include **« Je voudrais examiner les conditions dans lesquelles... »**, **« La question n'est pas seulement de savoir si..., mais aussi de déterminer comment... »**, and **« Je défendrai l'idée suivante... »**. Use them only if they match your actual plan. A memorized opening promising three parts is harmful if the talk contains two. Signposting should make the structure visible, not decorate an absent structure.

During questions, listen for the function: request for clarification, challenge to evidence, alternative proposal, or objection to a value judgment. Repeat the question's key point when useful: **« Si votre question porte sur le coût à long terme, nous ne disposons pas encore d'une estimation complète. »** Then answer what you can, state the limit, and explain a next step. Avoid filling every uncertainty with confident speculation.

Repair is part of fluent speech. **« Je reformule »**, **« Ce que je veux dire, c'est que... »**, **« J'ai employé le mot trop vite »**, and **« Je corrige : il s'agit d'une estimation, pas d'un montant définitif »** let you maintain trust while correcting yourself. If you cannot retrieve a word, define it: **« le document qui récapitule les décisions prises pendant la réunion »** can stand in for *le compte rendu*. Circumlocution is successful when it keeps meaning clear.

Record a presentation and assess different layers on separate listens. First assess structure and task fulfillment. Next assess grammar and vocabulary, noting only recurring or consequential errors. Finally assess audibility, rhythm, pausing, and listener effort. Do not interrupt every sentence to correct yourself during the first playback. Select two priorities for the next recording, such as clearer contrast markers and fewer ambiguous pronouns.

On-device speech recognition can provide a transcript if the browser and French language support are available. That transcript reflects recognition behavior, not a validated pronunciation score. A word can be recognized despite awkward rhythm, or misrecognized because of microphone conditions. Optional browser AI feedback can suggest revisions to a transcript, but it can also make mistakes. Use a competent human listener for assessments of pronunciation, interaction, and high-level communicative performance.

## 9. Integrated original practice examination

The following is an original course assessment inspired by advanced communicative tasks. It does not reproduce an official DALF paper, timing specification, marking grid, or copyrighted source. For current official requirements and sample papers, consult France Éducation international directly when accessible. The suggested times below organize this course's practice only.

**Reading and synthesis, 75 minutes.** Read the two original statements below. Write a neutral 120–160-word synthesis, then a separate 300–400-word recommendation. Keep the source account distinct from your own position.

> **Statement A — Cultural association.** « L'ancienne usine représente une occasion rare de créer un lieu culturel accessible. Le quartier manque de salles adaptées aux répétitions et aux ateliers. Nous proposons d'ouvrir progressivement le bâtiment, en commençant par les espaces dont la sécurité peut être assurée rapidement. Cette démarche permettrait d'ajuster le projet aux usages réels. Il ne faudrait toutefois pas que le caractère provisoire de l'ouverture serve de prétexte à reporter indéfiniment les travaux nécessaires. »
>
> **Statement B — Residents' group.** « Nous soutenons la réutilisation du bâtiment, mais nous demandons que les conséquences pour le voisinage soient examinées avant toute ouverture régulière. Les problèmes ne se limitent pas au bruit à l'intérieur : la circulation, les livraisons et les départs nocturnes doivent aussi être pris en compte. Une concertation utile suppose des informations précises, un calendrier public et une possibilité réelle de modifier le projet. »

**Listening and mediation, 30 minutes.** Ask a partner to read the following statement twice at a natural pace, or use TTS while acknowledging the synthetic source: **« L'ouverture progressive peut être une solution raisonnable, à condition que chaque étape fasse l'objet d'un bilan. Le premier mois ne donnera sans doute pas une image complète de la fréquentation, car la nouveauté attirera probablement des visiteurs occasionnels. Il faudra donc observer l'évolution sur une période plus longue et distinguer les visites ponctuelles des usages réguliers. »** Without seeing the text during the task, write the main recommendation, two qualifications, and a concise English explanation for someone who cannot attend.

**Speaking and interaction, 25 minutes including preparation.** Prepare for ten minutes, present for five, and discuss for ten with a partner. Question: **« À quelles conditions un projet culturel peut-il répondre aux besoins du quartier sans imposer des coûts excessifs à ses habitants ? »** Use the provided statements but do not invent numerical budgets. Your partner should challenge one assumption, ask for an alternative, and request clarification of one term.

**Language editing, 20 minutes.** Correct and explain: (1) **« Les propositions que nous avons retenu seront présentées demain. »** (2) **« Si les habitants auraient été consultés, le conflit aurait pu être évité. »** (3) **« Après avoir analysé le dossier, une décision sera annoncée. »** (4) **« Le rapport prétend que le bâtiment est disponible »** when neutral attribution is intended. (5) **« Tous les habitants soutiennent le projet »** when the source says only that a residents' group supports reuse under conditions.

## 10. Model reasoning and assessment keys

A sound synthesis identifies common ground first: both groups support reuse, while emphasizing different conditions. The cultural association stresses unmet space needs and staged opening; the residents' group stresses prior examination of practical impacts and meaningful consultation. Both want procedures that remain accountable over time. Do not summarize the second statement as opposition to culture, and do not summarize the first as support for opening regardless of safety.

A complete model synthesis follows. Its 135-word length fits the assigned short-source exercise; the numbered task uses a shorter synthesis because the source pack itself is brief.

> Les deux prises de position soutiennent la réutilisation de l'ancienne usine, mais elles insistent sur des conditions différentes. L'association culturelle met en avant le manque de locaux adaptés et propose une ouverture progressive des espaces dont la sécurité peut être garantie. Elle souhaite ainsi ajuster le projet aux besoins observés, sans que le provisoire conduise à différer indéfiniment les travaux nécessaires. Le groupe de riverains demande, pour sa part, une évaluation préalable des effets sur le quartier. Ses préoccupations concernent le bruit, mais aussi la circulation, les livraisons et les départs nocturnes. Il attend une concertation fondée sur des informations précises, un calendrier public et une possibilité réelle de modifier le projet. Les deux groupes associent donc leur soutien à des exigences de fonctionnement et de suivi, tout en mettant l'accent sur des priorités distinctes.

A complete model recommendation follows. It proposes a defensible course of action rather than presenting invented plans as source facts.

Je recommande une démarche progressive, mais assortie de conditions vérifiables avant chaque étape. Le besoin de locaux culturels mérite une réponse, et laisser le bâtiment inutilisé ne résout ni la question des ateliers ni celle de son avenir. Toutefois, l'utilité du projet ne dispense pas ses responsables d'examiner les conséquences pour les habitants.

La première étape devrait porter sur la sécurité des espaces susceptibles d'accueillir le public. Il faudrait préciser lesquels peuvent être utilisés, pour quelles activités et selon quelles modalités. Cette vérification permettrait d'éviter qu'une ouverture présentée comme limitée ne devienne, de fait, une occupation régulière insuffisamment encadrée.

En parallèle, une concertation devrait réunir les futurs usagers et les riverains autour d'informations concrètes. Les horaires, les livraisons et les conditions de départ méritent d'être discutés séparément : une activité silencieuse peut malgré tout provoquer une gêne à la sortie. Un calendrier public indiquerait les étapes du projet et les moments auxquels des ajustements restent possibles.

On pourrait objecter qu'une telle procédure ralentirait une ouverture attendue depuis longtemps. Cette réserve est compréhensible. Néanmoins, une expérimentation mal préparée risquerait de créer des tensions qui rendraient ensuite le projet plus difficile à poursuivre. Une préparation ciblée peut donc servir la continuité du lieu plutôt que la retarder inutilement.

Enfin, chaque phase devrait donner lieu à un bilan partagé portant sur les usages, les difficultés rencontrées et les modifications proposées. Les critères du bilan devraient être annoncés avant l'ouverture, afin que chacun sache sur quelles bases la suite sera décidée. La poursuite des travaux nécessaires devrait également faire l'objet d'un suivi distinct : le succès d'une première phase ne justifierait pas leur report permanent.

Cette démarche ne garantit pas l'absence de désaccord. Elle offre cependant un cadre dans lequel les besoins culturels et les conditions de vie du quartier peuvent être examinés ensemble, sans transformer l'une de ces préoccupations en simple obstacle à l'autre.

For the recommendation, several positions can be defensible. You might support a limited pilot with safety checks, transparent scheduling, consultation, and review before expansion. Or you might argue that certain unresolved conditions must be met before any opening. Evaluate whether the position responds to the sources, acknowledges a real objection, and explains why the proposed sequence is appropriate. There is no single correct political opinion hidden in the grammar.

The listening key should include a staged opening as reasonable if each stage is reviewed; the first month's attendance may be distorted by novelty; longer observation should separate occasional visits from regular use. A suitable English mediation is: *The speaker favors gradual opening with a review at each stage. Early visitor numbers may be unusually high because the place is new, so the project should be assessed over a longer period and distinguish one-off visitors from regular users.* A literal rendering of every word is unnecessary if the task is explanation rather than transcription.

Editing answers: (1) **« Les propositions que nous avons retenues... »** agrees with the preceding direct object. (2) **« Si les habitants avaient été consultés... »** supplies the unreal past condition. (3) **« Après avoir analysé le dossier, la direction annoncera une décision »** names the actor; another valid repair changes the introductory phrase. (4) Use **« indique »** or **« précise »** for neutral attribution when justified by context; *prétend* can imply distance or doubt. (5) **« Le groupe de riverains soutient la réutilisation du bâtiment, sous certaines conditions »** preserves the actual source and scope.

Use a six-dimension rubric: task fulfillment, source fidelity, organization, grammar, vocabulary/register, and spoken delivery/interaction where applicable. Score each 0–4 with quoted evidence: 0 absent, 1 frequent breakdown, 2 functional but uneven, 3 generally controlled, 4 flexible and precise. Do not convert the sum into an official C1 result. It is a diagnostic course score. A human reviewer should identify whether weaknesses are local slips or patterns that obstruct understanding. Revise one written response and repeat the oral task after targeted work; improvement in the second version is part of the assessment.

## 11. A vocabulary system that continues after the course

No finite wordlist contains all the French a person will ever need. Your needs change with work, interests, relationships, and the communities you encounter. Build a layered vocabulary system: a high-frequency core used reliably, broad receptive knowledge developed through reading and listening, and topic-specific clusters for your current goals. Keep those layers connected through sentences and meaningful tasks.

When collecting an item, decide whether it is worth active production. A rare literary adjective may deserve recognition only. A verb construction you need in weekly meetings deserves active practice. Record a word's gender or infinitive, construction, meaning in context, register, one natural example, and one contrast. If the example contains three other unknown words, simplify it before adding it to review. A review card should teach a retrievable relationship, not reproduce an entire difficult paragraph.

Space reviews according to actual recall rather than treating a fixed interval as a law. After an initial encounter, revisit the item soon, then after increasing gaps when retrieval succeeds. When you fail, inspect whether the problem was meaning, form, spelling, or construction. A French-to-English recognition card and an English-to-French production card test different things. Add a listening prompt when sound is the problem, and a sentence completion when the preposition is the problem.

Choose extensive input that is challenging enough to expand knowledge but readable enough to sustain attention. Alternate it with intensive study of short passages. Extensive reading builds familiarity with recurring patterns; intensive work explains a knot you cannot yet untie. If every page requires constant lookup, select an easier text for extensive practice and retain the harder one for a short focused session. Difficulty is useful when it produces learning rather than only interruption.

Once a month, complete a real project: write a review, explain a technical process, conduct an interview, discuss an article, or create a small presentation. Use the new vocabulary in service of that task, then remove expressions that feel forced. Ask for feedback on naturalness and meaning. The purpose of collecting words is an expanding ability to understand and act, not a large database that you rarely use.

# Grammar reference — Connecting forms, choices, and timelines

Use this reference when you need a compact explanation of a construction that appears across several chapters. The examples are original. First identify the meaning and grammatical relationship you need; then choose the form. The exercises at the end combine the distinctions rather than testing spelling alone.

## 1. Demonstrative pronouns: choosing a particular one

A demonstrative determiner accompanies a noun: **« ce livre »** — this book. A demonstrative pronoun replaces a noun phrase: **« celui de Nora »** — Nora's one. Choose the pronoun according to the noun it replaces, not the person who owns it.

| Replaced noun | Pronoun | Example and meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Masculine singular | celui | **« Je prends celui de droite. »** — I am taking the one on the right. |
| Feminine singular | celle | **« Ma réponse diffère de celle de Nora. »** — My answer differs from Nora's. |
| Masculine plural | ceux | **« Ceux qui arrivent tôt peuvent entrer. »** — Those who arrive early can enter. |
| Feminine plural | celles | **« Je garde celles que tu préfères. »** — I am keeping the ones you prefer. |

The simple forms normally need a following specification, such as **de + noun**, a relative clause, or another identifying complement. **« Ce stylo fonctionne ; celui de Nora aussi »** identifies a pen through its owner. **« Celui qui fonctionne est sur la table »** identifies it through a relative clause. For a standalone choice, use a compound form: **« Je prends celui-ci »**, rather than a bare **« Je prends celui »**.

Add **-ci** and **-là** to all four forms: *celui-ci, celle-ci, ceux-ci, celles-ci; celui-là, celle-là, ceux-là, celles-là*. They distinguish this one/these ones from that one/those ones. **« Cette veste-ci est légère ; celle-là est plus chaude »** — This jacket is light; that one is warmer. The distinction can be physical, textual, or conversational. In ordinary speech, *-là* is also widely used without a strict contrast in distance. With determiners, the suffix follows the noun: *ce livre-ci*. With pronouns, it attaches directly: *celui-ci*.

Distinguish an identifiable noun from an unspecified idea. **« Celui qui m'intéresse est moins cher »** means the one that interests me is cheaper; a masculine noun such as *modèle* is understood. **« Ce qui m'intéresse, c'est le prix »** means what interests me is the price; *ce* introduces the thing or idea at issue without matching a named noun's gender.

Choose **ce qui** for a relative subject: **« Je comprends ce qui te gêne »** — I understand what bothers you. Choose **ce que** for a direct object: **« Je comprends ce que tu demandes »** — I understand what you are asking for. Choose **ce dont** when the underlying construction needs *de*: **« Je comprends ce dont tu as besoin »** — I understand what you need. Rebuild the basic clause: *quelque chose te gêne; tu demandes quelque chose; tu as besoin de quelque chose*. That reveals the relationship more reliably than translating English *what* mechanically.

## 2. Possessive pronouns: agreement follows the possession

**« Mon dossier »** contains a possessive determiner and a noun. **« Le mien »** replaces the whole phrase. To choose a possessive pronoun, identify the owner, then the gender and number of the thing possessed. If a woman says **« Ce dossier est le mien »**, *mien* is masculine because *dossier* is masculine.

| Owner | One masculine item | One feminine item | Several masculine items | Several feminine items |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| je | le mien | la mienne | les miens | les miennes |
| tu | le tien | la tienne | les tiens | les tiennes |
| il/elle | le sien | la sienne | les siens | les siennes |
| nous | le nôtre | la nôtre | les nôtres | les nôtres |
| vous | le vôtre | la vôtre | les vôtres | les vôtres |
| ils/elles | le leur | la leur | les leurs | les leurs |

**« Vos conclusions sont proches des miennes »** — Your conclusions are close to mine. *Conclusions* is feminine plural, so *les miennes* is required; *de + les* contracts to *des*. **« Je préfère votre méthode à la leur »** — I prefer your method to theirs. **« Son bureau est plus grand que le nôtre »** — His or her office is larger than ours. *Son* alone does not establish whether the owner is male or female.

Keep the circumflex in the pronouns **nôtre** and **vôtre**. The determiners have no circumflex: **« notre projet »**, **« votre réponse »**. Compare **« Notre projet est prêt ; le vôtre avance »** — Our project is ready; yours is progressing. Do not write *le votre* in edited standard French. Pronouns normally keep their article; **« C'est mien »** is not the ordinary equivalent of *It is mine*. Use **« C'est le mien »** for an identified masculine object or **« C'est à moi »** to express ownership without choosing a gendered replacement.

## 3. Adverbs: forming them and choosing their scope

An adjective describes a noun; an adverb commonly modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, or a whole statement. **« Une réponse claire »** is a clear answer; **« Elle répond clairement »** means she answers clearly. Many manner adverbs use **-ment**, often added to the feminine adjective: *clair → claire → clairement; heureux → heureuse → heureusement; doux → douce → doucement*. An adjective already ending in *e* commonly adds *-ment* directly: *rare → rarement*.

For many masculine adjectives ending in **-é, -i, or -u**, add *-ment* to that masculine form: *aisé → aisément; poli → poliment; absolu → absolument*. Frequent **-ant** adjectives give **-amment**: *constant → constamment; suffisant → suffisamment*. Frequent **-ent** adjectives give **-emment**: *prudent → prudemment; apparent → apparemment*. In these endings, *-amment* and *-emment* are generally pronounced alike, so sound alone does not settle their spelling. These formation patterns are summarized in the [CCDMD adverb reference](https://francomane.ccdmd.qc.ca/sites/man.ccdmd.qc.ca/files/documents/synthese_les_adverbes_0_1.pdf) and the [OQLF formation guide](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/21700/lorthographe/cas-particuliers-lies-a-lorthographe/formation-des-adverbes-en-ment).

Do not turn patterns into an exception-free machine. Learn *lent → lentement*, *gentil → gentiment*, *bref → brièvement*, *précis → précisément*, and *profond → profondément*. *Bon* commonly corresponds to *bien*: **« Elle explique bien »** — She explains well. *Mauvais* commonly corresponds to *mal*: **« Il a mal compris »** — He misunderstood. *Vite* is an adverb in **« Elle marche vite »**; it does not become feminine or plural to match *elle*.

Position depends on length, function, and focus. A short frequent adverb often follows a simple finite verb: **« Je comprends bien »**. In a compound tense it often falls between auxiliary and participle: **« J'ai bien compris »**, **« Elle a souvent voyagé »**. A longer manner expression can follow the participle: **« Elle a expliqué la procédure très clairement »**. Sentence adverbs can frame the statement: **« Heureusement, personne n'a été blessé »** — Fortunately, nobody was injured.

Placement can change meaning. **« Il a simplement expliqué le problème »** may mean he merely explained it; **« Il a expliqué le problème simplement »** means he explained it in a simple way. **« Elle travaille encore »** means she is still working; **« Elle travaille seulement le lundi »** restricts the working day. Ask what the adverb modifies before deciding its location. Adverbs do not all share one compulsory slot; see the [OQLF overview of adverb functions](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/24328/la-grammaire/la-grammaire-actuelle/les-classes-de-mots-et-les-groupes/ladverbe-et-le-groupe-adverbial) for a continuing reference.

## 4. Indefinites and free choice

**Chaque** accompanies a singular noun; **chacun/chacune** replaces a person or item considered individually. **« Chaque candidate reçoit un dossier »** — Each candidate receives a file. **« Chacune reçoit un dossier »** — Each one receives a file. Use a singular verb in these examples. **« Chacun des participants a répondu »** distributes the action across the group without turning *chacun* into a plural subject. **« Tout le monde a répondu »** also takes singular agreement, despite referring to several people.

**Quelqu'un** means someone; **quelque chose** means something. An adjective after these constructions often uses **de**: **« quelqu'un de fiable »** — someone reliable; **« quelque chose d'utile »** — something useful. With *quelque chose*, the adjective is masculine singular in this construction: **« quelque chose de nouveau »**. Similarly, **« rien d'important »** means nothing important. Do not infer feminine agreement from the feminine noun *chose* inside the fixed indefinite expression.

In standard negative statements, use **personne/rien/aucun** with *ne*, normally without adding *pas*: **« Personne ne vient »**, **« Je n'ai rien reçu »**, **« Aucun dossier n'est complet »**. *Aucun* accompanies the singular noun here; *aucun des dossiers* can replace it. **« Personne n'a rien dit »** means nobody said anything; the negatives combine naturally. Learn the whole construction, rather than treating each word as an isolated English equivalent: **« sans rien dire »** means without saying anything. **« Est-ce que quelqu'un a vu quoi que ce soit ? »** asks whether anyone saw anything, without asserting that nobody did.

**Tout** can mean everything: **« Tout est prêt »**. **Tous/toutes** can mean all the members of an established group: **« Ils sont tous arrivés »**, **« Elles sont toutes arrivées »**. Before nouns, agreement follows the noun: *tout le dossier, toute la page, tous les dossiers, toutes les pages*. Distinguish **« tous les jours »** — every day — from **« toute la journée »** — all day.

Free-choice expressions widen the selection. **« N'importe qui peut participer »** — Anyone can take part. **« Choisis n'importe quoi »** — Choose anything. In **« Tu dis n'importe quoi »**, the expression can instead criticize the content as nonsense; context matters. **N'importe lequel/laquelle/lesquels/lesquelles** selects freely from a known set: **« Tu peux prendre n'importe laquelle de ces chaises »** — You can take any of these chairs. **« N'importe quel siège convient »** uses a determiner plus noun. **« Quel que soit votre choix »** — Whatever your choice — is a separate construction with subjunctive *soit*; its form should not be confused with *quelque chose*.

## 5. Future anterior and recent past: locating one event relative to another

The **futur antérieur** consists of the future of *avoir* or *être* plus a past participle: **« j'aurai terminé »**, **« tu auras compris »**, **« elle sera partie »**, **« nous serons arrivés »**. Choose the auxiliary as for the corresponding compound past construction and apply its agreement rules. **« Les lettres que j'aurai écrites »** has agreement with preceding direct object *lettres*; **« elles seront arrivées »** has agreement with the subject through *être*.

Think of a future reference point and look backward from it. **« Demain à dix heures, j'aurai terminé »** — By ten tomorrow, I will have finished. The finishing occurs before the future checkpoint. **« Quand tu arriveras, nous aurons déjà mangé »** — When you arrive, we will already have eaten. Arrival is future; eating is completed before that arrival. The future anterior does not require a second verb if an expression such as *demain à dix heures* supplies the checkpoint.

French generally retains a future tense after future-time conjunctions such as **quand, lorsque, dès que** where English commonly uses present: **« Dès que j'arriverai, je t'appellerai »** — As soon as I arrive, I will call you. Use the future anterior when completion matters: **« Dès que j'aurai terminé le rapport, je te l'enverrai »** — As soon as I have finished the report, I will send it to you. Do not import English tense choices word for word. This rule concerns future time clauses; conditional *si* follows a different system.

The **passé récent** uses **venir de + infinitive**. **« Je viens de terminer »** — I have just finished. Conjugate *venir* for the person: *je viens, tu viens, il vient, nous venons, vous venez, ils viennent*. The infinitive remains unchanged. **« Elles viennent de partir »** means they have just left; it is not *elles viennent de parties*. Distinguish physical origin: **« Je viens de Lyon »** — I come from Lyon — has a place, not an infinitive.

From a past viewpoint, use the imperfect of *venir*: **« Je venais de terminer quand elle a appelé »** — I had just finished when she called. First the work finishes; then, soon afterward, the call occurs. *Venais de* marks recency relative to that past moment, not necessarily to now. **« Ils venaient de s'installer »** — They had just moved in — puts the reflexive pronoun before the infinitive. These constructions help narrate immediate transitions without pretending that *just* always translates to one isolated French adverb.

## 6. Prepositions in space, transport, and time

For many ordinary cities, use **à** for location or destination and **de** for origin: **« Je vais à Paris ; je reviens de Paris »**. For countries, learn the article and then the common pattern:

| Country type | Location/destination | Origin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feminine | en France | de France |
| Masculine beginning with a consonant | au Canada | du Canada |
| Masculine beginning with a vowel | en Iran | d'Iran |
| Plural | aux États-Unis | des États-Unis |

**« Elle habite en France et travaille au Canada »** states locations; movement does not require a different preposition in these examples. Some names have conventional patterns outside the basic table: *à Cuba, de Cuba; à Madagascar, de Madagascar*. Learn uncertain place names as complete expressions rather than guessing from an ending. City names containing an article can also require contraction, as in *au Havre*.

For transport, the course's neutral patterns are **en voiture, en train, en bus, en avion, en bateau**, but **à pied, à vélo, à moto, à cheval**. **« Je vais au bureau à vélo »** describes the means of travel. **« Je suis dans le bus »** describes being inside the bus; **« Le sac est sur le vélo »** locates the bag on the bicycle. A physical-location phrase and a transport expression answer different questions.

Time expressions encode different relationships. **Depuis** connects an earlier start to a reference point: **« J'habite ici depuis deux ans »** — I have lived here for two years; **« depuis lundi »** gives the starting date. French uses present because the residence continues now. With a past reference, **« Il attendait depuis une heure quand je suis arrivé »** means he had been waiting for an hour when I arrived.

**Pendant** measures a bounded period: **« J'ai habité ici pendant deux ans »** — I lived here for two years. It does not by itself assert continuation now. **Il y a** places something before now: **« Je suis arrivé il y a deux ans »** — I arrived two years ago. **Dans** places an event after now: **« Je partirai dans deux heures »** — I will leave in two hours. Compare **« Je partirai pour deux semaines »** — I will go away for two weeks — which gives the planned duration, not the delay before leaving. Before translating English *for*, draw the start, duration, and endpoint you actually mean.

## 7. Tout before an adjective: intensity or all members?

When **tout** means *completely* or *very*, it functions adverbially. It normally stays unchanged before a masculine adjective, a feminine adjective beginning with a vowel, or a feminine adjective beginning with a **mute h**. The exception is a feminine adjective beginning with a consonant or **aspirated h**: *tout* then agrees in gender and number. The *h* distinction affects liaison and elision; an aspirated *h* is not normally an instruction to pronounce an English-style h sound.

Original contrasts: **« Ils sont tout surpris »** — They are completely surprised; **« Elle est tout étonnée »** — She is completely astonished; **« Elles sont tout heureuses »** — They are very happy; **« Elle est toute contente »** — She is very pleased; **« Elles sont toutes honteuses »** — They are thoroughly ashamed. *Heureux* has mute *h*; *honteux* has aspirated *h*. Check a dictionary when uncertain.

Compare the pronoun meaning *all*: **« Ils sont tous heureux »** means all of them are happy, whereas **« Ils sont tout heureux »** means they are very happy. In **« Elles sont toutes contentes »**, spelling alone can permit both all-members and intensity readings. If the distinction matters, write **« Elles sont toutes très contentes »** or **« Elles sont extrêmement contentes »**. The standard agreement rule is confirmed by [OQLF's adverbial tout reference](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/22907/la-grammaire/ladverbe/tout-employe-comme-adverbe) and the [Government of Canada writing guide](https://nos-langues.canada.ca/fr/cles-de-la-redaction/tout-accord-de-ladverbe).

## 8. Twelve exercises, with an explained key

1. Replace *la réponse de Nora*: **« Ma réponse diffère de ___. »**
2. Translate: *I am taking this one, not that one*, referring to two masculine books.
3. Complete: **« Je comprends ce ___ tu demandes et ce ___ tu as besoin. »**
4. Replace *nos conclusions*: **« Vos conclusions ressemblent à ___. »** Preserve any contraction.
5. Form the adverbs from *prudent, constant, gentil,* and *lent*.
6. Translate: *She has often traveled, and she explained the procedure clearly.*
7. Complete: **« ___ des candidates reçoit quelque chose ___ utile. »** Use *each one*.
8. Translate: *Nobody received anything. You can take any of these chairs.*
9. Complete: **« Dès que j'___ terminé, je t'___ le fichier. »** Use the future anterior of *terminer* and the simple future of *envoyer*.
10. Translate: *I had just left when she called.*
11. Complete: **« Je vais ___ Canada ___ avion. J'y habite ___ deux ans ; je suis arrivé ___ deux ans. »**
12. Complete with intensity meanings: **« Elles sont ___ heureuses, ___ contentes et ___ étonnées. »** Then translate *All of them are happy* with a masculine group.

**1.** **« celle de Nora »**. *Réponse* is feminine singular; *celle* takes its identifying complement. **2.** **« Je prends celui-ci, pas celui-là. »** The suffixes make the choices independently identifiable. **3.** **« ce que tu demandes et ce dont tu as besoin »**. *Demander* takes a direct object here; *avoir besoin de* supplies the relationship represented by *dont*.

**4.** **« aux nôtres »**. Feminine plural *conclusions* gives *les nôtres*; *à + les* contracts to *aux*. Keep the circumflex. **5.** *Prudemment, constamment, gentiment, lentement.* The first two follow productive endings; the latter two must be learned with their actual forms. **6.** **« Elle a souvent voyagé et elle a expliqué la procédure clairement. »** *Souvent* naturally sits inside the compound tense; the manner adverb can follow the object. Other well-formed placements may shift focus.

**7.** **« Chacune des candidates reçoit quelque chose d'utile. »** *Chacune* is feminine singular; *de* elides before *utile*. **8.** **« Personne n'a rien reçu. Tu peux prendre n'importe laquelle de ces chaises. »** The negative words combine without *pas*. *Laquelle* matches feminine singular *chaise*, since you select one chair from the set.

**9.** **« Dès que j'aurai terminé, je t'enverrai le fichier. »** Completion precedes the future sending. **10.** **« Je venais de partir quand elle a appelé. »** The recent past is located relative to a past call. **11.** **« au Canada en avion... depuis deux ans... il y a deux ans »**. Destination, transport, ongoing duration, and past starting event each need their own expression.

**12.** **« tout heureuses, toutes contentes et tout étonnées »**. Mute *h* and a vowel preserve the adverb's invariable form; feminine consonant-initial *contentes* requires agreement. **« Ils sont tous heureux »** expresses all members of the masculine group. The explanation must identify both grammatical function and following sound; copying the adjective's ending everywhere would give the wrong result.

## Reference and verification note

### A final set of distinctions for careful reading

**Neutral le** can stand for an idea, a property, or a clause rather than a masculine noun. In **« Elle est prête, et je le suis aussi »**, “She is ready, and I am too,” **le** represents being ready; it stays **le** even when the speaker is a woman. Compare **« Je la connais »**, “I know her,” where **la** represents a feminine person or noun. In **« Il viendra, je le sais »**, “He will come; I know it,” **le** represents the proposition. Identify what a pronoun replaces before assigning it a gender mechanically.

**Soi** is a stressed reflexive pronoun useful with a general or indefinite subject: **« On a parfois besoin de temps pour soi »**, “Sometimes one needs time for oneself.” With a particular person, use the appropriate stressed form: **« Elle pense à elle-même »**, “She is thinking about herself.” **« Chacun pour soi »**, “everyone for themselves,” is a fixed useful example. Conversational **on** meaning a specific “we” can behave differently from a fully general **on**, so inspect the intended reference rather than substituting **soi** for every pronoun after on.

For **color adjectives**, ordinary simple adjectives generally agree: **« des robes bleues »**, “blue dresses.” Many noun-derived color terms remain invariable: **« des chaussures marron »**, “brown shoes,” and **« des robes orange »**, “orange dresses.” Some established exceptions, including **rose**, agree: **« des fleurs roses »**, “pink flowers.” A compound color description normally remains invariable: **« des yeux bleu clair »**, “light-blue eyes.” Keep the distinction between a color adjective and a noun used as a color label; a rule such as “all colors are invariable” loses it.

**Pire** is an adjective meaning “worse,” contrasting with **meilleur**, “better.” **« Cette solution est pire que la première »** means “This solution is worse than the first.” **Plus mauvais** is also possible in many literal comparisons. **Moindre** can mean smaller or lesser, often with abstract nouns: **« un risque moindre »**, “a smaller risk.” For an ordinary physical size comparison, **plus petit** is usually the straightforward choice. Do not confuse adjective **pire** with adverb **pis** in fixed expressions such as **tant pis**, “too bad.”

**Quoique** means “although” and commonly introduces a subjunctive clause: **« Quoique ce soit difficile, nous continuons »**, “Although it is difficult, we continue.” **Quoi que** means “whatever,” as in **« Quoi que tu décides, préviens-moi »**, “Whatever you decide, let me know.” Their similar sound does not make their functions identical. **Quel que**, with forms agreeing with its subject, appears in **« Quelle que soit la raison, il faut répondre »**, “Whatever the reason, we must reply.” Contrast **quelque** as a determiner in **« quelque difficulté »**, “some difficulty,” or plural **quelques difficultés**, “a few difficulties.” Ask first whether the sentence means “although,” “whatever thing,” “whatever the identified noun may be,” or “some/a few.” That meaning test is more reliable than guessing the spelling from sound.
