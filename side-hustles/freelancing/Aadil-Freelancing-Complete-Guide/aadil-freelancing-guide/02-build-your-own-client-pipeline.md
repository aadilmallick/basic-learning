# 2. Build your own client pipeline

[Previous: offers and proof](01-positioning-offers-and-proof.md) · [Guide home](README.md) · [Next: sales and pricing](03-discovery-pricing-proposals-and-money.md)

## Start with a useful definition of a lead

A lead is a person or organization that might have a relevant problem and a plausible route to a purchasing decision. A company name is an account, a publicly listed email is a contact detail, and a social media follower is an audience member. None automatically becomes a qualified lead. This distinction matters because a developer can build impressive lists and automations while making little progress toward paid work. Your aim is to learn which accounts have a problem you can solve, then reach the right person respectfully.

For your extension service, a promising account might be a small agency with custom web integration work or a training business with a repeated browser workflow. Qualification still requires conversation. For your web improvement offer, look for a defined flow, deadline, or operational problem rather than merely an old design. For your tutorial service, find a developer company with an audience and a content owner, then establish whether an external contribution would help. These are different sales motions; do not send one generic message to all three.

A pipeline is a record of these conversations and next actions. It is not a machine that guarantees customers. Your initial pipeline can be a CSV or simple table with company, source, observed signal, contact role, relevant offer, status, last interaction, next action, and date. Add a source URL and date for every public observation. Keep private conversation notes separate from public marketing assets. The included CSV is fictional sample data, not a real prospect list, so it can be copied safely without implying anyone has expressed interest.

## Use three acquisition channels, with unequal attention

For your first month, spend most acquisition time on warm introductions and agency relationships. Add small-volume, researched outreach to direct buyers. Use helpful content to support those conversations and compound your evidence over time. A reasonable starting allocation is forty percent warm and partner activity, forty percent targeted outreach, and twenty percent proof content. These percentages are planning choices, not evidence that the channels will convert at those rates. Adjust after you see where qualified conversations actually originate.

Do not make audience growth a prerequisite for freelancing. A person with a small network can sell a clearly defined service through a relevant introduction. Conversely, a post with thousands of views may reach mostly peers who do not buy your service. Your LinkedIn goal is to make the right person understand your work and start a useful conversation. Your website's goal is to make that conversation easier. Views and traffic are supporting indicators; paid commitments and customer results are the commercial evidence.

The first constraint is usually relevance. If you cannot explain why an account might care without inventing its problem, more email volume will not solve the issue. Improve account selection and the offer. Once you have repeated evidence of a particular problem, you can streamline research and follow-up. Automating a confused offer produces more confused messages, often at additional financial and reputational cost.

## Warm introductions without making people feel used

Begin with people who actually know your work: a previous freelance client, classmates, alumni you already know, former collaborators, or agency contacts you have genuinely met. Your resume establishes education and employment history; it does not prove that any particular person will introduce you. Build your own list of real relationships and note how you know each person. Do not copy an employer directory or imply permission to use internal contact information for a side business.

An introduction request should be easy to understand and easy to decline. Instead of “Do you know anyone who needs a developer?”, describe the buyer and problem: “I am taking on small browser workflow and React integration projects. I am looking to speak with agency owners or training operations leads who have a repetitive browser task they want to improve.” Add one relevant demo and a short forwardable description. You are helping the contact recognize a situation, not asking them to design your marketing strategy.

A draft to a genuine former collaborator is: “Hi [name], I am opening a small number of scoped projects around browser extensions and web integrations. Here is a short example of my work: [link]. If you know a team repeatedly copying information between browser tools, I would appreciate an introduction to the person who owns that workflow. A short conversation would help establish whether I can be useful. No pressure if nobody comes to mind.” Personalize the relationship context truthfully before using it.

Ask a past client for two different things at the right time. First, ask for feedback about the actual work and permission to use a factual case study. Separately, ask whether they know a similar business facing a related issue. A referral request is more actionable when it includes the specific service you now offer. Avoid making positive feedback a condition of support or offering undisclosed incentives for endorsements. Your strongest referral asset is a customer who understands what you did and would willingly recommend it.

## Agency partnerships: your most practical direct channel to test

An agency already has customers, project management, and recurring needs. It may occasionally need help with a browser extension, a custom integration, a React component, or a difficult handover. Your role is to reduce its delivery burden. The offer should specify what you can own, how you communicate, how you protect the client relationship, and when you are available. “I can help with overflow” is a start, but a defined capability is easier to remember when a real project arrives.

Target design-led studios that build sites but may need custom functionality, small web application agencies with variable capacity, and specialist studios serving education or operations-heavy customers. Public service pages can suggest technical fit; they do not establish that the firm uses subcontractors. Look for an explicit freelance call, collaborator page, relevant delivery story, or a real introduction. If none exists, your first question is whether outside specialists are part of its delivery model.

Research directories as account sources rather than guaranteed lead feeds. Webflow's Certified Partners directory and Shopify's Partner Directory list real agencies and their areas of work. You do not need to become certified simply to identify possible partners. Review the agency's own site before contacting it, and do not imply you are a platform partner. Framer also has an expert ecosystem, but entry and ranking rules can change; pursue it only if you decide that platform-specific delivery is a real service you want to provide. [D1](references.md#d1), [D2](references.md#d2), [D3](references.md#d3)

Your agency pitch can be: “I noticed your team delivers [specific work from its public site]. My work includes browser extensions and focused React/TypeScript integrations. If you occasionally need an outside specialist for a small browser component or a defined application flow, I can provide a scoped pilot, reviewable code, and handover notes. Do you work with independent developers for that kind of task?” Include one proof link. This asks about fit without falsely claiming that you know the agency is understaffed.

Before taking subcontract work, agree who communicates with the end customer, who signs acceptance, who owns the repository, whether your name can appear publicly, and how payment works. Avoid an arrangement where your payment depends indefinitely on whether the agency gets paid. Clarify non-solicitation terms, project ownership, confidentiality, and what happens when the agency changes requirements. A good partner relationship is commercially explicit and operationally calm.

## Research a prospect in ten to fifteen minutes

First open the organization's own site. Identify what it sells, who uses it, and whether your chosen offer relates to its work. Write one sentence explaining the fit. If you cannot, stop researching that account. Next, look for a public signal: a relevant service, launch, job posting, documentation area, support pattern, or process described by the organization. Record the exact URL and observation date. Distinguish “observed fact” from “my hypothesis” in your notes.

Then identify the owner of the problem. For a small agency, it may be the founder, technical lead, or delivery director. For a training company, it may be the operations lead or owner. For a developer-tools company, it may be developer relations, documentation, or content leadership. Use official team pages, published professional profiles, and appropriate business contact routes. Do not collect personal addresses or assume a generic sales form welcomes vendor pitches. A supplier or partnership route is preferable when available.

Finally, choose the smallest sensible next action. It could be an introduction request, a question about subcontracting, a reply to a stated need, or a short suggestion for a tutorial. You do not need to build a free custom prototype for every prospect. An observation and an existing relevant demo are usually enough to establish whether a conversation is worthwhile. Record a follow-up date so the account does not disappear into an unstructured list.

## Find direct web development customers from business events

For web improvements, look for a business event that changes requirements: a new program, a second location, a booking process moving online, a service expansion, or a public request for a developer. These events create a reason to discuss a workflow. A dated visual design may be harmless. A new intake process with unclear ownership may create a real operational need. The buyer decides which matters; your research prepares an informed question.

Your BetterCare Meals experience suggests food-service and ordering workflows as one possible angle, but do not generalize that into expertise in regulated healthcare systems. Another fit is training and tutoring businesses, where you can understand the user's learning journey and administrative work. Ask how inquiries become bookings, how payments are reconciled, and which steps are manual. Sell the narrow improvement that emerges, not an entire replacement system by default.

Use local business directories, chamber event listings, alumni founder communities, and public company announcements to identify accounts manually. If your actual network is in Northern Virginia, DC, or William & Mary alumni circles, those are plausible starting areas; your education and resume do not establish current residence or active membership in a group. Attend events you can participate in meaningfully. A small number of recurring relationships can be more valuable than one large networking night followed by generic messages.

## Find browser workflow work without selling “extensions” first

Many potential buyers do not know that an extension could help. Ask about repeated work inside a browser: moving approved information, filling a repeated internal template, annotating resources, exporting data, or accessing a tool during another task. Observe the process with permission. You are looking for a small, frequent action with clear inputs and outputs. The customer may call it administration rather than software.

The extension should fit the environment. An internal team may have browser policies that prevent installation. A workflow on a third-party platform may be better served by an official API or a built-in feature. A task handling sensitive information may require a security review beyond your pilot scope. These are qualification facts, not reasons to abandon the entire niche. Finding them early lets you recommend a viable route and avoid spending days on an unsuitable prototype.

A useful discovery prompt in a public educational post is: “If your team repeatedly copies the same approved information between browser tabs, I can help assess whether an existing integration, a small extension, or a process change is the simplest fix.” This broadens the solution while keeping the problem narrow. It attracts people with a recognizable task and signals that you will not force a browser extension into every situation.

## Find technical writing customers through a reader problem

Developer companies buy educational work to help users succeed, explain a capability, support a launch, or improve adoption. Your pitch should identify a reader and a concrete result. “I can write about AI” is too broad. “A reproducible TypeScript example showing a user-triggered export with authentication and a retryable background step” is specific enough for an editor to judge. Before pitching it, search the company's existing docs and blog for the same idea.

Create a short pitch brief with the target reader, prerequisites, outcome, proposed structure, example repository, estimated scope, and proof sample. Explain why the idea connects to the product without inventing evidence that users are confused. Phrase an unverified gap as a question: “Would an end-to-end example combining these documented features be useful to your audience?” If an editor says no, ask which reader problem currently matters. That information can guide a better idea.

Publishers, technical content agencies, and developer education businesses are additional buyers. A content agency may offer repeat work but require white-label writing or different rights. A direct developer company may pay for deeper engineering but have a slower review process. Your proposal should separate technical implementation, writing, revisions, and publication rights. Treat commissioned education as a service business with delivery obligations, not merely a way to get paid for posting what you would write anyway.

## Write outreach that creates a useful next step

A good message has context, relevance, proof, and a low-friction next action. Context shows why this person is receiving it. Relevance links your service to a plausible problem. Proof reduces the risk of replying. The next action should be small enough for an unfamiliar person to take. Avoid a long biography, a list of every framework, or a forced calendar link before you know whether the offer fits.

For agencies: “Hi [name], I saw [specific relevant service or project on your site]. I build Chrome extensions and focused React/TypeScript integrations; [demo] shows one browser workflow. If a client needs a small custom browser component, I can scope it separately and hand over tested code and documentation. Do you ever use independent specialists for that work?” The bracketed information must be researched and replaced before sending. This is a commercial draft, not a message sent by this guide.

For a direct business after a genuine observation: “Hi [name], I noticed [specific public process or request]. I work on small web and browser workflow improvements. I wondered whether [clearly labeled workflow hypothesis] is something your team still handles manually. If it is a priority, I can help assess a small fix; here is a related example: [link]. Who would be the best person to ask?” Never claim that a form is broken unless you actually tested it appropriately, and avoid submitting fake customer orders just to create sales evidence.

For a tutorial editor: “Hi [name], I build browser extensions and write practical technical guides. I am exploring a tutorial for [reader] that produces [concrete outcome] using [documented capabilities]. I checked [relevant content] and would first confirm whether this overlaps your roadmap. My sample is [link]. Are external tutorial proposals useful to your team at the moment?” This respects the possibility that the company already has the content or does not commission it.

## Follow up with context and a stopping rule

A follow-up should make the decision easier, not merely repeat “checking in.” After several business days, add one relevant clarification: a smaller pilot scope, a closely matched demo, or a question about the right owner. A second follow-up can close the loop politely. A provisional cadence is an initial message, one follow-up after four to six business days, and a final one roughly a week later. This is a working practice, not a universal conversion formula. Stop immediately after a refusal or opt-out.

A final draft might say: “I will close the loop here. If a browser workflow or small integration becomes a priority later, this example shows the kind of scoped work I do: [link]. No need to respond if it is not relevant.” Include the appropriate commercial identification and opt-out information for the channel. Do not contact the same person through several channels merely because they have not answered. Silence is not permission to escalate pressure.

Track outcomes with useful categories: no response, wrong owner, no relevant problem, timing later, no budget, qualified conversation, paid diagnostic, won implementation, or declined. “Interested” is too vague to guide work. A prospect who asks for a proposal has not accepted it. A signed agreement without the required initial payment may not yet be ready to schedule. Accurate stages prevent wishful thinking from driving your calendar.

## Make commercial outreach lawful and deliverable

For US commercial email, the FTC states that CAN-SPAM applies to business-to-business messages as well as bulk mail. Use accurate sender information and subjects, identify the commercial nature appropriately, include a valid postal address and a clear opt-out, and honor opt-outs within the required period. The FTC guide specifies ten business days and requires the opt-out mechanism to operate for at least thirty days after sending. The template footer in the assets is unfinished until you add your valid business mailing details. One-to-one personalization does not remove these obligations. [C1](references.md#c1)

This guide uses a US starting context because that is the available context. International outreach can have additional consent and privacy rules, so restrict an initial campaign to a jurisdiction you have checked rather than assuming US practices work worldwide. A contact database or email verifier does not grant permission to market to someone. Review the communication channel's rules as well. The purpose is a sustainable business relationship, so a recipient's refusal belongs on a suppression list rather than in another sequence.

For email delivery, configure your domain using your mail provider's current instructions. Google distinguishes requirements for all senders from stronger bulk-sender requirements, and recommends SPF, DKIM, and DMARC. Do not copy a random DNS record: it must reflect your actual sending services. Use genuine, low-volume correspondence, monitor failures, and avoid fake engagement or domain rotation to evade filters. Authentication improves trust in the sender; it does not make an irrelevant message welcome. [C2](references.md#c2)

## Use research and CRM tools after the manual process works

Hunter supports company discovery, business contact finding, and email verification. Use it when finding the right professional address is genuinely slowing a carefully selected list. Verification statuses describe deliverability estimates, not buyer interest or consent; accept-all domains remain uncertain. Apollo combines account and contact data with prospecting workflows. Its broader feature set may suit repeated B2B account research, but it can be excessive for your first twenty prospects. Start with company sites and one clearly defined segment. [T1](references.md#t1), [T2](references.md#t2)

Attio is an example of a dedicated CRM with workflow features. It becomes useful when multiple follow-ups, relationships, and offers make a flat file hard to manage. You do not need it merely because modern sales teams use CRMs. Your purchase test is practical: which missed action will it prevent, how much time will it save, and can you export the records? A basic table that you review weekly is better than a sophisticated CRM you never maintain. [T3](references.md#t3)

A lean workflow is: research an account manually, record the evidence and hypothesis, draft a message, review the facts, send through your normal business account, and record the next action. Automate reminders and duplicate checks first. Keep human review before external messages. AI can summarize a public page or improve a draft, but it should not invent a reason to contact someone, infer private circumstances, or claim you watched a demo you did not watch. The source URL and your observation remain the grounding evidence.

## Use LinkedIn as evidence and relationship support

You want to grow on LinkedIn, and freelance work can give that effort a clear commercial focus. Build a profile that says what you do, show a relevant demo, and publish explanations that a buyer or referral partner can understand. You do not need to imitate another creator's exact voice or chase broad developer engagement. Your strongest differentiator is showing a useful thing working and explaining the decisions behind it.

A four-piece content sequence could show a browser task before and after, explain when an official integration is better than an extension, walk through a small web flow with failure handling, and describe a handover checklist. These are examples of topics, not scheduled posts or material published on your behalf. Each can end with a relevant invitation: “If your agency needs a scoped browser integration, I am open to discussing a pilot.” Keep the invitation connected to the example.

Respond helpfully to real questions in communities where participation rules allow it. Do not treat every technical question as permission for a sales pitch. A useful answer can establish credibility, after which a person may ask about paid help. Keep private conversations private and obtain approval before using a client story publicly. One precise tutorial can support many introductions over time, but content remains a supporting acquisition channel until it produces relevant inquiries.

## Compare alternatives to Upwork and Fiverr

There is no universally better freelance site. Platforms differ in the type of work, selection process, availability expectations, fees, and ownership of the customer relationship. Your first priority is independent lead generation, so use one platform as a supplemental experiment rather than joining six. Evaluate total time to a qualified conversation, fit with your available hours, net economics, and repeat-customer potential. A zero-commission claim is only one part of that comparison.

## Measure a funnel without pretending small samples are certainty

Define stages consistently. An account researched is one that fits your selected hypothesis. A contact attempt is a reviewed message sent to an appropriate business route. A positive reply expresses relevant interest. A qualified conversation establishes a problem, owner, possible budget, timing, and access. A paid engagement requires the agreed commercial commitment. You can add finer stages later, but do not change definitions midweek to make the numbers look better.

Imagine researching twenty-five accounts, contacting fifteen, receiving three relevant replies, holding two fit calls, and selling one diagnostic. That is a hypothetical teaching example, not a benchmark. The account-to-contact gap may be healthy filtering. The reply and sales counts are too small for confident predictions. Use the notes to learn why people responded. If the only buyer was a warm referral, do not assume fifteen cold messages will reliably create another sale.

Track acquisition hours as well as counts. Suppose six hours of research and outreach produce a $450 diagnostic that takes four additional hours. The initial gross return is $45 per combined hour before expenses and taxes. A later implementation may improve the relationship economics, but it is not guaranteed. This calculation helps compare channels honestly. A marketplace with fees can be better than direct outreach if it reduces sales time sufficiently; a direct channel can be better when referrals and repeat work compound.

## Diagnose where the pipeline is breaking

If you cannot find relevant accounts, your buyer definition may be too vague or too narrow. Change the source or refine the workflow hypothesis. If you find accounts but cannot identify an owner, test a different company size or a partner route. If messages receive no replies, inspect relevance, deliverability, and whether the requested next step is too large. Do not immediately conclude that your prices are wrong when nobody has yet seen an actual proposal.

If conversations happen but no one has urgency, your observed signal may be weak. Ask what the team currently does, what it costs in effort, and what would make it worth changing. If people want the result but cannot buy, speak to the actual owner or target a different customer profile. If proposals repeatedly stall, review discovery, risk, scope, and decision process. Chapter 3 explains these sales steps. A pipeline is useful because each failure points to a different intervention.

Do not change every variable at once. Run a small, coherent test around one buyer, one offer, one proof piece, and a consistent message structure. Keep individualized observations truthful. After roughly twenty well-researched approaches or several meaningful conversations, review patterns. That threshold is a planning checkpoint, not statistical proof. If the fit is clearly wrong sooner, adapt; persistence should mean disciplined learning, not mechanically continuing an irrelevant campaign.

## Your first thirty days of acquisition

In the first week, prepare the service statement and two proof pieces from chapter 1. List ten genuine relationships and ten potential agency accounts. Research enough to identify which are worth contacting. Ask for a few relevant introductions and begin several agency fit conversations. Keep the target small enough to complete while working on your actual commitments. This is a proposed operating plan, not a claim that any messages have been sent.

In the second week, add a direct-buyer list around one workflow. Observe public processes, speak to people where appropriate, and draft tailored messages. Publish one short, useful demonstration if you can do it without delaying conversations. Spend more time asking about real work than adjusting your brand colors. By the end of the week, you want evidence of repeated pain and objections, not merely a polished profile.

In the third week, qualify the most relevant conversations and propose paid discovery where investigation is needed. Follow up on open decisions with context. If no meaningful interest exists, review the buyer and offer before expanding volume. In the fourth week, deliver a small engagement if sold, request feedback, and review acquisition time. If no sale has occurred, the outcome is still diagnostic: identify which stage lacks evidence and plan the next focused test. No specific revenue is promised by this schedule.

## A weekly routine you can sustain

A ten-hour example allocates two hours to prospect research, one hour to introductions and follow-ups, one hour to proof or content, five hours to delivery or paid discovery, and one hour to administration and review. During a delivery deadline you may temporarily shift time, but preserve a small acquisition habit. Otherwise every project ends with an empty pipeline and pressure to accept the next unsuitable request. Update the allocation when your true capacity and service economics are known.

Keep a review note with three questions: which conversations revealed an important problem, which action is next for each active opportunity, and what evidence would justify changing the offer? This is not a motivational scorecard. It is a practical way to avoid both random activity and endless preparation. Once your process produces customers, you can automate reminders, standardize research fields, and refine content around the objections you actually hear.

## A detailed prospect research walkthrough

Suppose you find a small studio in a partner directory. Its official site describes custom websites and integrations for education companies. Record that observation with the page link. Do not write “needs an extension developer”; nothing has established that. Your hypothesis is narrower: a studio serving education customers may occasionally encounter browser or application integration requests. Check whether the site lists a collaboration route or independent developer call. If it does, follow those instructions rather than a generic contact script.

Next inspect one relevant case study. You are looking for the kinds of systems it works with, its project scale, and the vocabulary it uses to describe outcomes. If the studio only does visual design and explicitly avoids development, your role may be a complementary implementation partner. If it already has a large extension team, your current offer may be less distinctive. Either situation can produce a useful conclusion; research is allowed to disqualify an account.

Choose the proof piece most closely related to the potential collaboration. Loolite may demonstrate browser media capability; YouTube Bookmarker may demonstrate user interaction, persistence, and export. Neither proves you can meet every agency's process, so mention your proposed delivery method: a scoped task, progress demo, code review, and handover. Draft the message, check every claim, and send only through an appropriate route if you decide to pursue the account. Then record the next action rather than refreshing your inbox repeatedly.

If the owner replies, “We occasionally need this but have nothing now,” that is a relationship, not a forecast. Ask whether you may send a concise capability note and whether there is a particular type of project they would want you to flag. Do not invent a deal value or schedule monthly sales calls without agreement. A relevant relationship can mature later, but your current income plan should rely on actual commitments rather than optimistic pipeline arithmetic.
