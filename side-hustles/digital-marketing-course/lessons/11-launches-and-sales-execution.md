# 11. Launch planning, sales execution, and evergreen selling

[Previous](10-partnerships-affiliates-and-paid-acquisition.md) · [Course home](../README.md) · [Next: retention and the capstone](12-retention-scaling-and-capstone.md)

## A launch coordinates a clear invitation

A launch is a period in which you make a specific offer visible and help suitable people decide. It is not a requirement to create artificial urgency or promise a large revenue event. For a first product, a launch is also a test of delivery, expectations, customer fit, and your ability to operate the system.

By now you have a buyer hypothesis, offer specification, sample, sales page, content ideas, email drafts, and measurement plan. This lesson integrates them into a sequence. The aim is to reduce avoidable operational failures and make the result interpretable.

You can launch a small guide without a huge audience. You still need relevant distribution. “No audience required” is misleading if it hides the work of finding people who have the problem and inviting them appropriately.

## Choose the appropriate release model

An evergreen guide remains available continuously. A cohort course has real scheduling and capacity constraints. A preorder collects payment before delivery under explicit terms. A pilot limits the scope so you can learn without making an oversized commitment.

| Model | Reason to use it | Information the buyer needs | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Evergreen guide | Self-paced access at any time | Current edition, delivery, updates, support | Neglected maintenance or unclear ongoing expectations |
| Workshop | Live demonstration and questions | Date, duration, prerequisites, capacity, cancellation terms | Scheduling and delivery failure |
| Cohort | Shared progression and feedback | Schedule, workload, feedback scope, access | Support capacity exceeds the promise |
| Preorder | Validate demand for a defined future product | What exists, delivery plan, cancellation/refund arrangements | Selling an undefined or delayed promise |
| Pilot | Learn from a smaller real release | Limited scope and explicit pilot status | Calling an incomplete experience a finished comprehensive course |

Choose based on how the product is delivered. A downloadable file does not become scarce merely because a marketing calendar ends. A workshop's seat limit can be real if it reflects your actual ability to teach and answer questions.

## Define launch objectives that produce learning

Revenue is a useful objective, but it is incomplete. A first launch can also evaluate whether relevant people understand the offer, whether sample users want the complete product, whether checkout and access work, and whether buyers can begin without confusion.

Use a few explicit hypotheses. For example: “The guide's prerequisites are clear enough that beginner buyers do not repeatedly discover an unexpected knowledge gap.” “The sample accurately represents the paid material.” “The delivery email gets buyers to the first lab.” These hypotheses connect marketing to the product experience.

Set financial planning scenarios rather than one emotionally loaded target. You might model five, fifteen, and thirty orders with the same cost assumptions and calculate the resulting workload and contribution. The scenarios are not probabilities unless you have evidence to estimate them.

Do not add a public sales target merely to create pressure. The purpose of the launch is to build a business and learn, not to force strangers to validate a personal milestone.

## Prepare the offer before increasing attention

A readiness review should test the complete path. Open the public page in a fresh browser context. Inspect the sample, price, prerequisites, delivery details, and policies. Complete a test transaction using the platform's supported testing process or a controlled purchase where appropriate. Check payment confirmation, access, receipts, and refund handling.

Test failure paths too. What happens when payment fails? What does a buyer see if a download link expires? How do they contact you? Does an unsubscribed newsletter address still receive necessary purchase access through the appropriate transactional route? Does buying stop the relevant sales sequence?

Check the mobile experience. A sales page can look excellent on your desktop while its purchase button or sample preview is difficult to use on a phone. Ask someone unfamiliar with the product to explain what they expect after paying.

Read every promise as an operational commitment. If the page says “personal feedback,” define the amount and response conditions. If the offer says “updates,” define which updates and for how long. Launch attention magnifies ambiguity as well as value.

## Use prelaunch to improve understanding

Prelaunch content can teach the problem, show the product's approach, and invite interested people to inspect a sample. It should not consist entirely of vague announcements that something exciting is coming.

A practical sequence might start with a debugging problem, follow with a worked demonstration, show the representative chapter, explain the learning path, and invite people to the release. Each piece should be useful independently. If nobody buys, the educational material should still have helped relevant readers.

You can invite a small pilot group to review the experience, but describe their role accurately. Free reviewers are not automatically paying customers. Permission to give private feedback does not automatically permit public testimonials. If you later publish an endorsement, preserve context and required disclosures.

Collect questions during prelaunch. Repeated confusion about difficulty, format, or scope should change the sales page before the main invitation. Treat the questions as design feedback, not obstacles to defeat.

## A four-week example launch plan

The plan below is an optional operating sequence. You can stretch it around your available time. It is not a claim that four weeks is enough to build every course.

**Week 1: establish readiness.** Finalize the core offer, verify examples, prepare the sample, and test checkout and access. Interview a few relevant readers about what they think the page promises. Fix material misunderstandings.

**Week 2: demonstrate relevance.** Publish one problem explanation and one useful demo. Offer the sample through a clear signup or direct public link, depending on your chosen design. Start the welcome sequence only under accurate expectations.

**Week 3: make the offer.** Publish a product walkthrough that shows actual contents. Send a clear offer message to the appropriate subscribed audience. Explain fit, price, delivery, and limitations. Answer real questions without creating private pressure campaigns.

**Week 4: support and review.** Help buyers access and start the product. Investigate refunds and repeated questions. Compare results with your hypotheses. Publish a truthful update or continued invitation if the product remains available.

If you run a real scheduled cohort, include its actual enrollment deadline and capacity. If the guide remains evergreen, say so. A promotional discount can have a real end date without pretending that the product itself will disappear; state the distinction clearly.

## Handle sales conversations with clarity

A potential buyer may ask whether the guide fits their level, solves a particular problem, or permits team use. Answer the question directly. If the product does not fit, say so and suggest a relevant alternative where useful.

You do not need a manipulative closing script. A useful reply is: “The guide assumes you already know basic coroutine builders. It covers the cancellation and exception examples listed on the page, but it does not review your production code. The sample should help you judge whether the level is right.”

For team requests, collect the minimum information needed to define scope: learner level, desired tasks, number of seats, delivery format, timing, and constraints. Do not casually promise custom training at the individual guide price.

Record recurring questions in a shared internal list so the page and onboarding can improve. If you answer the same question ten times, the information may belong before checkout.

## Manage launch day as operations

Schedule time to check access and respond to buyer issues. Keep one authoritative list of the current offer, price, terms, and public destinations. If you revise a material detail, update the relevant pages and messages consistently.

Monitor actual payment records and delivery health. A spike in clicks is less urgent than a broken download for someone who paid. Prioritize fulfillment before experimenting with more promotional messages.

Prepare a simple incident response. If the file link breaks, acknowledge the access issue to affected buyers through the appropriate route, provide a verified alternative, and fix the cause. Do not continue promoting a product as immediately accessible if you know delivery is unavailable.

If sales are slow, resist changing the price, headline, sample, and audience simultaneously. First inspect whether the intended people actually received the invitation and whether the buying path works. A first launch may simply have insufficient relevant exposure to support a conclusion.

## Conduct a useful postlaunch review

Review four layers: distribution, decision, delivery, and outcome. Distribution asks who encountered the offer. Decision asks whether they understood and chose it. Delivery asks whether purchase and access worked. Outcome asks whether buyers received the promised value.

Use both numbers and accounts. “Twelve sales” is incomplete without price, costs, audience, refunds, and timeframe. “Everyone loved it” is incomplete without actual feedback and knowledge of who responded.

A useful review might say: “The campaign produced 400 qualified product-page visits and eight purchases in the reporting window. Three buyers asked the same setup question, and one refunded because prerequisites were higher than expected. We will improve the setup guide and clarify prerequisites before increasing distribution.” These are fictional numbers illustrating a decision-oriented report.

Do not interpret a weak launch as a verdict on your worth or an automatic need for a larger product. It may reveal a narrower problem: insufficient fit, unclear messaging, limited exposure, or a delivery issue.

## Turn the launch into an evergreen system

After launch, retain useful educational assets, the sample, the sales page, and the email sequence. Remove language that is no longer true, including expired dates or pilot descriptions. Keep genuine edition information and update policies current.

Build a recurring routine: publish or refresh useful content, answer relevant questions, monitor access and support, inspect contribution, and improve the most important bottleneck. You can run future workshops or releases around observed needs without constantly inventing a new launch event.

An evergreen system is still active work. Its advantage is that existing assets can keep helping buyers between promotional periods. Maintenance, customer support, and distribution remain necessary.

## Optional application: run a private rehearsal

Use the launch checklist and perform the complete buying experience in a controlled setting. Ask a relevant person to describe the offer, inspect the sample, and follow the intended path. Record failures and fix them. Then write a bounded launch plan with actual dates and responsibilities for yourself.

The course authoring process has not launched or published your product. You decide when your offer and operations are ready. A successful rehearsal leaves you with a concrete invitation you can fulfill, rather than a calendar full of promotions attached to an uncertain product.
