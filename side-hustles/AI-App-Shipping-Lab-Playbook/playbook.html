2 Understand the mechanism before executing the scripts

A funnel is a sequence of useful decisions. It is not a collection of social accounts.

A short demonstration helps a developer recognize a problem. A substantive explanation earns trust. A free checklist lets them inspect their own work. A workshop demonstrates your teaching. The paid sprint supplies a longer sequence, feedback, and accountability. Member outcomes provide evidence for future buyers.

Your campaign follows one thread:

The prototype works on one example. Can you explain and test what happens on the next ten?

That question creates demand for diagnosis, then naturally connects to the paid product. It avoids inventing a fear or promising income.

The four kinds of content

Recognition content names a familiar situation: the app looks finished but breaks on an incomplete input.

Teaching content explains a small mechanism: a response can match a schema while still inventing a requirement.

Evidence content shows the actual artifact, test result, or revision. Label demonstrations and synthetic examples clearly.

Invitation content explains the next step, who it suits, the price, and the commitments. It should make the decision easier, including deciding not to buy.

One asset can serve several jobs, but give it one primary call to action. The first month’s default is the free AI App Shipping Checklist. Later emails and the workshop introduce membership.

Level Concrete value

Free checklist A way to assess a project and choose the next fix

Paid membership A coherent project sequence, assignments, bounded reviews, and a facilitated working group

You do not need to make the free material confusing to sell the paid experience. The paid offer helps people apply knowledge to a larger project with feedback.

The two feedback loops

The business loop is relevant visitors, consented leads, workshop participants, paying members, renewals, and contribution after costs.

The learning loop is assignment, attempt, feedback, revision, and independent explanation.

If the first loop works while the second fails, you have a quality problem. If the second works while the first fails, inspect positioning and distribution.

For every campaign, ask what behavior the asset should cause and what evidence would support that explanation. A successful post produces relevant next steps, not merely an attractive impression count.

4 Build the reference project that powers the campaign

The Client Brief Copilot

The input is a fictional client’s rough notes about a small website or internal tool. The output is an editable draft containing project goal, intended users, stated requirements, open questions, assumptions requiring confirmation, and an explicitly unapproved status.

The application must not silently convert guesses into client commitments. It should preserve uncertainty and require human review before export.

Use one supported stack: TypeScript, a React-based interface, a server endpoint for model access, and your familiar test runner. The exact framework is less important than a reference you can reliably explain. Keep provider credentials on the server. Support a deterministic mock response for lessons and demos.

Do not begin with autonomous agents, payments, multiple cloud providers, document uploads, or a marketplace. Those features add work without improving the first learning outcome.

Build it in this order

  1. Write the fictional user story and output contract.
  2. Create ten synthetic input cases before prompting a model.
  3. Make a deterministic mock that renders a draft in the interface.
  4. Add edit, approve, and export behavior.
  5. Build the server boundary and request validation.
  6. Add model access behind the same interface.
  7. Validate returned structure and handle invalid responses.
  8. Add timeouts, a retry boundary, a usage limit, and safe error messages.
  9. Run the same cases, record failures, and make one justified improvement.
  10. Deploy a training version, verify it from a fresh browser, and document setup and limitations.

These are production tasks for your reference project, not a claim that a complete codebase has been delivered with this report.

5 The exact paid curriculum to produce

Create six modules with three lessons each. Record explanations in short segments and use written notes for details that change. Target roughly 10 to 20 minutes per recorded lesson, with exercises taking longer. These durations are production budgets, not required viewing lengths.

The first cohort can be taught live while you improve the materials. Before taking payment, have the reference project, the whole roadmap, week one, and the stated delivery dates ready. Tell buyers which lessons are live and which recordings already exist.

Module one Specify the behavior

Lesson 1 is “Define done before asking AI to code.” Explain the user, input, output, uncertainty, and acceptance criteria. Demonstrate the difference between “make an AI assistant” and a bounded brief-drafting workflow. Assignment: write the one-page project specification below. Acceptance: another learner can tell what is in and out of scope.

Lesson 2 is “Map the interface and server boundary.” Draw the request moving from the interface to a server endpoint, then to the model, back through validation, and into a draft. Use the mock path first. Assignment: implement or modify the mock and trace one request. Acceptance: the learner explains where credentials, validation, and approval belong.

Lesson 3 is “Create examples before implementation.” Work through C01, C02, and C03. Explain why missing information and contradiction are different problems. Assignment: add two original cases with expected behavior. Acceptance: expectations are written before observing the model response.

Module two Direct the coding assistant

Lesson 4 is “Give the assistant a small contract.” Show a request that supplies the existing file boundary, required behavior, non-goals, and tests. Assignment: ask for one small change and inspect the diff. Acceptance: the learner can explain every changed file.

Lesson 5 is “Review a diff instead of approving a story.” Demonstrate how a plausible explanation can accompany a wrong change. Compare actual behavior against the acceptance criteria. Assignment: find an intentionally seeded bug in a teaching branch. Acceptance: explain the bug, produce a minimal correction, and show the relevant check.

Lesson 6 is “Make the output editable and unapproved.” Build the draft editor and approval state. Assignment: edit one requirement and verify the exported result. Acceptance: changed content does not retain an inappropriate approval state.

Module three Treat model output as data

Lesson 7 is “Validate shape and meaning separately.” Show a structurally valid response with an unsupported requirement. Assignment: distinguish schema checks from semantic review in three examples. Acceptance: the learner identifies both kinds of failure.

Lesson 8 is “Represent uncertainty explicitly.” Demonstrate unknown fields and open questions. Assignment: prevent a missing budget or deadline from becoming an invented commitment. Acceptance: the interface visibly distinguishes provided information, assumptions, and questions.

Lesson 9 is “Handle failure without endless generation.” Work through malformed output and timeout behavior. Assignment: add bounded recovery and a useful message. Acceptance: retries are limited and a user can recover without seeing secrets or internal traces.

Module four Evaluate the same workflow

Lesson 10 is “Build a small repeatable evaluation.” Record results for the ten cases using a fixed rubric. Assignment: evaluate a baseline version. Acceptance: results include inputs, version, expected behavior, and observed behavior.

Lesson 11 is “Change one thing and compare.” Modify a prompt, validation rule, or interface behavior to address a known failure. Assignment: rerun the same cases and describe tradeoffs. Acceptance: no claim of improvement without comparable observations.

Lesson 12 is “Exercise untrusted and sensitive input.” Use synthetic examples and distinguish a demonstration from a security guarantee. Assignment: document where content is trusted, stored, sent, and logged. Acceptance: no private data or credentials appear in the teaching artifacts.

Module five Prepare a controlled deployment

Lesson 13 is “Protect the request boundary.” Review server-side configuration, input size, usage controls, and duplicate actions. Assignment: demonstrate the configured boundary. Acceptance: the reference app behaves as documented when the limit is reached.

Lesson 14 is “Make errors observable without exposing content.” Choose a few useful operational events and redact unnecessary information. Assignment: diagnose a simulated failure from a safe event trail. Acceptance: the learner can identify the failing stage.

Lesson 15 is “Deploy and verify from scratch.” Use one supported training deployment. Assignment: run the setup and verification checklist from a fresh environment. Acceptance: the README is sufficient for another person to reproduce the training app.

Module six Demonstrate independent ability

Lesson 16 is “Extend the workflow without copying.” Give each learner a small variation, such as a different brief type or export format. Assignment: implement it with the same boundaries. Acceptance: explain the new behavior and its checks.

Lesson 17 is “Write the handoff and limitations.” Produce setup notes, supported inputs, known failures, data handling, cost controls, and maintenance notes. Assignment: a one-page handoff. Acceptance: a reader understands what the app can and cannot do.

Lesson 18 is “Demo defend and revise.” Each member demonstrates the app, handles a failure, explains a tradeoff, and receives feedback. Assignment: revise one issue and record a short final explanation. Acceptance: meet the rubric rather than merely watch every lesson.

The exact first lesson text

Use this as the written lesson and the basis of your first recording:

Before we ask an assistant to generate code, we need a way to decide whether the code did the job.

Our application takes rough client notes and produces a draft brief. That sounds simple until we inspect an incomplete note. If the client did not specify a deadline, what should appear in the deadline field?

A fluent model may fill the gap with something plausible. For this workflow, that is a failure. The correct behavior is to preserve the uncertainty and ask a question.

Write the user first: a developer organizing an initial client conversation. Write the input: rough notes supplied with permission, or synthetic notes in this course. Write the output: a draft the developer can edit. Write the boundary: the application does not approve scope, give a binding quote, or send anything to the client automatically.

Now create three examples. The first contains enough detail for a useful draft. The second contains almost no detail. The third contains a contradiction. For each, write what a useful response should do before you run a model.

Notice that a test expectation does not have to prescribe the exact sentence. It can require that the response identify the contradiction, leave an unknown field empty, or remain unapproved.

This is the first part of engineering an AI feature: deciding which behavior you will accept, what evidence you will inspect, and what remains a human decision.

Your assignment is to write the specification below and add one example of your own. Do not implement a larger app yet. If you cannot state the intended behavior, more generated code will make the uncertainty harder to see.

The assignment template

Complete these sentences: “The user is… The user is trying to… The input is… The output is… Information the app must not invent includes… A human must review… The workflow deliberately excludes… A normal case is… A missing-information case is… A contradiction case is… I will know the first version works when…”

Submit one page and your four cases. A reviewer should ask a clarifying question rather than silently complete the learner’s product decisions.

The completion rubric

Score functionality, evaluation evidence, safe boundaries, explanation, and handoff from 0 to 2 each. Propose an 8 out of 10 completion threshold, with critical unresolved data or credential issues requiring correction.

A learner can revise. Completion is your educational assessment, not a professional certification. The final artifact should make the value of the membership visible without promising a job or income.

6 The free resource and the exact landing page

Your free resource is the AI App Shipping Checklist. Make it a short web page or downloadable document that can be used in 15 minutes. It should help someone diagnose a project even if they never buy.

The resource body

AI App Shipping Checklist

Use this on one small AI feature before inviting another person to rely on it. Mark each item Yes, No, or Unknown and write one next action.

  1. Can I name the user and the single job this feature performs?
  2. Can I explain the request path from the interface to the server and model?
  3. Are credentials kept out of distributed browser code and shared screenshots?
  4. Do I validate incoming requests and the returned response structure?
  5. Can the output be structurally valid while still being wrong, and have I checked for that?
  6. Does the interface preserve missing information rather than inventing facts?
  7. Do I have normal, incomplete, conflicting, and failure cases written down?
  8. Can the user recover from a timeout or malformed response?
  9. Are retries, duplicate requests, and usage bounded?
  10. Have I decided what data is stored, sent, and logged?
  11. Does a human review the result before any consequential action?
  12. Can another person run the setup and understand the limitations?

A Yes needs evidence. “The assistant said it handled that” is not evidence. Use a test, a direct inspection, or a documented manual check.

Choose the highest-impact No or Unknown. Write the expected behavior. Create the smallest example that exposes the problem. Make one change. Rerun the example and the earlier cases. Record what improved and what did not.

Example: a rough note says, “We might need accounts later.” A draft that states “Customer accounts are included” has turned uncertainty into scope. A useful revision keeps accounts as an open question until the user confirms them.

Your next action should be specific enough to complete: “Add a case where accounts are uncertain and verify the draft asks a question.” Avoid “make the app more reliable.”

This checklist is an educational starting point. Passing it does not establish suitability for every production, privacy, security, or regulated use case.

24 How to repeat this method for another niche

Your transferable skill is connecting a buyer’s recent problem to a useful demonstration, a teachable outcome, a coherent funnel, and sustainable delivery.

The ten-step method

  1. Choose a reachable person with a specific starting point.
  2. Ask about a recent attempt and its actual obstacle.
  3. Define a result that person can inspect.
  4. Confirm you can independently deliver and explain it.
  5. Build a small reference and an assessment.
  6. Create a free diagnostic that helps without requiring purchase.
  7. Demonstrate one mechanism publicly in the formats your audience uses.
  8. Invite a small number of suitable people into a clearly priced pilot.
  9. Measure learning, renewals, costs, and founder effort.
  10. Scale the parts that work and revise the weak link.

For a cloud community, the reference might be a recoverable training deployment and the free diagnostic a release checklist. For freelancing, it might be a scoped proposal and the free diagnostic an offer checklist. The structure transfers; the proof and instruction must change.

Turn one lesson into a campaign

Start with an observed obstacle. Explain the underlying principle. Show a small before-and-after behavior only if it actually occurred. Give the reader an exercise. Invite a next step that supplies more practice or feedback.

The long video demonstrates the mechanism. The article makes the reasoning searchable and skimmable. The LinkedIn post connects it to a professional problem. The X post tests a concise explanation. The short video shows one visible moment. Email helps an interested person take the next appropriate step.

Repurposing means adapting the explanation to the medium. It does not mean pasting the same oversized text everywhere.

Evaluate an idea before making a large product

Write the buyer, recent trigger, result, current alternative, relevant proof, prerequisites, price, support limit, and acquisition route.

Then write what evidence would make you stop. For example: “After ten suitable offers, fewer than two pay and the objections do not reveal a practical correction.” A stopping rule protects you from treating preparation time as proof of demand.

Do not use a high-value niche label as a substitute for observing buyer behavior. A small group can pay well when the outcome and feedback matter; a large audience can still have little willingness to buy.

Learn from the outcome of the campaign

After each intake, write five sentences: who bought, what they wanted, what they completed, why they stayed or left, and which source produced the best-fit members.

Use that record to choose your next project and public examples. This is how you become better at community creation rather than merely better at following one script.
