# 5. Content strategy, LinkedIn, and building a useful reputation

[Previous](04-copywriting-and-sales-pages.md) · [Course home](../README.md) · [Next: short video](06-short-video-and-recording.md)

## Content should demonstrate the work you want to be trusted for

If you want to sell coding education, useful content lets people experience your teaching before paying. A well-explained bug, a small working demo, or a careful comparison can show both subject knowledge and communication skill.

Posting about every trending technology may attract attention without establishing a clear reason to buy your guide. At the other extreme, repeating product advertisements gives people little reason to follow. A content strategy connects buyer questions, your expertise, the product's scope, and sustainable production.

This lesson assumes that you know your buyer and have a specific offer. It teaches a practical content system with LinkedIn as the initial social channel. The principles also apply to other platforms, but their interfaces and distribution behavior differ. No schedule or format in this lesson guarantees reach.

## Separate business outcomes from platform metrics

Impressions show exposure, not necessarily unique interested people. Reactions suggest some form of response, not willingness to purchase. Followers can create future distribution opportunities, but a large count does not reveal fit or trust.

Useful business signals include relevant replies, sample usage, qualified email subscriptions, product-page visits, purchases, and successful customer use. Even these need context. A free giveaway can generate many subscriptions from people who only want unrelated freebies.

Define the job of each piece of content before judging it. A tutorial may build trust and receive saved bookmarks. A product walkthrough may generate fewer reactions but more qualified visits. An audience conversation may produce research insights rather than immediate sales. Treating every post as a direct-response advertisement makes these functions invisible.

A practical measurement note for each post contains its intended buyer, topic, format, next step, and observed result. Avoid assigning meaning to tiny differences between posts that differ in topic, audience exposure, and timing.

## Choose content pillars from buyer problems

A content pillar is a recurring area of useful coverage. It should be broad enough to generate several examples and narrow enough to reinforce your positioning.

For Coroutine Field Guide, possible pillars are debugging explanations, application demos, learning approaches, and product development evidence. Under debugging, you can explain scope ownership, trace cancellation, and compare exception cases. Under demos, you can show a screen closing during a request or a retry flow that behaves unexpectedly.

Product development evidence includes showing why you revised a diagram, how you tested a lab, or what you learned from readers. It should reveal useful reasoning rather than simply announce that you worked hard.

A practical mix might include one explanation, one demo, and one question-driven discussion per week. This is a starting workload hypothesis, not a platform requirement. If you can create only one excellent piece per week, build a system around that capacity. Consistency means a reliable process you can sustain, not publishing at a universal daily quota.

## Use different formats for different teaching jobs

| Format | Useful teaching job | Common failure | Better approach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Text post | Explain one decision or misconception | Broad opinion without an example | Include a concrete case and consequence |
| Code image | Show a small comparison | Unreadable code or missing explanation | Enlarge the important lines and explain in text |
| Document or carousel | Guide through several related steps | Dense slides with decorative headings | Give each page one clear reasoning step |
| Short video | Show behavior or a workflow | Tiny full-screen editor and long intro | Frame the relevant area and begin with the problem |
| Long article | Resolve a complex question | Search-oriented filler | Use runnable cases and a clear answer path |
| Product walkthrough | Explain what buying delivers | Only a mockup and vague benefits | Show the actual chapter, lab, and delivery format |

A format is not a strategy. If the underlying topic is irrelevant to the buyer, repackaging it as video does not make it relevant.

## Write a complete technical post

Start with one recognizable problem. Explain the mechanism through a small example. Give the reader a useful conclusion. Add a next step only when it follows naturally from the content.

An illustrative draft:

> A coroutine can be cancelled while the screen that started it disappears.
>
> Before adding another error handler, draw the ownership relationship: which scope created the work, and when does that scope end?
>
> In a small demo, I compare the request while the screen stays open with the request after it closes. The logs make the lifecycle difference visible.
>
> The useful habit is to inspect ownership before treating every stopped operation as a network failure.
>
> I am developing a guide that explains these cases with runnable labs. The sample shows the same code-and-diagram approach.

This is a structure illustration, not a claim that a demo has been recorded. Your actual post must include or link the promised evidence and match the technical details of your example. Replace general descriptions with the exact tested behavior you are teaching.

The post gives value before the product mention. It also creates a coherent bridge: the paid guide expands the same teaching approach. The bridge matters more than attaching a generic “link in bio” to every unrelated idea.

## Make your profile an understandable destination

A visitor should quickly understand what you build, what you teach, and where to explore further. Your headline can combine developer identity with a focused teaching area. Your about section can explain the audience you serve, examples of work, and the product's purpose. Featured assets can include a representative sample and a strong project demonstration.

Avoid positioning yourself as an expert in every adjacent field. A credible profile can say you are documenting and teaching a domain you have worked carefully through. If you are still learning a topic, make that context clear rather than manufacturing seniority.

Use an actual destination for the next step. A profile that says “get my free sample” should link to a functioning sample page. If you change the product, update the profile and old high-traffic destinations where practical.

Do not include private employer code or confidential customer material in public demonstrations. Create representative examples you have the right to share.

## Distribute through participation

Distribution includes answering relevant questions, explaining your reasoning in discussions, collaborating with adjacent creators, and sharing material in communities where it is welcome. A thoughtful reply can show expertise more clearly than an isolated announcement.

Read the local rules before posting in a technical community. If self-promotion is restricted, participate in the permitted way. When someone asks a question, answer it directly rather than making a purchase the price of receiving help. Link your resource only when relevant and permitted, and disclose your connection to it.

Avoid generic automated comments and engagement groups. They can create activity without useful relationships. Your goal is to become recognizable for helpful judgment among people who encounter the relevant problem.

Direct messages can support real conversations when there is a reason and consent. Do not scrape member lists or send unsolicited sales sequences merely because someone liked a post. A specific reply to someone who requested your sample has a different context from mass outreach.

## Study creators without copying their identity

You can learn from creators' structure: how they introduce a problem, demonstrate a result, explain a tool, and invite discussion. Study several examples and record what is observable. Separate that observation from guesses about their revenue, algorithmic advantage, or audience demographics.

For each example, note the intended reader, opening, evidence, teaching sequence, visual design, and call to action. Then create an original piece using your own project, experiment, and explanation. Copying distinctive stories, code, or phrasing does not create a durable voice.

A creator's visible success may depend on an existing audience, a distribution network, years of prior work, or products you cannot observe. A popular post is evidence that the post received visible response; it is not proof of a universal growth formula. This course does not claim to have audited specific creators' accounts or sales.

Your voice develops through repeated decisions: what you choose to explain, how precisely you name uncertainty, which examples you build, and how you respond when corrected. A developer brand can be calm and technically careful. It does not need constant dramatic claims.

## Repurpose the idea, not just the file

Suppose you produce a useful article about cancellation. Extract one narrow misconception for a text post, one behavior for a video, a diagram for a document, and one practical lesson for the newsletter. Each version should stand on its own within the format's constraints.

A short video cannot comfortably carry every caveat in a long article. Narrow the claim rather than removing necessary context. A text post can link to a deeper explanation. A newsletter can connect the concept to the reader's existing learning sequence.

Keep an evidence asset folder: tested code, original diagrams, screenshots of your own demonstrations, source notes, and sample explanations. This reduces the cost of producing accurate content while preserving consistency. Repurposing becomes efficient because the reasoning has already been done.

## Build a production routine

Use a small weekly cycle. Collect buyer questions and observations. Choose one or two ideas that match the offer. Produce the underlying example. Write or record the explanation. Check accuracy and readability. Publish through the actual platform interface. Respond to relevant comments. Record useful feedback and aggregate results.

Batch work when tasks share setup. You might record several short demonstrations after preparing one environment. Do not batch so aggressively that you lose the opportunity to incorporate feedback.

Maintain a content backlog with a reason for each idea. “A reader asked why a child cancelled” is stronger than “I need a post for Thursday.” You can plan dates, but the calendar should serve useful communication rather than manufacture topics.

## Interpret weak performance carefully

A quiet post may have reached few people, addressed the wrong problem, required too much context, or simply received little visible engagement. Before changing your entire niche, compare several pieces and inspect qualitative signals.

If relevant readers reply with detailed follow-up questions, the post may be doing useful work despite modest reach. If a broad opinion receives thousands of reactions but no relevant visits, it may have limited value for this product. Neither observation alone requires you to abandon the format.

Do not assert rules such as “external links always destroy reach” or “posting at one exact time guarantees distribution.” Treat timing, format, and placement as things you can investigate with your own audience. The platform's underlying distribution system is not fully visible to you.

## Optional application: build your first content sequence

Draft six connected pieces: two explanations, two demonstrations, one product-design insight, and one clear sample invitation. Give each piece a buyer question and a sensible next step. These counts are an optional production plan, not a success threshold.

Publish only when the examples and destinations are ready. Use the content planner in the workbook to track useful replies and qualified actions. The next lesson develops the video demonstrations so your work remains understandable on a phone and without sound.
