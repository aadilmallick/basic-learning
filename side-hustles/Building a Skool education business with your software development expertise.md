# 1 Business fundamentals you can reuse in any niche

## Customers buy progress in a particular situation

A topic is a category of knowledge. An offer is a useful change for a particular buyer. “AI education” is a topic. “Ship a document question-answering feature with a small evaluation set and a cost limit” is a project outcome. “Earn more as a developer” is a desire; it needs a credible path and evidence before it becomes a useful offer.

Define five things before you choose a community name:

- The buyer and their starting skills.

- The situation that makes the problem urgent.

- The result they can inspect.

- The obstacles your teaching and community remove.

- The time, money, and effort the result requires.

For an experienced JavaScript learner, the obstacle may be extension message passing and release preparation. For a freelancer, it may be scoping client work. For a beginner, it may be understanding asynchronous code. These buyers need different lessons and different support.

## Demand means behavior with a cost

People can sincerely like your idea without buying it. Stronger evidence includes spending money on a competing solution, repeatedly attempting the project, attending a relevant workshop, and purchasing your clearly described pilot. Record the difference between compliments, email signups, attended sessions, payments, completed projects, and renewals.

Ask about the last time the problem occurred. A learner who says “I should learn AI someday” is different from one who tried to build a tool last weekend, got stuck, and is looking for help now. Prioritize the second situation.

## Positioning creates a reason to choose you

A useful positioning sentence is: “For [specific people] who need [specific progress], this community provides [method and support], backed by [relevant proof].”

Your proof can be a functioning extension, a clear technical explanation, or a learner artifact. A long skill list is less persuasive because the buyer must work out how it helps them.

Differentiation should be a meaningful choice. You might focus on small tools that work reliably, transparent engineering tradeoffs, and learners explaining their own decisions. A competitor can also say “great community”; fewer can show your particular teaching process and reviewed artifacts.

## A paid community has several jobs

The curriculum explains what to do. Exercises build ability. Feedback identifies mistakes. Accountability helps learners return. Peers supply examples and useful relationships. Facilitation makes those interactions happen.

A library of videos can be a useful product, but recurring membership needs continuing value. After a member finishes the first project, what useful work happens next month? If you cannot answer that, sell a finite course or cohort and let members leave successfully.

## Value and willingness to pay are related but different

A skill might be valuable over a career while a learner has little current budget. A business owner might have budget but no time to study. The first buyer needs accessible teaching; the second may need an implementation service. Do not force both into one subscription.

You can estimate value by asking about time lost, current alternatives, missed deadlines, and the importance of finishing. Test willingness to pay by making a real offer. Avoid pricing from imagined salary increases or hypothetical client income.

## Distribution is part of the product business

A valuable community cannot grow if suitable people never encounter it. A channel works when it repeatedly reaches the right people at a tolerable cost. Your content, workshops, referrals, and partnerships are mechanisms for creating that contact and trust.

Marketing should make a useful promise, show evidence, invite a next step, and help people judge fit. It should also disqualify unsuitable buyers. Selling to someone without the prerequisites can raise refunds and consume delivery time.

## Retention is a consequence to investigate

People renew when the next period is worth its price. They leave because of poor fit, insufficient time, success and graduation, disappointing support, weak outcomes, or changing priorities. These require different responses.

You should not try to retain someone through guilt or hiding cancellation. A member who finishes, leaves satisfied, and recommends you can be more valuable than a disengaged subscriber.

## The complete business loop

A clear problem attracts relevant people. A specific offer earns purchases. Effective onboarding creates a first result. Good teaching produces larger results. With permission, those results become evidence that attracts the next suitable members. Renewal funds continued delivery.

Each part depends on the others. More traffic cannot repair a confusing curriculum. More lessons cannot repair an audience that does not want the outcome. Diagnose the stage where progress stops.

# 2 The numbers and terms to understand before scaling

Revenue is money earned from sales before expenses. Gross recurring membership revenue counts the recurring membership value before payment fees and delivery costs. Monthly recurring revenue, or MRR, normalizes recurring charges into monthly amounts. A $1,990 annual membership contributes about $165.83 in monthly recurring value; its $1,990 collection is cash received that month.

Keep one-time workshops, consulting, sponsorships, and affiliate income separate. They may fund the business, but they do not establish $200,000 in recurring Skool membership revenue. Sales taxes collected for authorities are not your operating revenue.

## The minimum operating formulas

- Ending paid members = starting paid members + new paid members + reactivations − lost paid members.

- Gross MRR = sum of each active paid subscription’s monthly equivalent.

- ARPU = gross MRR ÷ active paid members, using the same snapshot and definitions.

- Monthly member churn = lost members from the starting paid cohort ÷ starting paid members.

- First renewal rate = eligible first-time members who renew ÷ members whose first renewal came due.

- Cash CAC = acquisition spending ÷ new paying customers attributed to that spending.

- Contribution per member = membership revenue − payment costs − channel commission − refunds allowance − variable delivery costs.

- Contribution payback = cash CAC ÷ monthly contribution, as a rough approximation before allowing for churn.

- Gross revenue retention = (starting MRR − churned MRR − downgrades) ÷ starting MRR.

- Net revenue retention adds upgrades and expansion to that numerator.

Use a separate cohort measure for people who join and leave in the same month, rather than concealing them in a starting-cohort churn rate. Report cancellations scheduled for later and failed payments alongside the active paid snapshot.

A planning approximation is contribution lifetime value = monthly contribution ÷ monthly churn. It assumes constant churn and contribution and can be badly misleading for a young community. Prefer observed 90-day contribution, then 6- and 12-month cohorts. Never spend on ads based only on a flattering theoretical lifetime value.

## A simple example

Suppose you begin with 100 paid members, add 15, and lose 6 of the starting members. You end with 109 if there are no other changes. Starting-cohort member churn is 6%. At $149 for everyone, ending gross MRR is $16,241.

If you spent $600 to acquire the 15 new members, cash CAC is $40. If you spent 20 founder hours as well, those hours still matter. At an internal planning value of $60 an hour, fully loaded acquisition cost is ($600 + $1,200) ÷ 15 = $120.

A business can have positive cash flow and still pay you poorly for your time. Track cash profit and founder hours together.

## What your dashboard should prioritize

For acquisition, track qualified page visitors, qualified conversations, applications, paid starts, source, and cash CAC. For delivery, track onboarding completion, first useful artifact, project completion, review time, and unresolved blockers. For retention, track renewal eligibility, renewals, cancellations, and reasons.

Do not equate a page view with learning or an approved member with a current paying customer. Skool’s own analytics definitions include trials in member counts and certain failing payments in MRR. Reconcile platform statistics against your paid subscription and payout records. [Skool analytics definitions](https://help.skool.com/article/216-analytics-definitions)

Choose stable definitions for your own decisions. If your dashboard uses a different churn formula from Skool’s displayed retention, name the difference rather than comparing the numbers as though they were identical.

## Product and pricing

Test $79 to $99 per month for a study-oriented membership. A $149 tier can include a scheduled, bounded review once you know its cost. Explain the expected eight-week schedule and the likely number of monthly charges.

This idea may require more support for each dollar of revenue. It is attractive if you particularly enjoy foundational teaching and can reach learners through educational networks.

## The learning sequence

Teach one idea through a complete loop: a concrete problem, a small explanation, a worked example, a partially completed exercise, an independent variation, and an explanation by the learner.

Possible weekly themes are data flow, asynchronous behavior, API contracts, database modeling, testing, debugging, deployment, and design tradeoffs. Keep the initial application small so each lesson can use the same context.

Grade an independently modified feature rather than a copied final repository. Ask learners to predict behavior before running the program and revisit earlier concepts in later exercises.

## The marketing playbook

1. Choose one common misunderstanding, such as why an asynchronous operation finishes after a render.

2. Publish a short visual explanation and a debugging exercise.

3. Invite people to submit their reasoning, then show a clear worked solution.

4. Teach a free “explain and debug this small app” session.

5. Invite learners who meet the basic prerequisites into an eight-week project group.

6. Use your alumni or educational contacts as possible introduction paths. Ask organizers whether the workshop fits their audience.

7. Publish anonymous before-and-after examples of explanations with permission. Show how understanding improved, rather than relying only on attractive screenshots.

8. Ask graduates which next application skill they need. Build the next sprint around a recurring need.

9. Grow through teaching partnerships and learner recommendations before using paid acquisition.

The first 10 members should reveal the true prerequisite gaps. At 50, add a preparatory path with self-checks. At 100, use small peer groups and train reviewers in a shared rubric. At larger scale, separate entry levels rather than slowing every session to the least prepared member.

## Retention and the failure signal

Members can progress through applications, testing, architecture, collaboration, and maintenance. Successful graduation remains a valid outcome.

If learners repeatedly fail the first assignment, improve prerequisites and lesson scaffolding. If they can reproduce code but cannot explain it, reduce solution copying and add independent variations. If you want to promise employment support later, first build genuine employer relationships and a track record; keep that separate from the current learning outcome.

# 10 Community idea five Cloud and Production Readiness Lab

## Buyer and outcome

Target developers who can build an application locally but have difficulty deploying, diagnosing, and maintaining it. This is a strong later track if you have a reference system you can confidently operate.

The six-week result is a small deployed application with a deployment checklist, environment separation, monitoring, backup and recovery practice, and an incident runbook. The outcome is a reviewed training deployment. It does not certify that an application is suitable for every production or regulated environment.

## Product and pricing

Test $149 per month with at most 10 learners. Consider $249 for a bounded additional review tier after measuring support. Learners pay any hosting or cloud usage directly, with spending controls and clearly stated prerequisites.

Choose one supported deployment path per sprint. An AWS CDK and TypeScript teaching example would align with your résumé, provided you create it independently and keep it small. Multi-cloud breadth will increase maintenance and troubleshooting costs before you have evidence that learners need it.

## The teaching sequence

Week 1 maps application dependencies and cost exposure. Week 2 sets up a reproducible deployment process. Week 3 handles configuration and secret separation. Week 4 adds useful logs and a few meaningful alerts. Week 5 practices backup restoration and rollback in a training environment. Week 6 runs a simulated incident and a final explanation.

Each learner should demonstrate a recovery step. Screenshots of a healthy dashboard are weaker evidence than restoring a deliberately broken training deployment.

## The marketing playbook

1. Build a reference application using synthetic data.

2. Record a small failure, such as a missing configuration value, and demonstrate diagnosis and recovery.

3. Publish a practical release checklist and explain one common omission.

4. Hold a workshop on taking a local application to a controlled training deployment.

5. Invite developers with an existing small app into the pilot. Decline projects whose scope or sensitivity exceeds the teaching environment.

6. Partner with frontend educators whose learners get stuck at deployment.

7. Publish case studies about specific improvements: a successful restore, a reproducible release, or a clearer runbook. Avoid unmeasured uptime or savings claims.

8. Create recurring maintenance and incident exercises so members have a reason to practice again.

9. Hire or contract a qualified reviewer when unfamiliar infrastructure exceeds your expertise.

The first 10 are best acquired through developer relationships and one focused workshop. The next 40 can come from frontend education partners. At 100, enforce a supported-stack policy and add another reviewer before opening more office hours.

## Retention and the failure signal

Monthly value can come from operational drills, cost reviews, dependency updates, new deployment patterns, and architectural discussion. Members should practice on systems they are authorized to change.

If you spend most sessions troubleshooting unsupported environments, narrow the stack and scope. If learners expect you to operate their live businesses around the clock, the offer needs clearer boundaries or a separate professional service agreement. Keep this concept secondary until your first community has dependable operations.

# 11 Validate demand before building the full community

Your first task is to reduce three uncertainties: whether a specific learner wants the outcome, whether they will pay you to help, and whether you can deliver it within your available time.

## Days 1 through 3 Choose one testable offer

Write a one-page offer containing the audience, prerequisites, six-week result, schedule, support limits, monthly price, billing frequency, cancellation terms, and what exists today. Show a working reference demo. Clearly identify anything you will build during the pilot.

Start with the extension offer unless interviews reveal a stronger paid need. Build only the reference demo, a prerequisite self-check, a short first lesson, and the first assignment. Estimate preparation and review hours honestly.

List 30 possible interviewees across the extension, practical AI, and freelance delivery audiences. Seek 10 conversations per audience over the first two weeks, adjusting if one segment becomes clearly unsuitable. A relevant participant has recently attempted the kind of work you want to teach.

## Days 4 through 14 Conduct problem interviews

Ask for 20 minutes, explain that you are exploring a teaching offer, and make participation optional. Do not pretend the conversation is independent research if you intend to discuss your paid pilot.

Use these questions:

1. What were you trying to build or achieve most recently?

2. What happened the last time you tried?

3. Where exactly did you stop or lose time?

4. What have you already tried to fix it?

5. Have you paid for help, a course, a tool, or coaching? What did it cost?

6. What would a useful finished result look like?

7. Why does finishing matter now?

8. How much time can you consistently spend each week?

9. What would make a group program unsuitable?

10. What would you need to see before trusting someone to teach this?

11. After finishing the first project, what would you want help with next?

12. May I send you the pilot details if they match what you described?

Record the person’s own description, recent behavior, current alternatives, prerequisites, time available, and permitted follow-up. Do not treat hypothetical “yes, I would buy” answers as payments.

## Make a real offer and interpret the result

After enough conversations to find a coherent pattern, invite suitable people to inspect the pilot. Count offers made, payments received, declined offers, and reasons. A deposit is useful only if its terms, start date, refund conditions, and balance are clear. A full paid subscription start is stronger evidence than a vague reservation.

Suggested decision gates for a small pilot:

| Evidence | Decision |
| - | - |
| At least 5 of 10 qualified people offered the pilot pay | Deliver a small cohort and learn; this is still a small sample |
| 2 to 4 pay | Ask why the others declined, change one major factor, and test again |
| 0 or 1 pay | Pause content production and rework the segment, problem, proof, or offer |
| Buyers pay but cannot complete prerequisites | Fix admissions or create a prerequisite path |
| Buyers finish but see no second-month value | Prefer finite cohorts or redesign the recurring product |

The 5-of-10 rule is a local spending gate, not a forecast of a 50% public conversion rate. Warm relationships and interview selection make this sample unusually favorable.

Cap initial validation spending at $500 and roughly 20 preparation hours before five paid starts. If the pilot misses its minimum size, communicate the decision and honor the refund terms you published.

## Choose a business based on evidence

After 30 conversations or four weeks, compare the concepts using actual qualified offers, payments, delivery effort, and enjoyment. Do not keep interviewing indefinitely to avoid selling. Pick one for a six-week experiment.

A completed validation cycle produces a decision, not merely a list of interesting ideas. Write: “I will teach this person this outcome at this price using this format, because these buyers paid and these delivery assumptions appear feasible.”

# 12 Build a high quality learning experience

Quality means members can do something useful and can explain it. It also means the experience is organized, accurate, accessible, and sustainable for you to run.

## Define the assessment before recording lessons

For the first extension sprint, use a five-part rubric scored 0, 1, or 2 per part:

| Dimension | A score of 2 requires |
| - | - |
| Functionality | The intended workflow works on the stated supported page or test environment |
| Reliability | Defined failure cases are handled and the test checklist is reproducible |
| Security and privacy | Permissions and data handling are explained and unnecessary exposure is removed |
| Explanation | The learner can explain data flow and a design tradeoff |
| Delivery | Setup, demo, limitations, and release instructions are complete |

Set a proposed completion standard of at least 8 out of 10 with no unresolved critical security or privacy issue. Explain that this is your teaching rubric, not an industry certification. Allow a revision after feedback.

For an AI project, add an evaluation artifact and cost or usage controls. For a freelancing project, substitute offer clarity, prospect fit, scope quality, professional communication, and delivery readiness. The general principle is the same: inspect the work you promised to improve.

## Use a repeatable lesson design

Each lesson should state one outcome and its prerequisites. Start with the problem, explain the concept, demonstrate a small worked example, give a partial exercise, then ask the learner to complete a variation independently.

Finish with a self-check, a short explanation prompt, common mistakes, and a link to the next step. A concise written explanation and a short demonstration are often easier to maintain than a long lecture.

Use retrieval and revisiting as design choices: ask learners to explain yesterday’s idea without notes, then apply it in a different setting later. Treat these as practical teaching methods and judge whether your learners actually improve.

## Establish an achievable weekly rhythm

A 10- to 12-hour founder schedule can support approximately:

- 2 hours of lesson and reference-repository preparation.

- 1 hour of live group teaching.

- 1 hour of bounded reviews, roughly 15 reviews of four minutes each or a smaller number of deeper reviews.

- 1.5 hours answering organized questions across scheduled blocks.

- 3 hours of marketing and partner conversations.

- 1 hour of onboarding, metrics, and administration.

- 1 to 2 hours of contingency.

The four-minute review assumption is a triage review, not a thorough code audit. If pilot reviews take 15 minutes each, 15 monthly reviews take 3.75 hours per month. Adjust the schedule and member cap using observed effort. Do not promise a deep review every week to everyone at $99.

Record sessions with permission and provide a written summary and relevant timestamps. Rotate session times occasionally if demand justifies it; do not promise to cover every time zone.

## Organize the classroom around progress

Use a small number of courses or sections: Start Here, prerequisites, current sprint, reference lessons, troubleshooting, and past project examples. Keep the current path prominent and put optional material behind clear labels.

Start Here should answer: what happens this week, what should I submit, where do I ask a question, when will I hear back, and what does my plan include? An overwhelming archive can make the product harder to use.

Every starter repository needs a clean setup check, dependency notes, a known-good example, a limitations section, and a last-reviewed date. Recheck instructions before each sprint. Replace tool-specific setup without rewriting the underlying conceptual lesson.

## Make the community useful to other members

Give each member a brief introduction template: starting skill, project, available time, and first milestone. Pair people by compatible goals and schedules only with their agreement.

Use three structured weekly threads: project intention, blocker with a minimal reproduction, and demo with one lesson learned. Ask for useful peer responses, not generic encouragement. Praise clear reasoning and helpfulness rather than posting volume.

Use a question template: expected behavior, observed behavior, smallest example, error text, what was tried, and relevant version. Ask people to remove keys, private data, and client identifiers.

## Make the boundaries visible

Define business-day response windows, such as within two business days for scoped questions. State that emergency production support, unlimited private messages, full application audits, and done-for-you builds are outside the membership unless explicitly purchased and staffed.

For review tiers, define a maximum scope and time budget. For example, one 10-minute review of a small pull request per month is concrete. “Personal mentoring whenever you need it” is not.

Give written alternatives when video is inconvenient, captions where feasible, readable materials, and an asynchronous path for people who miss a session. Quality should not depend on being available for one live hour.

# 14 Marketing fundamentals applied to your business

## Build one coherent path

Your initial path should be: useful public demonstration, relevant free resource or workshop, clear paid offer, first member result, and permissioned proof.

Each step should help the same person with the same problem. A viral mathematics post may reach interesting people but contribute little to a paid extension sprint. It is fine to have broader interests; evaluate business content by relevant response and purchases.

A free resource should help someone make progress without requiring a large commitment. For your first offer, use a small extension starter plus a release checklist. Ask separately for consent to ongoing email. Receiving a download should not silently enroll someone in unrelated promotions.

## Match the message to buyer awareness

Someone unaware of the problem needs a recognizable demonstration. Someone trying to build already needs an explanation of the obstacle. Someone comparing paid programs needs curriculum, prerequisites, support boundaries, and proof. Someone ready to join needs unambiguous terms and a working checkout.

This is why repeating only “join my community” is ineffective. It does not supply the missing information at each stage.

## Understand the funnel with denominators

An illustrative monthly funnel is:

| Stage | Assumed rate | Result |
| - | - | - |
| Relevant content impressions | Starting quantity | 10,000 |
| Visits to the workshop or resource page | 2% of impressions | 200 |
| Consented leads | 30% of visitors | 60 |
| Workshop attendees | 40% of leads | 24 |
| New paid members | 15% of attendees | 3.6 expected |

This is arithmetic, not a benchmark. Real customers are whole people, and repeated impressions are not unique visitors. At $149, 3.6 expected starts represent $536.40 in new gross MRR before churn and costs.

The lesson is that considerable attention can yield few buyers. Improving a weak step can matter more than posting more. Track direct buyers separately from this workshop path so you do not count them twice.

## Use proof that matches the promise

For technical teaching, show the artifact, the learner’s starting point, the assignment, the feedback, and the resulting improvement. For freelance teaching, show actual process improvements and appropriately documented commercial outcomes.

A large GitHub repository does not establish that beginners can learn from it. A satisfied consulting client does not establish that students will get clients. Choose evidence for the exact claim you make.

## Learn through small experiments

For every marketing experiment, write the audience, message, channel, offer, expected mechanism, spending limit, duration, and decision metric. Change one important variable where feasible.

At low traffic, formal A/B tests can be inconclusive. Use interviews, objection patterns, and actual purchases before declaring a headline a winner from a few clicks.

Example: over two weeks, compare two demonstrations addressing different extension problems while keeping the paid offer constant. Count relevant conversations, resource visits, and paid starts. If one receives likes but no qualified interest, investigate audience mismatch.

## Build an asset you control

Keep your own domain, lesson and repository backups, a consented contact list, financial records, and documented operating procedures. Skool is the teaching and community environment; your entire ability to contact willing customers should not depend on one discovery feed.

Use native analytics and a simple source field at checkout or onboarding where available. Add a voluntary “how did you first hear about us?” question. This complements last-touch attribution rather than replacing it.

Use an email service with clear subscription and unsubscribe handling. Keep a separate record of what the person asked to receive.

2. Explain the most common setup failure and how to diagnose it.

3. Show a real example and its limitations.

4. Invite the reader to a workshop or the current project sprint.

5. Explain fit, schedule, price, billing, support, and how to decide.

Remove or adjust sales messages after someone buys. After the sequence, send one useful teaching update per week if that is what subscribers agreed to receive.

List 20 plausible hosts: JavaScript meetups, extension educators, bootcamp alumni groups, university organizations, and complementary developer newsletters. Inspect their audience before contacting them.

Ask satisfied members whether they know someone with the same problem. Give them a clear description to share. Keep referrals optional and separate from reviews.

Do not start with ads to an untested broad community. First obtain paid starts, inspect their first renewals, and understand contribution.

A first paid test might cap spend at $300 to $500, use one clear offer, and run long enough to observe paid starts and refunds. These are cash limits for learning, not a sufficient sample to prove scalability.

If a lead costs $12 and only 5% become paying members, acquisition cost is $240 before other sales costs. If your permitted CAC is $150, that path is unsuitable even if the lead count looks impressive. Fix the offer, audience, or economics before increasing spend.

Keep direct ads, affiliate referrals, and Growth Boost in separate acquisition cohorts. Do not assume the same customer’s acquisition costs are mutually exclusive unless the actual attribution and payment records establish that.

# 16 Pricing and packaging without creating an impossible workload

## Start with one understandable offer

Your founding extension sprint can use a $99 monthly subscription with 15 seats. State that the first project lasts six weeks and that the membership continues until cancelled. Tell buyers what happens after the sprint and let them decide whether to continue.

A finite alternative is a one-time six-week cohort with a defined access period and a separate optional alumni subscription. This is often easier to explain if the main value is finishing one project. Keep its one-time revenue out of MRR.

For the founding recurring offer, a simple delivery promise is one group session per week, the structured project path, scoped questions answered within two business days, and one bounded individual review per month. Adjust that promise before launch if your observed effort exceeds the time budget.

## Test prices in steps

Begin with the smallest credible paid offer, then change the new-member price when the evidence changes.

| Stage | Proposed price | Evidence needed before the next step |
| - | - | - |
| Founding sprint | $99 per month | At least 5 paid starts and feasible delivery |
| Improved project membership | $149 per month for new members | Finished projects, clearer onboarding, and first renewals |
| Established reviewed membership | $199 per month for new members | Several cohorts and sustainable review effort |
| Additional review tier | $499 per month with a hard capacity cap | Trained reviewers and demand for the additional support |

Do not raise prices simply because a date passes. Improve the offer and test whether new buyers accept it. Existing members’ actual prices must remain in your forecast.

A permanent founder discount can create a long-term margin burden. Offer only the small number you can sustain and specify the conditions, such as remaining continuously subscribed, consistently with current platform capabilities and terms. Never imply that existing subscribers will be automatically repriced later.

## Price the support before promising it

Estimate monthly reviewer minutes, instructor hours, and administrative cost. At $60 per reviewer hour, a 20-minute review costs $20 before scheduling and supervision. Two such reviews consume $40. One hour of individual time per member per week would exceed the entire monthly membership price at many of the proposed tiers.

A later $499 tier might include two 20-minute scoped reviews per month, a small-group clinic, and the main project membership. It should not include unlimited consulting or an exclusive hour with you every week.

The final offer must say who gives the review. If a trained coach will deliver it, do not sell it as guaranteed access to you.

## Annual pricing after the product is understood

Annual membership can improve cash collection and reduce billing frequency, but it also creates a year of service obligations and can conceal weak engagement.

For example, $199 monthly is $2,388 per year if paid for 12 months. An optional $1,990 annual plan is a $398 discount and contributes approximately $165.83 monthly recurring value, not $199.

Under the current documented higher Pro fee bracket, a $1,990 transaction would have a modeled fee of $77.91 at 3.9% plus $0.30, before commissions. Recheck actual fees and refund treatment before offering it.

Wait until you understand at least several monthly cohorts and have a credible year of ongoing value. Maintain enough cash to deliver annual commitments. Do not use annual collections as personal spending money.

## Trials and refunds

A trial is useful when a suitable prospect can experience the first result during it. A poor fit trial can produce large signup counts and few paying members.

For a first small cohort, an explicit paid pilot and clear initial refund policy may produce cleaner feedback. Later, test a trial against paid starts using activation, refunds, and 60-day contribution. Evaluate the full path, not the signup rate alone.

# 18 Retention and community operations

## Give members a reason to return

A useful monthly cycle is choose a project, build a small version, get feedback, show it, and select the next meaningful improvement. Repetition creates a familiar process while the work becomes more challenging.

Keep a rolling three-month project calendar. Explain which projects suit which skills. Let members help prioritize it through their actual problems, while you retain responsibility for a coherent learning sequence.

Do not make endless new content the only reason to stay. Feedback, peer learning, maintenance exercises, and access to a well-facilitated working group can be valuable recurring services.

## Track cohorts and outcomes

| First eligible monthly renewal | Seek at least 80% before broad acquisition | Interview leavers and improve recurring value |
| Scoped support response | At least 90% within the promised window | Reduce intake or add coverage |
| Refund requests | Investigate every one; use 5% as an escalation trigger | Fix message mismatch, fit, or quality |
| Founder delivery time | Stay inside the published capacity budget | Narrow support or train help |

With 15 members, one person changes a percentage by 6.7 points. Report counts with rates and use several cohorts before making large spending decisions.

An 80% first renewal threshold is an early warning gate, not good mature retention. As the product becomes established, test whether you can sustain substantially lower monthly churn, such as 4% to 6%, over several months. Do not present that range as an industry norm.

Even 4% monthly churn retains only about 61% of a starting cohort after 12 months if the rate stays constant. At 6%, it is about 48%; at 10%, about 28%. Acquisition must continually replace those losses.

## Find the cause before applying a fix

If people never begin, inspect onboarding and promises. If they start and stall, inspect scope, prerequisites, and feedback. If they finish and leave, investigate whether graduation is the right product design. If they stop because of time, offer a lighter path where it is genuinely useful.

Send a brief optional cancellation question: “What was the main reason, and what would have made the experience more useful?” Offer an appropriate remedy without making cancellation depend on a conversation.

Use win-back messages only when you have something meaningfully relevant to the reason they left and permission to contact them. Do not continually pressure satisfied graduates to return.

## Moderation and member trust

Publish rules against harassment, spam, plagiarism, unsolicited selling, and posting private data. Give moderators an escalation route and document serious actions. Explain where peer help is welcome and where a qualified instructor must review.

Separate community promotion from learning. Members should not have to read a sales pitch every time they open an assignment.

Build a small group of reliable peer contributors. Train and compensate recurring instructional work appropriately rather than assuming enthusiastic members will supply the support you sold.

## Review quality each month

Inspect a sample of reviewed work for consistency. Check whether advice was correct, actionable, and within scope. Update stale lessons and note meaningful changes.

Ask three useful questions: What did members become able to do? What blocked the people who did not finish? What delivery work repeatedly consumed more time than expected? Use the answers to set the next month’s priorities.

# 21 Grow through operating stages and hire for measured work

## Stage one Prove the first outcome

Start with 5 to 15 paying members. Your job is to teach, observe, and improve. Keep one acquisition offer, one supported project, and one weekly live session.

Advance when members complete meaningful work, your support promise is feasible, and some eligible members choose to renew for a clear next project. A full founding cohort with poor outcomes is a delivery problem to fix.

## Stage two Establish a repeatable small business

At roughly 30 to 100 paying members, maintain a stable project sequence and a consistent source of qualified prospects. At a $149 to $199 uniform price, 100 members would represent $14,900 to $19,900 gross MRR before discounts and costs.

Document onboarding, common questions, review rubrics, refunds, and lesson maintenance. Contract a small amount of administrative or editing help only when it removes a measured bottleneck. Keep founder teaching and customer understanding close.

Before accelerating acquisition, seek at least three completed intake cohorts, evidence that outcomes and renewals are holding, and a positive contribution margin. If cohorts overlap, define the comparison consistently.

## Stage three Train delivery beyond yourself

At approximately 100 to 300 members, your main constraint may become review and facilitation. Hire a part-time technical reviewer or community operator based on recorded workload.

Train the reviewer on real examples. Have both of you review the same small set of artifacts, compare feedback, and resolve inconsistent judgments. Supervise the first month and inspect samples thereafter.

Publish who provides each service. Preserve a visible founder role without promising personal attention to every member.

## Stage four Build an education operation

At roughly 300 to 700 members, assign responsibility for curriculum quality, community operations, acquisition, and financial reporting. One person may cover several roles, but every recurring job needs an owner and backup.

Add a second track inside the community only when there is paid demand and a clear path between skill levels. Maintain separate onboarding and events where appropriate. Avoid placing beginners and advanced learners into an undifferentiated feed.

Use source-level contribution and retention to allocate growth spending. Diversify only after a channel works; do not require five channels to succeed simultaneously.

## Stage five Test the route to the larger target

At 700 to 1,275 or more paying members, the business needs multiple reliable acquisition sources and enough trained teaching capacity. The exact member count depends on actual ARPU, including legacy and annual prices.

A possible $30,000 nonfounder monthly staffing budget is $6,000 for community operations, $7,500 each for two instructors or reviewers, $6,000 for content and growth operations, and $3,000 for fractional quality or specialist support. Add the modeled $12,000 founder compensation to reach $42,000.

## Check capacity with minutes of work

In the illustrative mature tier mix, one 10-minute monthly review for 450 members requires 75 reviewer hours. Two 20-minute monthly reviews for 75 premium members require another 50 hours. That is 125 review hours per month.

If asynchronous support averages eight minutes per member per month across 1,275 members, that adds 170 hours. Together that is 295 hours, or about 68 hours per week over an average month, before preparation, teaching sessions, moderation, management, and marketing.

The library tier must not promise the same individual reviews as higher tiers. Schedule reviews and intake so they do not all land on one day. Add capacity before the support queue breaches your promise.

## When a second community makes sense

Open a second paid community only if the buyer is meaningfully different, separate positioning improves acquisition, a dedicated lead can deliver it, and the first business has remained healthy without your constant intervention for about 90 days.

Require paid validation for the new community as though it were a new business. Separate its acquisition cost, retention, labor, and profit. Shared lessons do not make support free.

If you cannot explain why a new community is better than a track, stay with the track. Your broad interests can support a strong curriculum without becoming separate businesses immediately.

## A reasonable planning horizon

Use the first 90 days for validation and a working pilot. Use the next quarter to establish cohorts and channels if the pilot earns that investment. Beyond that, plan quarterly around measured acquisition, retention, and capacity.

Reaching $200,000 monthly gross revenue could take years, may require full-time leadership and a team, and may never be the most profitable choice. There is no evidence here supporting a promised date. Build an attractive small business at each stage.

## Avoid unsupported claims

Do not promise that learners will earn a particular income, get clients, get hired, or reach a skill level you have not defined and assessed. Your own $200,000 target belongs in internal planning, not in a student earnings pitch.

The FTC’s action involving Lurn illustrates the consequences of unfounded money-making claims in business coaching. Market the skills, support, and outcomes you can substantiate. ## Protect your material and other people’s information

Use teaching code and assets you own or are licensed to distribute. Keep employer work, confidential client information, and private repositories out of lessons unless you have explicit rights and permission.

Use synthetic data for demonstrations. Keep secrets out of distributed source and browser bundles. Explain data handling before asking learners to submit work. Obtain separate permission for public case studies.

Do not imply an affiliation with your employer, a tool vendor, or Skool that you do not have. A technical background supports your expertise; it does not grant permission to reuse someone else’s materials.

## Make charges understandable

Display the price, recurring frequency, expected program duration, support scope, cancellation route, and refund terms before purchase. An annual plan must visibly say annual. A six-week program billed monthly should explain the expected second charge.

Before launch, review current consumer requirements relevant to your buyers and your business. The operational aim is simple: buyers should know what they are agreeing to and be able to leave through the promised process.

## Limit platform dependence

Back up original lessons, repositories, assessment rubrics, financial records, and consented contacts. Export only information you are entitled to retain and use.

Keep an orderly plan for notifying members if an outage or platform change affects delivery. Do not assume exported contact information creates consent for unrelated marketing.

## Precommit to stopping rules

Pause new enrollment if a critical security issue affects the teaching repository, the promised support window repeatedly fails, or actual delivery capacity is exhausted.

Pause a paid channel at its loss cap or when observed contribution cannot justify its acquisition cost. Rework an offer after two clearly measured tests fail to produce enough paid interest.

If the community fails to justify its workload over two quarters, choose between a finite cohort, lower-support library, consulting, or closing it responsibly. Continuing because of sunk preparation time is not a strategy.

## The universal community creation process
