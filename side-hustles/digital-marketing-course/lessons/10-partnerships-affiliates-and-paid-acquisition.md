# 10. Partnerships, affiliates, and paid acquisition

[Previous](09-analytics-and-experiments.md) · [Course home](../README.md) · [Next: launches](11-launches-and-sales-execution.md)

## Borrowing distribution creates responsibilities

Once an offer is understandable and deliverable, other people or paid placements can help it reach relevant buyers. Partnerships, affiliates, sponsorships, and ads all exchange something for distribution. They differ in incentives, control, cost, and trust.

A technical newsletter can introduce your sample to readers who already care about the problem. A workshop partner can contribute complementary expertise. An affiliate can earn a commission for qualified sales. An ad can place a message in front of selected audiences. None of these automatically fixes a weak product or unclear offer.

This lesson assumes that you understand contribution and can measure confirmed purchases. It teaches how to evaluate borrowed distribution, structure a small test, and avoid scaling from misleading revenue numbers.

## Compare the distribution models

| Model | What you exchange | Main advantage | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Educational collaboration | Expertise and preparation | Demonstrated usefulness and shared trust | Time spent with a poorly matched audience |
| Newsletter sponsorship | A fixed placement payment | Predictable placement scope | Paying before knowing buyer fit |
| Affiliate arrangement | Commission under agreed terms | Acquisition cost follows eligible sales | Misleading promotion, attribution disputes, margin loss |
| Search ads | Budget for query-related exposure and clicks | Access to expressed intent | Costly queries that do not fit the offer |
| Social ads | Budget for feed placements | Controlled creative testing | Broad engagement without profitable purchases |
| Team outreach | Research and personal communication | Specific organizational need | Procurement work and long decision cycles |

You can combine models, but track their economics separately. A partner receiving both a placement fee and commission has a different cost structure from a commission-only partner.

## Choose partners by audience problem

A partner is useful when their audience experiences the problem you solve and the proposed activity adds value. Audience size alone is insufficient. A small newsletter about Kotlin production practices may fit better than a large general technology account.

Inspect public content, the typical question level, the topics readers discuss, and the partner's stated promotional approach. If considering a paid placement, ask for current placement details and relevant aggregate reporting. Do not treat a screenshot of total followers as evidence of qualified reach.

Your proposal should explain the audience benefit. “Would you promote my product?” asks the partner to solve your problem. “I can teach a focused cancellation debugging session for your intermediate Kotlin readers, with a representative lab they can keep” offers a concrete contribution.

Partnership outreach examples in this course are drafts for your review. Creating the course does not authorize contacting anyone, publishing endorsements, or entering agreements on your behalf.

## Design a collaboration with a useful result

Choose a bounded activity: a guest article, live debugging session, newsletter Q&A, or joint comparison of approaches. Agree on audience, topic, preparation, date, delivery format, promotion, recording permissions, and use of the material afterward.

A good collaboration should stand alone even for attendees who never purchase. Teach the promised concept, then identify the deeper resource transparently. If you host a workshop with a product invitation at the end, set expectations accurately rather than disguising a long sales pitch as instruction.

Decide how email subscriptions work. Registration through one partner does not automatically establish that attendees agreed to join every collaborator's newsletter. Use clear choices and the applicable requirements. Avoid exchanging contact lists simply because two creators worked together.

Afterward, evaluate relevant visits, voluntary subscriptions, questions, purchases, and preparation time. A collaboration may be worthwhile for research or credibility even with few immediate sales, but label that objective before the event rather than inventing it afterward to excuse every outcome.

## Structure affiliate economics before recruiting

An affiliate earns compensation under defined conditions, often for an attributed eligible sale. Specify what counts, the commission base, the attribution method and window, treatment of refunds, payment timing, prohibited promotion, and termination terms. Have appropriate terms for your arrangement; this lesson is not a substitute for a legal agreement.

Consider the $39 guide with $30 contribution before acquisition. If a 30% commission is calculated on the $39 product price, commission is $11.70. Contribution after that commission is $18.30, before fixed costs and any other partner expenses. If a discount also reduces the selling price, recalculate contribution rather than subtracting the commission from the old margin and assuming nothing else changes.

Do not pay on taxes collected unless the agreement and economics deliberately account for that. Define whether commissions use gross price, net receipts, or another base. Ambiguous words like “revenue share” can hide very different calculations.

A small pilot with a partner whose teaching you trust is easier to monitor than a large open program. Review actual promotional material so partners do not invent guarantees or fake scarcity on your behalf.

## Disclose incentives clearly

The FTC's influencer guidance explains that material connections, including payment or free products, may require clear disclosure. Disclosures should be easy to notice and understand in the endorsement's context; video endorsements may need disclosure in the video itself. See [R8](../SOURCES.md#r8-endorsement-disclosures).

A practical plain-language disclosure is “I earn a commission if you buy through this link.” The placement and context matter, so a sentence buried on an unrelated page is not a reliable approach. Platform disclosure tools can help but do not eliminate the need to evaluate whether the audience understands the relationship.

Do not encourage a partner to claim personal experience they have not had. Provide access for evaluation and allow an honest decision. If they find the product unsuitable, use the feedback rather than requesting a positive testimonial anyway.

The advertising relationship should not distort the teaching. An affiliate comparison can still discuss limitations and appropriate alternatives.

## Know when to postpone paid ads

Ads are easier to evaluate when you have a clear offer, reliable checkout, a representative sample, working measurement, and some evidence of buyer fit. That evidence need not come from a large audience. It should be stronger than friends saying the idea sounds good.

You may use a small paid test to investigate demand, but label the uncertainty and cap the spend. It is not a shortcut to stable profitability. At low prices, a few dollars per click can exceed the contribution your purchase rate supports.

If you cannot afford to lose the test budget, do not frame the test as money that must return a profit. Use lower-cost research, direct conversations, or useful organic distribution first. A course about marketing should not create pressure to spend before you understand the decision.

Do not set a campaign objective that rewards cheap engagement when the decision you need concerns purchases. Platform options change, so verify current campaign capabilities and policies on the official platform before implementation.

## Derive a maximum affordable click cost

Start with contribution before acquisition, not the selling price. Suppose it is $30 per order. Assume a 2% click-to-purchase rate for planning. Break-even click cost before fixed costs is:

**$30 × 0.02 = $0.60 per click.**

If you want at least $10 contribution after acquisition, allowable CAC is $20. At the same 2% rate, allowable click cost becomes:

**$20 × 0.02 = $0.40 per click.**

These calculations rely entirely on the assumed purchase rate and cost model. At 1% purchase rate, the affordable click cost halves. If traffic quality changes as you scale, the old rate may not apply.

Now imagine paying $1 per click for 500 clicks. Spend is $500. At 2% purchase rate, ten orders generate $390 gross sales and $300 contribution before acquisition. The campaign loses $200 contribution after advertising. Revenue exists, but the acquisition system is economically weak.

## Distinguish search and social creative

Search ads meet an expressed query. Query relevance and destination match are central. A developer searching for a free syntax reference may not respond like someone comparing a paid course. Broad technical keywords can attract many intentions.

Social ads interrupt a feed. The creative must make the problem recognizable and offer a reason to inspect further. A clear demonstration may help, but a visually compelling clip can also generate curiosity from people outside the buyer segment.

For both channels, use one coherent offer and destination per test. Do not send an ad about debugging cancellation to a homepage about all your projects. Make the price, sample, and terms inspectable before payment.

Retargeting means advertising based on prior interaction where permitted and technically supported. It raises privacy and consent considerations that depend on context. Do not assume that installing a tracking pixel is the only requirement. For a first launch, you can operate without retargeting.

## Write a bounded paid-test brief

A test brief names the audience, hypothesis, creative, destination, budget cap, timing, measurement, and stop conditions. For example: “We will test whether intermediate Kotlin developers who inspect a debugging demo purchase the guide at a contribution level that supports this channel.”

Set the budget before running. Stop for broken checkout, misleading creative, missing measurement, policy issues, or a reached budget cap. Do not increase spend simply because a platform reports many inexpensive clicks. Wait for the relevant purchase and refund window before evaluating mature economics.

At low volume, the result may be inconclusive. “This test produced too few purchases to estimate CAC reliably” is a useful finding. It can justify revising the test, choosing another channel, or improving offer evidence. It does not require spending indefinitely until a favorable result appears.

## Team sales as a separate path

A team buyer may care about onboarding consistency, shared vocabulary, licensing, and a defined workshop. Their decision can involve a manager, procurement, and security or finance processes. A $39 individual checkout is not necessarily the right buying process for them.

Create a separate scope for a team pilot: number of learners, materials, session, access period, support, and permitted use. Price the service component based on preparation and delivery, not only the individual guide price multiplied by seats.

Find an actual organizational problem before writing a generic sales pitch. A respectful, specific conversation with an interested manager can teach you more than mass messages to every technical leader you can find.

## Optional application: select one borrowed-distribution test

Compare a collaboration, a placement, an affiliate pilot, and an ad test using audience fit, cash risk, time, measurement, and contribution. Choose one that resolves an important uncertainty. Draft the proposal or test brief in the workbook; execute only after real terms and readiness checks are complete.

Borrowed distribution helps when it introduces a credible offer to relevant people under sustainable terms. Its value comes from fit and execution, not from the fact that someone else has a larger audience.
